A reading from the Book of Isaiah

Isaiah 52:13—53:12
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

52:13Behold my servant shall understand, he shall be exalted, and extolled, and shall be exceeding high.

52:14As many have been astonished at thee, so shall his visage be inglorious among men, and his form among the sons of men.

52:15He shall sprinkle many nations, kings shall shut their mouth at him: for they to whom it was not told of him, have seen: and they that heard not, have beheld.

53:1Who a hath believed our report? and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed?

53:2And he shall grow up as a tender plant before him, and as a root out of a thirsty ground: there is no beauty in him, nor comeliness: and we have seen him, and there was no sightliness, that we should be desirous of him:

53:3Despised, and the most abject of men, a man of sorrows, and acquainted with infirmity: and his look was as it were hidden and despised, whereupon we esteemed him not.

53:4Surely he hath borne our infirmities and carried our sorrows: and we have thought him as it were a leper, and as one struck by God and afflicted.

53:5But he was wounded for our iniquities, he was bruised for our sins: the chastisement of our peace was upon him, and by his bruises we are healed.

53:6All we like sheep have gone astray, every one hath turned aside into his own way: and the Lord hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.

53:7He was offered because it was his own will, and he opened not his mouth: he shall be led as a sheep to the slaughter, and shall be dumb as a lamb before his shearer, and he shall not open his mouth.

53:8He was taken away from distress, and from judgment: who shall declare his generation? because he is cut off out of the land of the living: for the wickedness of my people have I struck him.

53:9And he shall give the ungodly for his burial, and the rich for his death: because he hath done no iniquity, neither was there deceit in his mouth.

53:10And the Lord was pleased to bruise him in infirmity: if he shall lay down his life for sin, he shall see a longlived seed, and the will of the Lord shall be prosperous in his hand.

53:11Because his soul hath laboured, he shall see and be filled: by his knowledge shall this my just servant justify many, and he shall bear their iniquities.

53:12Therefore will I distribute to him very many, and he shall divide the spoils of the strong, because he hath delivered his soul unto death, and was reputed with the wicked: and he hath borne the sins of many, and hath prayed for the transgressors.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 52:15The passage moves from spectacle to mission: the servant’s work spills past Israel toward “many nations,” and the reaction of kings is not devotion but speechlessness. The narrative assumes history will be instructed after the fact, by an event no audience initially requested.
  • R 53:3,53:4The text insists on a misreading built into religious perception: affliction is interpreted as divine curse. That false inference becomes part of the mechanism by which the servant is rejected and yet effective.
  • R 53:8,53:11Two logics are held together: removal by “judgment” and the later justification of “many.” The servant’s efficacy is not rhetorical persuasion but a transfer enacted through suffering; knowledge here is not information but the servant’s enacted competence to set others right.
On the Gospel
  • G 18:8,18:9John narrates control through concession: Jesus yields himself while actively delimiting the arrest’s reach (“let these go”), and fulfillment language turns contingency into design. The Passion is portrayed less as tragedy than as execution of a prior word.
  • G 18:28,18:31,18:32Legal theater dominates: purity scruples outside the hall coexist with a push for death they cannot legally perform. Responsibility is dispersed across jurisdictions, yet the text keeps dragging it back to the revealed shape of Jesus’ own death as the governing explanation.
  • G 19:7,19:11The charge escalates from politics to theology (“Son of God”), and Jesus answers political power with a doctrine of delegated authority. The narrative assigns comparative guilt without dissolving any agent into fate: degrees of sin exist inside a providential frame.
  • G 19:23,19:24,19:36,19:37Scripture fulfillment is attached to mundane acts—garment division, unbroken bones, a pierced body—so that material details become textual signals. The crucified body is treated as a site where Israel’s writings find their literal completion.

Doctrinal The Servant’s abasement is the chosen instrument of divine rule, whereby guilt is transferred and many are justified. In the Passion, Jesus remains sovereign even in surrender, and his death completes Scripture as a deliberate act.

Heterodox Reading
Isaiah’s servant is not a savior offered to satisfy heaven; he is the perfect instrument for a community that cannot bear its own guilt without making it visible. They need a body to hold what they feel but refuse to name, so they choose the one with “no beauty,” the one already easy to ignore, and then call their violence healing. The line that should chill you is not that he suffers, but that “the Lord was pleased to bruise him”: the sacred becomes the community’s permission slip, the metaphysical stamp that turns cruelty into meaning. John stages the same psychology with colder precision. Jesus does not get dragged to death; he walks into it, knowing, speaking, and the armed men fall backward as if his consent is the true force in the scene. His silence is not meekness; it is control. Peter’s denials by the coal fire are the ordinary heart’s instinct: stay warm, stay safe, let someone else be the cost. The dread is that the cross works because everyone needs it to work. Pilate wants innocence without responsibility, the priests want purity without mercy, the crowd wants a king they can hate, the disciples want love without risk. “It is consummated” lands like the closing of a transaction: the world finally gets the victim it has been craving, and calls the relief redemption.
Semina Verbi
Speculation: the Servant’s disfigurement and quiet consent echoes the Bodhisattva ideal in Mahayana, where compassion accepts injury without retaliation, and the Jataka tales where a life is spent to spare others. The “man of sorrows” also resembles the Hindu motif of the world-bearer—Shiva as the one who drinks poison for the cosmos—less as payment, more as metabolizing what would kill the many. Speculation: John’s “I am” that knocks armed men backward has kinship with the Bhagavad Gita’s theophany, a flash of absolute presence that makes power buckle, and with Sufi fana, where the ego collapses before the Real. Pilate’s “What is truth?” feels like the koan tradition in Zen: not cynicism but the mind’s last defense against a truth that cannot be managed. Speculation: the refusal of the sword and the chosen cup parallels Stoic assent to fate and the Taoist wu-wei of yielding that outlasts force, while the pierced side pouring blood and water recalls Dionysian and Orphic images of life poured out to remake a community—violent, yet transfiguring in its psychic effect.
