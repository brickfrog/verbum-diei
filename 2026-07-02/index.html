    SextAd Sextam12:00

    NonesAd Nonam15:00

    VespersAd Vesperas18:00
    You, Lord, who illumine the night and make the light dawn anew after the darkness, grant that during the night now beginning we may be free from every fault, and that, when the new day breaks, we may gather once more in your presence to give you thanks again. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever.
    ComplineAd Completorium21:00
    Let us pray: Lord our God, grant us a peaceful rest that restores our strength, now worn down by the day’s work; so that, strengthened by your help, we may always serve you with all our body and spirit. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    IIILectioAmos 7:10-17

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Amos

    Amos 7:10-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    10And Amasias the priest of Bethel sent to Jeroboam king of Israel, saying: Amos hath rebelled against thee in the midst of the house of Israel: the land is not able to bear all his words.

    ☞R 10
    Priest as informant; prophecy recast as sedition. “Land” made fragile by speech.

    11For thus saith Amos: Jeroboam shall die by the sword, and Israel shall be carried away captive out of their own land. Jeroboam shall die by the sword... The prophet did not say this; but that the Lord would rise up against the house of Jeroboam with the sword: which was verified, when Zacharias, the son and successor of Jeroboam, was slain by the sword. 4 Kings 15.10.

    12And Amasias said to Amos: Thou seer, go, flee away into the land of Juda: and eat bread there, and prophesy there.

    ☞R 12
    “Flee” and “eat bread”: prophecy reduced to a trade. Bethel defined as royal property, not holy ground.

    13But prophesy not again any more in Bethel: because it is the king's sanctuary, and it is the house of the kingdom.

    ☞R 13
    “Flee” and “eat bread”: prophecy reduced to a trade. Bethel defined as royal property, not holy ground.

    14And Amos answered and said to Amasias: I am not a prophet, nor am I the son of a prophet: but I am a herdsman plucking wild figs. I am not a prophet... That is, I am not a prophet by education: nor is prophesying my calling or profession: but I am a herdsman, whom God was pleased to send hither to prophesy to Israel.

    ☞R 14
    Anti-credential: not guild, not lineage. The fig-plucker outranks the court priest by call, not craft.

    15And the Lord took me when I followed the flock, and the Lord said to me: Go, prophesy to my people Israel.

    16And now hear thou the word of the Lord: Thou sayest, thou shalt not prophesy against Israel, and thou shalt not drop thy word upon the house of the idol. The house of the idol... Viz., of the calf worshipped in Bethel.

    17Therefore thus saith the Lord: Thy wife shall play the harlot in the city, and thy sons and thy daughters shall fall by the sword, and thy land shall be measured by a line: and thou shalt die in a polluted land, and Israel shall go into captivity out of their land.

    ☞R 17
    Judgment targets the priest’s household and acreage: sex, blood, survey-line. Exile named as the final pollution.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 9:1-8

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 9:1-8 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And entering into a boat, he passed over the water and came into his own city.

    2And behold they brought to him one sick of the palsy lying in a bed. And Jesus, seeing their faith, said to the man sick of the palsy: Be of good heart, son, thy sins are forgiven thee.

    ☞G 2
    Faith is seen, not argued; forgiveness spoken before cure. The deeper paralysis is moral.

    3And behold some of the scribes said within themselves: He blasphemeth.

    ☞G 3
    Silent accusation meets exposed interiority. Their “within” is already a courtroom.

    4And Jesus seeing their thoughts, said: Why do you think evil in your hearts?

    ☞G 4
    Silent accusation meets exposed interiority. Their “within” is already a courtroom.

    5Whether is easier, to say, Thy sins are forgiven thee: or to say, Arise, and walk?

    ☞G 5
    “Easier” becomes a trap: the invisible claim is proved by a visible command. Bed turns from burden to evidence.

    6But that you may know that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins, (then said he to the man sick of the palsy,) Arise, take up thy bed, and go into thy house.

    ☞G 6
    “Easier” becomes a trap: the invisible claim is proved by a visible command. Bed turns from burden to evidence.

    7And he arose, and went into his house.

    8And the multitude seeing it, feared, and glorified God that gave such power to men.

    ☞G 8
    They fear the gift more than the disease. Power “to men” is heard, but its source is missed.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s word is not licensed by sanctuary or guild; it is authorized by divine seizure and exercised as judgment. In Christ that same authority appears on earth as the power to forgive sins, publicly ratified by bodily restoration.

    In Lectionem

    • R 15,16The dispute is jurisdictional: who may speak for God to “my people.” The priest’s prohibition attempts to domesticate revelation inside a state cult; the prophet answers by relocating authority to a prior seizure—God’s initiative that precedes institution.
    • R 10,11The charge of rebellion shows how judgment against a dynasty is treated as treason against the nation. The text forces a choice: stability as the highest good, or truth that destabilizes; the priest chooses the former and thereby indicts himself.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,6,7The scene is staged as a public verification: the Son of Man’s authority is asserted “on earth” and then enacted in ordinary space—house, bed, walking. The miracle is not spectacle but credential, binding bodily restoration to a legal act of absolution.
    • G 2,8The crowd’s response frames the event as delegated power, but the narrative has already centered a singular agent who reads hearts and pronounces remission. Their doxology is real yet incomplete: they praise the category while the text presses the identity.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Amos is treated as a public health hazard: the land can’t “bear” his words. Amaziah isn’t defending doctrine; he’s defending the nervous system of the state. Bethel is the king’s sanctuary, the house of the kingdom, where speech must be domesticated into reassurance. Amos refuses the professional mask. He is a herdsman, an amateur, which is why he’s terrifying: he has no career to protect, no institution to soothe, only a compulsion that arrived from outside the system.

    The curse on Amaziah is obscene on purpose. It isn’t piety; it’s humiliation as policy. When power tells the truth-teller to go earn his bread elsewhere, the truth-teller answers by stripping power’s family of dignity, lineage, and land. Prophecy here is not moral instruction; it’s counter-violence, the language of dread used to break a monopoly on meaning.

    In Matthew, the same struggle is staged more elegantly. Jesus begins with the invisible: forgiveness. The scribes call it blasphemy because it bypasses their gatekeeping. He reads their thoughts, not to shame them, but to show that authority now operates at the level of interior life. The paralytic’s body is the proof, but the real miracle is administrative: a new jurisdiction over guilt.

    The crowd fears because they recognize what has happened. Power has shifted from temple and court to a man who can rename a person’s past and make it stick. “Such power to men” is not comfort; it’s terror and desire at once—the longing to be released, and the dread that someone can see you, absolve you, and therefore own you.

    Semina Verbi

    Amos reads like the archetype of the unwilling truth-teller: an ordinary worker conscripted by a voice that won’t let him keep his life small. That pressure resembles the Greek daimonion in Socrates, an interior check that overrides prudence, and it also echoes the Hindu idea of dharma arriving as an inescapable duty rather than a career. Speculation: the priest’s panic is less about doctrine than about institutional homeostasis; the “land cannot bear” is a psyche defending itself against shame.

    Jesus’ move—naming forgiveness before movement—parallels Buddhist accounts where release of grasping precedes bodily ease, and Daoist healing where alignment comes before technique. The scribes’ silent accusation reads like a superego policing who is allowed to absolve; Jesus answers by exposing thought itself as the battleground. Speculation: the paralysis functions as embodied guilt, and the public miracle is a social reauthorization of the man’s place, akin to restorative rites in many Indigenous traditions where reintegration heals as much as medicine.
