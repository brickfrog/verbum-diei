    IIILectioEcclesiastes 1:2-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book Ecclesiastes

    Ecclesiastes 1:2-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    2Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes: vanity of vanities, and all is vanity.

    ☞R 2
    Superlative piled on superlative: language collapses into a verdict.

    3What hath a man more of all his labour, that he taketh under the sun?

    ☞R 3
    Profit-question: work measured by surplus, not by meaning.

    4One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth standeth for ever.

    ☞R 4
    Generations are replaceable; the earth is the indifferent constant.

    5The sun riseth, and goeth down, and returneth to his place: and there rising again,

    ☞R 5
    The sun’s “return” mocks human notions of progress.

    6Maketh his round by the south, and turneth again to the north: the spirit goeth forward surveying all places round about, and returneth to his circuits.

    7All the rivers run into the sea, yet the sea doth not overflow: unto the place from whence the rivers come, they return, to flow again.

    ☞R 7
    Inflow without fullness: motion without arrival.

    8All things are hard: man cannot explain them by word. The eye is not filled with seeing, neither is the ear filled with hearing.

    ☞R 8
    Senses as hungry mouths; perception never closes the account.

    9What is it that hath been? the same thing that shall be. What is it that hath been done? the same that shall be done.

    10Nothing under the sun is new, neither is any man able to say: Behold this is new: for it hath already gone before in the ages that were before us.

    11There is no remembrance of former things: nor indeed of those things which hereafter are to come, shall there be any remembrance with them that shall be in the latter end.

    ☞R 11
    Oblivion is the final equality; even the future is erased.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 9:7-9

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 9:7-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    7Now Herod, the tetrarch, heard of all things that were done by him. And he was in a doubt, because it was said

    ☞G 7
    Power hears; power does not understand. Rumor is his intelligence.

    8By some that John was risen from the dead: but by other some, that Elias had appeared: and by others, that one of the old prophets was risen again.

    ☞G 8
    Resurrection reduced to options on a courtier’s menu.

    9And Herod said: John I have beheaded. But who is this of whom I hear such things? And he sought to see him.

    ☞G 9
    A clean confession of violence, then a dirty curiosity: “who is this?”

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisUnder the sun, human striving cannot produce surplus, novelty, or lasting remembrance. The gospel exposes how worldly power, trapped in repetition and guilt, can only misname Christ and seek to control what it cannot comprehend.

    In Lectionem

    • R 5,6,9,10The passage builds a cosmology of recurrence: celestial paths, winds, waters, and human history all loop. The argument is not that events repeat in detail, but that the world’s patterns absorb every novelty claim into an older cycle, leaving “new” as a rhetorical mistake.
    • R 2,3,8The text attacks the economy of self-justification: labor seeks remainder, speech seeks mastery, sensation seeks satiety. Each instrument—work, word, eye, ear—fails to deliver closure, so the human project cannot secure its own verdict.

    In Evangelium

    • G 7,8,9Herod’s court interprets Jesus through recycled categories—John, Elijah, prophets—because political imagination cannot name a living threat except as a return of the past. Herod’s desire “to see” is not discipleship but surveillance: the ruler who can kill a prophet wants the next one legible.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ecclesiastes isn’t preaching humility; it’s reporting the symptom of a mind that has watched the world repeat itself until repetition becomes a kind of nausea. The sun’s circuit, the rivers’ return, the eye that can’t stop looking—these are not neutral observations but compulsions. “Vanity” here is not moral judgment; it’s the dead feel of experience when desire keeps firing and nothing ever finally arrives. The terror is not death but endlessness: a treadmill that outlasts every generation, with the added cruelty that even the treadmill won’t remember you.

    Herod is the human face of that dread. He has tried to make an ending—he beheads John, a decisive act meant to close the story—and the story refuses to close. Rumor resurrects what he killed; names recycle like seasons: John, Elijah, an old prophet, anyone but the truth that he cannot undo himself. His “who is this?” is not curiosity; it’s panic that the world is looping back to him. He seeks to see Jesus the way an anxious man checks a locked door, hoping sight will grant control.

    Put together, the texts read like a psychology of power and boredom: the cosmos repeats, and the ruler, trapped in his own act, can’t bear the thought that something genuinely new might be walking toward him. The desire to see is the desire to master; the dread is that the new will not be new at all, but the return of what he cannot live with.

    Semina Verbi

    Qoheleth’s turning world has the same dry, lucid taste as the Buddha’s diagnosis of craving: the eye and ear never fill, so the self keeps buying experience like a drug that cannot satisfy. The rivers returning without gain echoes Stoic ekpyrosis and eternal recurrence: nature cycles; the ego’s demand for a climax is the delusion. Speculation: it also brushes the Daoist sense of the ten thousand things flowing back to the Dao, where naming and explanation fail.

    Herod’s curiosity is the anxious mind faced with an event that won’t fit its categories. He treats the uncanny as a recycling of old figures, like a courtier reaching for familiar masks to domesticate threat. This resembles the Mahabharata’s kings who consult omens and genealogies to avoid moral choice, and the Shinto instinct to locate a new power as a returning kami rather than a new claim on conscience. Speculation: it is also Freud’s return of the repressed in political form—guilt seeking a face to attach to, so the ruler can look without changing.
