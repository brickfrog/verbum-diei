Reading

A reading from the Book of Isaiah

Isaiah 58:1-9
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

1Cry, cease not, lift up thy voice like a trumpet, and shew my people their wicked doings, and the house of Jacob their sins.

2For they seek me from day to day, and desire to know my ways, as a nation that hath done justice, and hath not forsaken the judgment of their God: they ask of me the judgments of justice: they are willing to approach to God.

3Why have we fasted, and thou hast not regarded: have we humbled our souls, and thou hast not taken notice? Behold in the day of your fast your own will is found, and you exact of all your debtors.

4Behold you fast for debates and strife, and strike with the fist wickedly. Do not fast as you have done until this day, to make your cry to be heard on high.

5Is this such a fast as I have chosen: for a man to afflict his soul for a day? is this it, to wind his head about like a circle, and to spread sackcloth and ashes? wilt thou call this a fast, and a day acceptable to the Lord?

6Is not this rather the fast that I have chosen? loose the bands of wickedness, undo the bundles that oppress, let them that are broken go free, and break asunder every burden.

7Deal thy bread to the hungry, and bring the needy and the harbourless into thy house: when thou shalt see one naked, cover him, and despise not thy own flesh.

8Then shall thy light break forth as the morning, and thy health shall speedily arise, and thy justice shall go before thy face, and the glory of the Lord shall gather thee up.

9Then shalt thou call, and the Lord shall hear: thou shalt cry, and he shall say, Here I am. If thou wilt take away the chain out of the midst of thee, and cease to stretch out the finger, and to speak that which profiteth not.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 6,8Isaiah reframes religion from self-inflicted deprivation to public justice, then ties divine communion to a moral causal chain: liberation yields illumination. The oracle is not ethical advice but a rerouting of cult to covenant, where God’s nearness is mediated through the neighbor’s release.
  • R 2,9The passage exposes a transactional theology—ritual as leverage—and replaces it with a liturgy of repair. The climax is not ecstasy but an unblocked address: prayer works when the community’s internal coercions are dismantled.
On the Gospel
  • G 15Jesus relocates fasting inside a messianic timetable: it belongs to the interval after removal, not as merit but as the bodily language of longing. The bridegroom image makes practice derivative of presence—asceticism is not the condition for God, but the sign of desiring him when he is hidden.

Doctrinal God rejects fasting that preserves domination and receives fasting that breaks it. Christian fasting is calibrated to the presence and absence of the Bridegroom, not to the vanity of religious display.

Heterodox Reading
Isaiah isn’t praising charity; he’s diagnosing a kink in religious desire. The people fast because they want to be seen by the sky and rewarded by it. They perform emptiness to force a response, and when nothing answers they grow cruel: they squeeze debtors, they pick fights, they point the finger. The ritual becomes a machine for resentment, a way to convert private hunger into public dominance. God’s “Cry like a trumpet” is less moral lecture than exposure: your piety is a mask for appetite. The alternative fast is not sweeter; it’s more frightening. Loosen bonds, break burdens, bring the homeless into your house, cover nakedness, don’t despise your own flesh. That last line cracks the whole economy: the needy are not “others” but extensions of you, and that recognition destroys the safe distance on which sanctity feeds. Light and healing come only after you surrender the pleasure of being the righteous one. Matthew sharpens the tension. While the bridegroom is present, fasting would be false—mourning would be theater. But he will be taken away, and then fasting returns as the only honest act: not a bargaining chip, but grief made bodily. The text admits what Isaiah implies: religion is driven by longing, and when the beloved disappears, the only truth left is the ache. If you fast to control God, you become violent; if you fast because you’ve lost him, your hunger might finally open into justice.
Semina Verbi
Isaiah’s attack on performative fasting matches the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence that austerity without right intention is sterile, and the Buddhist critique of “wrong practice” that inflates ego instead of cutting craving; in all three, discipline becomes vanity when it fails to reduce harm. The demanded fast—breaking chains, feeding, housing—resembles zakat and sadaqah in Islam: devotion proven by redistribution and protection of the vulnerable, not by theatrical self-denial. The bridegroom image in Matthew carries a psychological rhythm found in Sufism’s alternation of presence and absence (wajd and qabd), where practices intensify precisely when felt nearness withdraws. Speculation: it also echoes the Taoist sense that forcing ritual in the moment of fullness is clumsy; restraint belongs to the season of loss, when appetite needs re-education rather than display.
