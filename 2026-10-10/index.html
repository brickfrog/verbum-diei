    IIILectioGalatians 3:22-29

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Galatians

    Galatians 3:22-29 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    22But the scripture hath concluded all under sin, that the promise, by the faith of Jesus Christ, might be given to them that believe.

    ☞R 22
    Scripture as jailer: it "concludes"—shuts the case—so promise can arrive only as gift.

    23But before the faith came, we were kept under the law shut up, unto that faith which was to be revealed.

    ☞R 23
    Law as custody: "shut up" until disclosure; confinement with a horizon.

    24Wherefore the law was our pedagogue in Christ: that we might be justified by faith.

    ☞R 24
    Pedagogue, not parent: authority that escorts, not one that begets.

    25But after the faith is come, we are no longer under a pedagogue.

    26For you are all the children of God, by faith in Christ Jesus.

    27For as many of you as have been baptized in Christ have put on Christ.

    ☞R 27
    Baptism as clothing: identity worn, not merely assented to.

    28There is neither Jew nor Greek: there is neither bond nor free: there is neither male nor female. For you are all one in Christ Jesus.

    ☞R 28
    Three binaries dismantled; unity is not sameness but a new location: "in Christ".

    29And if you be Christ's, then are you the seed of Abraham, heirs according to the promise.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 11:27-28

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 11:27-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    27And it came to pass, as he spoke these things, a certain woman from the crowd, lifting up her voice, said to him: Blessed is the womb that bore thee and the paps that gave thee suck.

    ☞G 27
    Praise aims at biology—womb and breast—holiness reduced to origin.

    28But he said: Yea rather, blessed are they who hear the word of God and keep it.

    ☞G 28
    Christ reroutes beatitude from flesh to obedience: hearing that becomes keeping.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisJustification and inheritance are granted by union with Christ received in faith, not by the Law or natural lineage. Blessedness is measured by obedient reception of God’s word, not by biological proximity to Jesus.

    In Lectionem

    • R 25,26,29Paul marks a regime change: the end of tutelage (4) yields filial status (5), and that status redefines genealogy—Abrahamic descent is no longer a bloodline but an inheritance claim grounded in belonging to Christ (8). The argument is juridical and adoptive: who counts as heir, and by what title.

    In Evangelium

    • G 28Jesus refuses a cult of proximity. The criterion of blessedness is not access to the holy body but submission to the holy word; the kingdom’s honors are distributed by reception and practice, not by kinship or acclaim.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul sounds like a man staging a jailbreak. The law isn’t wisdom; it’s custody. “Shut up” is the verb of a cell. Sin is the universal charge, not because everyone is equally wicked, but because the system needs everyone guilty to keep everyone manageable. Then “faith” arrives like an inside contact, and the old guard—the pedagogue, the paid escort of minors—loses his job. Freedom comes not by becoming better but by changing jurisdictions.

    “Put on Christ” is not moral improvement; it’s disguise, costume, skin. Baptism becomes a wardrobe change that erases the old identifiers—ethnicity, class, sex—not because those differences stop existing, but because the new identity is totalizing. The sweetness of “all one” carries a threat: if you belong to this body, you don’t get to keep your separateness. Desire for unity and dread of absorption are braided together.

    Luke lets a woman try to anchor holiness in biology, in the warm fact of a mother’s body. Jesus refuses the sentimental trap. He won’t be claimed by origin, lineage, or the erotic gratitude of nursing. He shifts blessedness to hearing and keeping: an obedience that any body can attempt, and that no mother can guarantee. It’s colder, harsher, and more democratic. The womb is praised; the word is demanded.

    Taken together, the texts offer a frightening consolation: you can be released from the prison of inherited roles, but only by submitting to a voice that will not flatter your blood, your gender, or your story. The old bonds are cut. A new bond tightens.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “pedagogue” feels like Plato’s ladder in the Symposium: desire educated by forms until it can stand without the tutor. The move from markers of status to a single identity echoes the Stoic cosmopolis, where citizenship is not blood or city but participation in reason; speculation: Paul’s “put on” functions like a moral uniform that dissolves local costumes.

    Luke’s reversal from womb to obedience parallels Buddhist suspicion of lineage and charisma: the Buddha refuses holiness by birth in favor of practice. It also resembles Confucius’s shift from noble pedigree to cultivated virtue, and the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence that hearing and doing outweighs inherited station; speculation: the blessing relocates intimacy from biology to attention, making “family” a discipline rather than a genealogy.
