Before the festival day of the pasch... This was the fourth and last pasch of the ministry of Christ, and according to the common computation, was in the thirty-third year of our Lord: and in the year of the world 4036. Some chronologers are of opinion that our Saviour suffered in the thirty-seventh year of his age: but these different opinions on this subject are of no consequence.

2And when supper was done (the devil having now put into the heart of Judas Iscariot, the son of Simon, to betray him),

3Knowing that the Father had given him all things into his hands and that he came from God and goeth to God,

4He riseth from supper and layeth aside his garments and, having taken a towel, girded himself.

5After that, he putteth water into a basin and began to wash the feet of the disciples and to wipe them with the towel wherewith he was girded.

6He cometh therefore to Simon Peter. And Peter saith to him: Lord, dost thou wash my feet?

7Jesus answered and said to him: What I do, thou knowest not now; but thou shalt know hereafter.

8Peter saith to him: Thou shalt never wash my feet, Jesus answered him: If I wash thee not, thou shalt have no part with me.

9Simon Peter saith to him: Lord, not only my feet, but also my hands and my head.

10Jesus saith to him: He that is washed needeth not but to wash his feet, but is clean wholly. And you are clean, but not all.

11For he knew who he was that would betray him; therefore he said: You are not all clean.

12Then after he had washed their feet and taken his garments, being set down again, he said to them: Know you what I have done to you?

13You call me Master and Lord. And you say well: for so I am.

14If then I being your Lord and Master, have washed your feet; you also ought to wash one another's feet.

15For I have given you an example, that as I have done to you, so you do also.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 23,26Paul frames the Eucharist as an entrusted deposit that makes the community public: repetition is not private devotion but the Church’s standing testimony in history. The horizon “until he come” makes every celebration eschatological—an interim regime ordered to return, not completion.
  • R 24,25The actions and words bind body and blood to a juridical-act: “new testament” is not metaphor but enactment. The community’s identity is constituted by a covenantal performance, not by shared sentiment.
On the Gospel
  • G 1,3,12,14,15John stages the footwashing as a deliberate sign-act inside Jesus’ self-knowledge: he acts from origin and destination (“came from God… goeth to God”), so abasement is not loss but disclosure of divine power. The question in line 12 turns the event into instruction: imitation is commanded, yet grounded in asymmetry—Lord remains Lord while kneeling, establishing a hierarchy that obligates service rather than dissolving rank.
  • G 2,6,7,9The scene is contested pedagogy: Judas is already in motion; Peter oscillates between resistance and excess. Jesus governs the timing of understanding (“not now… hereafter”), implying that participation precedes comprehension and that discipleship is learned by undergoing, not by negotiating terms.

Doctrinal Christ institutes communion with himself through covenantal signs that both proclaim his death and bind his disciples into a commanded pattern of humble service. Participation is conditional on receiving his cleansing, and that cleansing obligates imitation under his lordship.

Heterodox Reading
This is not a love story that calms. It is a controlled descent. The night is named by betrayal first, and everything tender is staged inside that fact. Bread and cup are not consolations; they are a method for keeping the wound open. “Do this” means repeat the loss until it becomes your language. You don’t remember him; you rehearse his death, publicly, compulsively, “until he come,” an ending that never arrives inside the ritual. Desire is trained to circle an absence. John’s scene sharpens the dread. The one who “has all things into his hands” uses those hands to touch the dirty, intimate parts people hide. The towel is a kind of leash; the basin a silent verdict on the body. Peter’s refusal is shame speaking: don’t cross that boundary. Jesus answers like a lover and a threat: let me humiliate you or you have no part in me. Peter swings to excess, begging for total washing, because partial intimacy is unbearable. “You are clean, but not all” is the cruel line. Cleanliness is not purity; it is belonging, and it is granted while the betrayer is still in the room. The community is founded with an infection included. The example is not sentimental service; it is the replication of power’s strangest move: to own others by kneeling, to bind them by lowering oneself, to make them indebted to tenderness. The command to wash each other’s feet is the command to repeat that uncomfortable intimacy—again and again—until love and dread are indistinguishable.
Semina Verbi
This scene fuses two human technologies for defeating panic: ritual repetition and enacted humility. The meal-word makes grief portable; you can carry the loss forward without dissolving into it. The foot-washing makes power inhabitable; authority sheds its costume, touches what is socially low, and proves it can descend without ceasing to be itself. Together they form a psyche where belonging is not secured by purity displays but by consenting to be handled, cleansed, and then sent to do the same. Speculation: the “do this” cadence resembles the Jewish Passover’s anamnesis and the Haggadah’s insistence that memory becomes participation, not nostalgia. The basin gesture echoes Hindu and Buddhist guru seva and the washing of guests’ feet in ancient hospitality codes, where reverence is shown through service rather than flattery. The clean/not-all-clean tension has kinship with Stoic prohairesis and Buddhist karma: outward proximity to the good does not guarantee inward alignment. The vow-like refusal and reversal in Peter recalls Zen’s pedagogical shock and Sufi adab, where the disciple’s pride must be tenderly broken to enter the circle.
