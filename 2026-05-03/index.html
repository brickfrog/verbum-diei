    IIILectioActs 6:1-7

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 6:1-7 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And in those days, the number of the disciples increasing, there arose a murmuring of the Greeks against the Hebrews, for that their widows were neglected in the daily ministration. Greeks... So they called the Jews that were born and brought up in Greece.

    ☞R 1
    Growth breeds friction; charity fails first at the margins (widows), and ethnicity supplies the vocabulary of complaint.

    2Then the twelve, calling together the multitude of the disciples, said: It is not reason that we should leave the word of God and serve tables.

    ☞R 2
    “Word” versus “tables”: not contempt for bodies, but refusal of role-confusion.

    3Wherefore, brethren, look ye out among you seven men of good reputation, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, whom we may appoint over this business.

    ☞R 3
    Administration is spiritual work: reputation, Spirit, wisdom—qualifications for logistics.

    4But we will give ourselves continually to prayer and to the ministry of the word.

    5And the saying was liked by all the multitude. And they chose Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Ghost, and Philip and Prochorus and Nicanor, and Timon and Parmenas and Nicolas, a proselyte of Antioch.

    6These they set before the apostles: and they praying, imposed hands upon them.

    ☞R 6
    Hands imposed: authority is transferred, not improvised; service is commissioned.

    7And the word of the Lord increased: and the number of the disciples was multiplied in Jerusalem exceedingly. A great multitude also of the priests obeyed the faith.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 14:1-12

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 14:1-12 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Let not your heart be troubled. You believe in God: believe also in me.

    2In my Father's house there are many mansions. If not, I would have told you: because I go to prepare a place for you.

    ☞G 2
    “Many mansions”: not scarcity but ordered abundance; belonging is architectural.

    3And if I shall go and prepare a place for you, I will come again and will take you to myself: that where I am, you also may be.

    4And whither I go you know: and the way you know.

    ☞G 4
    They “know” and yet do not; discipleship carries unrecognized knowledge.

    5Thomas saith to him: Lord, we know not whither thou goest. And how can we know the way?

    6Jesus saith to him: I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No man cometh to the Father, but by me.

    ☞G 6
    Not a map but a person: access is exclusive because communion is personal.

    7If you had known me, you would without doubt have known my Father also: and from henceforth you shall know him. And you have seen him.

    8Philip saith to him: Lord, shew us the Father; and it is enough for us.

    9Jesus saith to him: Have I been so long a time with you and have you not known me? Philip, he that seeth me seeth the Father also. How sayest thou: Shew us the Father?

    ☞G 9
    The demand to “see” the Father is answered by the scandal of visibility: God in a face.

    10Do you not believe that I am in the Father and the Father in me? The words that I speak to you, I speak not of myself. But the Father who abideth in me, he doth the works.

    11Believe you not that I am in the Father and the Father in me?

    12Otherwise believe for the very works' sake. Amen, amen, I say to you, he that believeth in me, the works that I do, he also shall do: and greater than these shall he do.

    ☞G 12
    “Greater works”: extension, not rivalry—Christ’s acts propagated through believers.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChrist alone is the access to the Father, and his Church shares in his mission through ordered ministries that unite prayer, word, and concrete service.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2,4,7The passage resolves an internal justice crisis by differentiating offices: apostolic speech/prayer remains primary while a delegated ministry stabilizes the community’s material life. The result is not mere efficiency but mission expansion—“word increased”—showing that right order in the Church is an instrument of evangelization, even reaching the priestly class.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,3,10,11Jesus answers anxiety with ontology: the consolation offered is not information about a destination but participation in his relation to the Father. The discourse moves from promise (“I will come again”) to identity (“I am…”) to mutual indwelling, grounding faith not in private insight but in the Father’s works manifested through the Son.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Acts opens with a complaint the community wants to call “murmuring” so it can stay holy. But it’s hunger speaking. Widows are being missed, bodies slipping through the cracks, and the apostles’ first instinct is triage: the word must not be contaminated by tables. They invent a new layer of sanctified labor so the dirt of need can be handled without touching the men of prayer. The laying on of hands is not only blessing; it’s delegation dressed as sacrament, a way to keep the center clean while the margins absorb the mess.

    John answers that same anxiety with a lullaby that is also a threat. “Let not your heart be troubled” lands because the heart is already troubled; he is leaving. The “many mansions” are not comfort so much as architecture for abandonment: there will be space, yes, but not presence. Thomas and Philip ask for coordinates and a face; they want something they can hold. Jesus gives a person instead of a map, then folds the Father into himself so tightly that the disciples’ desire to see becomes a rebuke. The way is not knowledge but attachment.

    Put together, the texts admit what they won’t say outright: the church grows by managing absence. The apostles withdraw upward into prayer, Jesus withdraws into the Father’s house, and everyone left behind is told that the withdrawal is love. The promise of “greater works” is the psychological bargain—do more because I am gone—an inheritance of power that feels like consolation until you notice it’s also a sentence to keep the machinery running while longing is renamed faith.

    Semina Verbi

    Acts’ split between word and tables echoes the Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga and jnana-yoga: action purified by right order, contemplation protected from administrative drag, both serving one body. It also resembles Confucian li: social harmony restored by appointing reputable mediators so resentment doesn’t rot the community from within.

    John’s “many mansions” and “I am the way” parallels Sufi tariqa: a path that is not merely a map but a living guide whose presence reorients desire. “Seeing me sees the Father” brushes against Advaita’s claim that the real is known by identity rather than inspection; speculation: it stages a psychological move where the craving for an external proof is answered by an invitation to recognize the source already acting through the familiar.

    “Greater works” has a Zen-like twist: the teacher’s work is to make students capable of surpassing him, not by egoic conquest but by transmission that removes dependence. In Mahayana terms, compassion scales through delegation: the community becomes the miracle.
