    IIILectio2 Timothy 1:1-3,6-12

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Second Letter of St. Paul to Timothy

    2 Timothy 1:1-3,6-12 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ, by the will of God, according to the promise of life which is in Christ Jesus:

    ☞R 1
    Apostleship is not self-authorization; it is will and promise, not résumé.

    2To Timothy, my dearly beloved son. Grace, mercy and peace, from God the Father and from Christ Jesus our Lord.

    3I give thanks to God, whom I serve from my forefathers, with a pure conscience, that without ceasing I have a remembrance of thee in my prayers, night and day.

    6For which cause I admonish thee that thou stir up the grace of God which is in thee by the imposition of my hands.

    ☞R 6
    Grace is embers: it must be stirred, not merely possessed.

    7For God hath not given us the spirit of fear: but of power and of love and of sobriety.

    ☞R 7
    Fear is named as alien; the given Spirit has a different grammar: power, love, sobriety.

    8Be not thou therefore ashamed of the testimony of our Lord, nor of me his prisoner: but labour with the gospel, according to the power of God.

    ☞R 8
    Shame is the social weapon; Paul answers with a chain and calls it fellowship.

    9Who hath delivered us and called us by his holy calling, not according to our own works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before the times of the world:

    10But is now made manifest by the illumination of our Saviour Jesus Christ, who hath destroyed death and hath brought to light life and incorruption by the gospel. By the illumination... That is, by the bright coming and appearing of our Saviour.

    11Wherein I am appointed a preacher and an apostle and teacher of the Gentiles.

    12For which cause, I also suffer these things: but I am not ashamed. For I know whom I have believed and I am certain that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him, against that day.

    ☞R 12
    Faith is custody: a deposit committed, a Keeper, and a fixed day.

    IVEvangeliumMark 12:18-27

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Mark

    Mark 12:18-27 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    18And there came to him the Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection. And they asked him, saying:

    19Master, Moses wrote unto us that if any man's brother die and leave his wife behind him and leave no children, his brother should take his wife and raise up seed to his brother.

    20Now there were seven brethren: and the first took a wife and died leaving no issue.

    21And the second took her and died: and neither did he leave any issue. And the third in like manner.

    22And the seven all took her in like manner and did not leave issue. Last of all the woman also died.

    23In the resurrection therefore, when they shall rise again, whose wife shall she be of them? For the seven had her to wife.

    ☞G 23
    They reduce resurrection to property law; the question is a trap built from categories that expire.

    24And Jesus answering, saith to them: Do ye not therefore err, because you know not the scriptures nor the power of God?

    ☞G 24
    Double ignorance: text without God’s power becomes clever unbelief.

    25For when they shall rise again from the dead, they shall neither marry, nor be married, but are as the angels in heaven.

    ☞G 25
    Resurrection is not extension of the old order; it is a new mode of life beyond marriage.

    26And as concerning the dead that they rise again have you not read in the book of Moses, how in the bush God spoke to him, saying: I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob?

    ☞G 26
    God names himself by living persons; covenant speech refuses to memorialize.

    27He is not the God of the dead, but of the living. You therefore do greatly err.

    ☞G 27
    The living God makes “dead” an interpretive mistake, not a final state.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s covenant identity entails resurrection: the living God cannot be finally related to the dead. Therefore Christian courage is rational—what is entrusted to Christ is kept against the Day, and shame has no jurisdiction.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2,3Paul frames ministry as inherited service and filial address: continuity with “forefathers” and intimacy with Timothy. The letter’s authority is relational before it is argumentative.
    • R 9,10,11The logic runs from eternal purpose to historical manifestation: grace “before the times” becomes visible in Christ’s appearing, and that appearing reorders Paul’s vocation toward the Gentiles. Suffering is presented as a consequence of that disclosure, not an accident.

    In Evangelium

    • G 18,19,26Jesus answers a denial of resurrection by appealing to Moses, the Sadducees’ own ground. He argues from God’s self-identification, not from speculative mechanics, forcing the dispute onto the nature of God and covenant rather than onto hypothetical edge-cases.
    • G 20,21,22The repeated deaths and childlessness expose the limits of levirate logic: it can preserve a name but cannot generate life. The narrative’s monotony is the point—law cannot outpace mortality.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “not a spirit of fear” is a tell: fear is already in the room, thick as prison air. He writes like a man trying to hypnotize his beloved son into courage, but the spell is made of pressure. “Stir up the grace” means keep the fire going or it dies; the laying on of hands is less a blessing than a claim. He calls himself “prisoner” and dares Timothy not to be ashamed, which is another way of admitting shame is the natural response. The promise of “life” sounds like a counterweight strapped onto dread.

    Mark stages a legalistic sadist’s joke: a woman passed hand to hand, seven times, and still no child, no future, only a chain of deaths. The Sadducees weaponize marriage as property law to make resurrection look ridiculous. Jesus doesn’t rescue romance; he abolishes the whole economy of belonging. No marriage, no being given, no contracts to stabilize desire. Resurrection is not the restoration of what we can manage; it is the end of our arrangements.

    Both texts turn on the same terror: what if death is not only an ending but an exposure, stripping away the roles we used to justify our lives? Paul clings to a Keeper “against that day” because he knows he has committed himself beyond recall. Jesus names God as present tense—of the living—so the dead can’t be used as arguments. The comfort is sharp: you will live, but not as possession, not as legacy, not as the self you can explain.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “spirit of power and love and sobriety” has the same inner geometry as the Bhagavad Gita’s call to act without paralysis, with steadiness and devotion; different metaphysics, similar psychology: fear contracts the self, disciplined love dilates it. Speculation: the “deposit” he entrusts resembles a yogic samskara reversed—an imprint not of habit but of vowed identity held against time.

    Jesus’ refusal to let resurrection be reduced to marital bookkeeping echoes the Upanishadic move where ultimate reality is not an extension of social roles but their transcendence; persons are not erased, but relations are reconfigured beyond ownership. The “God of the living” also brushes against Stoic providence and the Daoist sense that the real is what cannot be trapped in legalistic categories; speculation: the Sadducees’ puzzle is a defense mechanism—turning mystery into an absurdity so they can keep control.

    “Like the angels” parallels Platonic eros purified into contemplation and, more charitably, certain Buddhist depictions of non-clinging where attachment is not condemned but outgrown; the point is not anti-body but anti-possession. The burning bush name—present tense being—has an affinity with Vedantic sat and with Ibn Arabi’s emphasis on the Real as ever-actual; speculation: it’s an existential therapy, anchoring courage in a reality that doesn’t die, so shame loses its leverage.
