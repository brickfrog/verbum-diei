Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    LaudsAd Laudes06:00
    Lord, almighty God, you have brought us to the beginning of this day; save us today by your power, that we may not fall into any sin, but that our words, thoughts, and actions may follow the way of your commandments. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    TerceAd Tertiam09:00
    O God, Father full of goodness, you have willed that we should work in such a way that, by cooperating with one another, we might achieve ever greater success; help us, then, to live amid our labors, always aware that we are your children and brothers and sisters of all. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    SextAd Sextam12:00
    Lord, you are the master of the vineyard and the fields; you assign the tasks and give the workers their just wage. Help us to bear the burden of the day and the heat of the journey without ever complaining of your designs. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    NonesAd Nonam15:00
    You have called us together, Lord, into your presence at that very hour when the apostles were going up to the temple for the evening prayer; grant that the supplications we now address to you in the name of Jesus, your Son, may obtain salvation for all who invoke this name. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    VespersAd Vesperas18:00
    Almighty and eternal God, who have willed to help us in the work that we, your poor servants, have carried out today, as we come to the end of this day, accept our evening offering, in which we give you thanks for all the benefits we have received from you. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    ComplineAd Completorium21:00

    IIILectio1 Corinthians 2:1-5

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 2:1-5 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And I, brethren, when I came to you, came not in loftiness of speech or of wisdom, declaring unto you the testimony of Christ.

    ☞R 1
    Renounces rhetorical capital; testimony, not performance.

    2For I judged not myself to know anything among you, but Jesus Christ: and him crucified.

    ☞R 2
    Knowledge narrowed to a single scandal: crucifixion.

    3And I was with you in weakness and in fear and in much trembling.

    ☞R 3
    Apostolic presence marked by bodily instability.

    4And my speech and my preaching was not in the persuasive words of human wisdom, but in shewing of the Spirit and power:

    ☞R 4
    Proof shifts from argument to manifestation: Spirit as evidence.

    5That your faith might not stand on the wisdom of men, but on the power of God.

    ☞R 5
    Faith must not be collateralized by intellect; it rests on force.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 4:16-30

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 4:16-30 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    16And he came to Nazareth, where he was brought up: and he went into the synagogue, according to his custom, on the sabbath day: and he rose up to read.

    17And the book of Isaias the prophet was delivered unto him. And as he unfolded the book, he found the place where it was written:

    ☞G 17
    He does not receive a text; he selects a target.

    18The spirit of the Lord is upon me. Wherefore he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor, he hath sent me to heal the contrite of heart,

    19To preach deliverance to the captives and sight to the blind, to set at liberty them that are bruised, to preach the acceptable year of the Lord and the day of reward.

    20And when he had folded the book, he restored it to the minister and sat down. And the eyes of all in the synagogue were fixed on him.

    ☞G 20
    Sits: the posture of authority; the room becomes a tribunal.

    21And he began to say to them: This day is fulfilled this scripture in your ears.

    ☞G 21
    Fulfillment is timed, not abstract; the present tense judges.

    22And all gave testimony to him. And they wondered at the words of grace that proceeded from his mouth. And they said: Is not this the son of Joseph?

    ☞G 22
    Grace heard, pedigree demanded; familiarity as refusal.

    23And he said to them: Doubtless you will say to me this similitude: Physician, heal thyself. As great things as we have heard done in Capharnaum, do also here in thy own country.

    24And he said: Amen I say to you that no prophet is accepted in his own country.

    ☞G 24
    Prophecy fails locally; proximity breeds contempt.

    25In truth I say to You, there were many widows in the days of Elias in Israel, when heaven was shut up three years and six months, when there was a great famine throughout all the earth.

    26And to none of them was Elias sent, but to Sarepta of Sidon, to a widow woman.

    ☞G 26
    Election crosses borders; Israel’s need does not guarantee Israel’s claim.

    27And there were many lepers in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet: and none of them was cleansed but Naaman the Syrian.

    ☞G 27
    Election crosses borders; Israel’s need does not guarantee Israel’s claim.

    28And all they in the synagogue, hearing these things, were filled with anger.

    29And they rose up and thrust him out of the city: and they brought him to the brow of the hill whereon their city was built, that they might cast him down headlong.

    30But he passing through the midst of them, went his way.

    ☞G 30
    Violence cannot arrest him; sovereignty moves through the mob.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod discloses himself by sovereign power rather than human prestige, and his mercy is not owed to the familiar or the near. The crucified Christ and the rejected prophet are the same divine judgment on entitlement.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,2Paul’s strategy is a deliberate self-emptying so that the content—Christ crucified—cannot be mistaken for a product of elite culture. The messenger is made weak to prevent the message from being domesticated as a school of thought.
    • R 4,5The passage sets an epistemology: God is known by God’s own act, not by human technique. The community’s cohesion is secured by divine agency rather than by the charisma or competence of its founder.

    In Evangelium

    • G 16,18,19Jesus enters the ordinary rhythms of Nazareth and turns liturgy into announcement: the Isaianic program names a reversal of status—poor, captive, blind, bruised—placing divine favor where the town’s social imagination least expects it.
    • G 23,25,28,29The conflict is not over miracles but over entitlement. By invoking Elijah and Elisha, he frames God’s mercy as free and outward-facing; the synagogue’s rage exposes a piety that wants privilege without conversion.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “weakness and trembling” isn’t humility as virtue; it’s strategy as symptom. He refuses “loftiness” because eloquence would make the message negotiable, a thing you can admire without obeying. He brings only the crucified body, the scandal that collapses taste, status, and the safe distance of ideas. Faith, for him, is not persuasion but capture: a dependence engineered by stripping away every other support.

    Jesus in Nazareth stages the same ambush. He reads liberation, then turns it into insult. They want hometown dividends, a miracle as proof of belonging, but he offers them a mirror in which they are not the poor, not the captive, not the chosen audience. He names outsiders—Sidon’s widow, Syria’s leper—as the true recipients, and the congregation’s desire curdles into rage because he has touched the real wound: they don’t want salvation, they want preference.

    The “acceptable year” arrives as deprivation. The good news is that grace is not theirs to manage, and that is why they try to throw him off a cliff. He passes through them because the violence is the point: a power that won’t flatter must be escaped or destroyed, and it keeps walking anyway, leaving them with their furious hunger.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s refusal of rhetorical dominance has the flavor of Zen’s distrust of “special understanding” and the Tao Te Ching’s suspicion of clever speech: authority that arrives unarmed, so the hearer can’t outsource assent to technique. It also resembles the Sufi preference for hal over qāl, state over talk, where trembling is not incompetence but the body registering contact with what exceeds it.

    Nazareth’s offense is the psychology of the familiar: the ego’s demand that the transcendent stay domesticated, payable in local currency, verified by hometown favors. That recoil echoes the Buddha’s warning that attachment to views turns medicine into poison, and it parallels the Hindu motif of the avatāra unrecognized in one’s own house, the divine hidden by proximity.

    The turn to the widow of Zarephath and Naaman the Syrian reads like the Qur’anic insistence (speculation) that guidance is not a tribal entitlement, and like Stoic cosmopolitanism’s claim that virtue is not inherited by citizenship. The crowd’s rage is the violence of wounded status: when grace crosses borders, prestige feels robbed.

    The passing through their midst suggests the Bhagavad Gītā’s image of the self that cannot be seized, and the Daoist sage who is unharmed because he does not contend. Not escape artistry, but a kind of ungraspability: the sacred refusing to be reduced to a spectacle or a scapegoat.
