    IIILectio1 Corinthians 1:17-25

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 1:17-25 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    17For Christ sent me not to baptize, but to preach the gospel: not in wisdom of speech, lest the cross of Christ should be made void.

    ☞R 17
    Rhetoric is treated as a solvent: eloquence can dissolve the cross into “void.”

    18For the word of the cross, to them indeed that perish, is foolishness: but to them that are saved, that is, to us, it is the power of God.

    ☞R 18
    A single utterance splits humanity: “word of the cross” as sieve, not slogan.

    19For it is written: I will destroy the wisdom of the wise: and the prudence of the prudent I will reject.

    20Where is the wise? Where is the scribe? Where is the disputer of this world? Hath not God made foolish the wisdom of this world?

    21For, seeing that in the wisdom of God, the world, by wisdom, knew not God, it pleased God, by the foolishness of our preaching, to save them that believe.

    ☞R 21
    God’s “pleased” is an affront: salvation routed through what the world calls absurd.

    22For both the Jews require signs: and the Greeks seek after wisdom.

    23But we preach Christ crucified: unto the Jews indeed a stumblingblock, and unto the Gentiles foolishness:

    ☞R 23
    Crucified Messiah: scandal to the religious, comedy to the cultured—same object, different recoil.

    24But unto them that are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ, the power of God and the wisdom of God.

    25For the foolishness of God is wiser than men: and the weakness of God is stronger than men.

    ☞R 25
    Divine “weakness” is not a defect but a comparative weapon: it wins by losing.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 25:1-13

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 25:1-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Then shall the kingdom of heaven be like to ten virgins, who taking their lamps went out to meet the bridegroom and the bride.

    ☞G 1
    They go out with lamps, not with certainty; procession begins in partial light.

    2And five of them were foolish and five wise.

    3But the five foolish, having taken their lamps, did not take oil with them.

    ☞G 3
    Folly is not having no lamp; it is having no reserve.

    4But the wise took oil in their vessels with the lamps.

    5And the bridegroom tarrying, they all slumbered and slept.

    ☞G 5
    Delay equalizes the field: all sleep; vigilance is not insomnia.

    6And at midnight there was a cry made: Behold the bridegroom cometh. Go ye forth to meet him.

    7Then all those virgins arose and trimmed their lamps.

    8And the foolish said to the wise: Give us of your oil, for our lamps are gone out.

    9The wise answered, saying: Lest perhaps there be not enough for us and for you, go ye rather to them that sell and buy for yourselves.

    ☞G 9
    Refusal is framed as arithmetic, not malice: readiness is not transferable.

    10Now whilst they went to buy the bridegroom came: and they that were ready went in with him to the marriage. And the door was shut.

    ☞G 10
    The decisive act is banal—arrival—yet it seals history: “the door was shut.”

    11But at last came also the other virgins, saying: Lord, Lord, open to us.

    12But he answering said: Amen I say to you, I know you not.

    ☞G 12
    “I know you not” makes ignorance judicial: exclusion as verdict, not accident.

    13Watch ye therefore, because you know not the day nor the hour.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod saves by the crucified Christ, overturning the world’s measures of wisdom and power. The kingdom admits only those whose readiness endures delay; the shut door ratifies what their preparation has made them.

    In Lectionem

    • R 19,20,22,24Paul stages a courtroom against the world’s epistemology: Scripture is subpoenaed, the intellectual classes are cross-examined, and the verdict is that God’s self-disclosure is not accessible by the world’s preferred instruments. The cross functions as God’s chosen criterion of recognition, so “calling” names the act by which perception itself is re-made.

    In Evangelium

    • G 2,4,6,7,13The parable is an eschatological sorting that turns on time: the same invitation, the same night, the same summons, but different preparedness when the moment arrives. Wisdom is defined as provision for delay; the midnight cry exposes what was already true, and the final imperative to watch is a command to live under an unknown deadline.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul isn’t praising ignorance; he’s staging an attack on the ego’s favorite drug: being right. “Wisdom” is the polished story we tell to keep suffering at arm’s length. The cross is obscene because it won’t cooperate with our need for a world that makes sense. It humiliates the mind that wants to master God as an idea, and it seduces the part of us that secretly longs to be undone, to have our arguments finally fail.

    The virgins aren’t sorted by morality but by appetite. All of them sleep; vigilance isn’t insomnia. The wise carry oil because they’ve already consented to delay, to longing without payoff, to the slow burn of desire that can’t be borrowed at the last minute. The foolish aren’t “bad”; they’re consumers of the immediate, expecting the world to be stocked when they finally feel like showing up.

    The cruel line—“I know you not”—lands like a psychological verdict: you can’t improvise intimacy at midnight. When the moment arrives, only what has been tended in secret gives light. The door shuts not as punishment but as reality hardening. The gospel’s dread is that the feast comes, and your lamp reveals what you actually loved.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “foolish” cross and Matthew’s “wise” oil meet in the same psychological hinge: the ego wants credentials—signs, arguments, last-minute borrowing—while the soul is trained by waiting, loss, and unshareable readiness. The lamp is persona; the oil is interior formation that cannot be outsourced when the night turns. What looks like weakness is a deliberate refusal to win on the world’s terms, because winning that way would only enthrone the same anxious self that needs proof.

    Semina Verbi: Zen’s “beginner’s mind” and the koan tradition (speculation) echo a wisdom that arrives by surrendering the clever answer. Sufism’s fana names a strength discovered through self-emptying rather than display. The Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga, action without grasping at results, parallels readiness that accrues quietly over time. Stoic prohairesis, the inner faculty of choice, resembles oil as a private reserve no one can lend at the crisis. In Jewish mysticism, the hiddenness of God and the discipline of watchfulness (speculation) rhyme with a midnight arrival that defeats prediction and rewards preparation over spectacle.
