    Lord Jesus Christ, who at the hour of Sext went up upon the cross for our salvation while darkness covered the world, grant that your light may always shine upon us, so that, guided by it, we may attain eternal life. You who live and reign for ever and ever.

    IIILectio1 Corinthians 15:12-20

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 15:12-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12Now if Christ be preached, that he arose again from the dead, how do some among you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?

    ☞R 12
    A preached fact meets a local denial; doctrine as social contagion.

    13But if there be no resurrection of the dead, then Christ is not risen again.

    14And if Christ be not risen again, then is our preaching vain: and your faith is also vain.

    ☞R 14
    Two collapses at once: kerygma and credence; emptiness is contagious.

    15Yea, and we are found false witnesses of God: because we have given testimony against God, that he hath raised up Christ, whom he hath not raised up, if the dead rise not again.

    ☞R 15
    Apostles risk perjury against God; the scandal is not error but blasphemous testimony.

    16For if the dead rise not again, neither is Christ risen again.

    17And if Christ be not risen again, your faith is vain: for you are yet in your sins.

    ☞R 17
    No rising, no acquittal; sin remains a present-tense jurisdiction.

    18Then they also that are fallen asleep in Christ are perished.

    19If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable.

    ☞R 19
    Hope reduced to lifespan becomes a cruelty; consolation turns indictment.

    20But now Christ is risen from the dead, the firstfruits of them that sleep:

    ☞R 20
    Firstfruits: resurrection as harvest logic, not isolated miracle; sleepers are seed.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 8:1-3

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 8:1-3 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And it came to pass afterwards he travelled through the cities and towns, preaching and evangelizing the kingdom of God: and the twelve with him:

    ☞G 1
    Kingdom announced on foot; itinerancy as refusal of settled power.

    2And certain women who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities: Mary who is called Magdalen, out of whom seven devils were gone forth,

    ☞G 2
    Seven devils: total occupation, total eviction; the number is a verdict.

    3And Joanna the wife of Chusa, Herod's steward, and Susanna and many others who ministered unto him of their substance.

    ☞G 3
    Herod’s household leaks resources to the rival reign; patronage defecting from the court.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChrist’s resurrection is the non-negotiable premise of Christian proclamation; deny it and you void both faith and forgiveness. The kingdom advances by creating witnesses and reallocating allegiance, even from within hostile powers.

    In Lectionem

    • R 13,16,18Paul builds a reductio that makes denial of the general resurrection logically eat the particular resurrection, then forces the community to face the downstream casualties: Christ’s status, the dead in Christ, and the entire economy of salvation. The argument is not mystical but juridical and communal: what you say about the dead rewrites what you are allowed to say about God.
    • R 12,20The passage turns on a public claim (“preached”) and a public reversal (“now”): resurrection is treated as the hinge of proclamation, not a private comfort. The final assertion functions as a legal settlement after cross-examination—Paul ends the dispute by re-stating the datum that governs all other speech.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,3Luke frames the kingdom’s advance as a moving court with a mixed retinue: the Twelve as official witnesses, and women as materially sustaining agents. The narrative quietly relocates legitimacy from palace administration to a traveling proclamation, showing the kingdom’s infrastructure forming inside and against existing political economies.
    • G 2The healed women are not decorative; they are evidence. Deliverance and funding are paired to show that the kingdom creates both new persons and new patterns of allegiance.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul sounds less like a herald and more like a man cornered by his own invention. His argument is a dare: take away resurrection and you don’t just lose comfort, you expose the whole enterprise as a calculated lie, a public perjury staged “against God.” The dread is not metaphysical; it’s reputational and psychic. Without the rising, the community becomes a support group for a beautiful fraud, and the dead are not “asleep” but erased. He admits the secret fear underneath piety: that faith is a bargain for meaning, and if the receipt is counterfeit then you are not merely sad, you are ridiculous.

    Luke then shows how the bargain is funded. While the twelve trail behind the preacher like official witnesses, the movement is materially carried by women with damaged histories and sharp needs. “Healed of evil spirits” is the social label for what they’ve survived; “ministered of their substance” is the erotic economy of gratitude and dependence turned into patronage. The kingdom advances on coins and devotion, on bodies that have been reclaimed and now refuse to go back. Resurrection here is less a doctrine than a hunger: the desire to have one’s ruined life count for something, to convert stigma into authority. The tension is that the whole story rests on those most vulnerable to longing—and most capable of paying for it.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s logic has the hard edge of Zen: if awakening is only an idea, it is not awakening. Speculation: his “firstfruits” sits near the Buddhist claim that one realized life proves the path is real, not merely consoling.

    His insistence that a faith without resurrection is self-deception echoes the Stoic demand that philosophy cash out in a transformed life rather than rhetoric, and the Vedantic suspicion that mere belief without liberation is vanity. Speculation: “still in your sins” reads like karma unbroken, the old causal chain still running.

    Luke’s women patrons resemble the dana economy around the Buddha and the lay networks that sustained Sufi orders: healing draws loyalty, and loyalty becomes material support. Their presence also matches the bhakti pattern where devotion is not peripheral but infrastructural, carried by those outside official rank.

    Mary Magdalene’s exorcism parallels shamanic and Daoist accounts of spirit-affliction resolved by a healer, after which the healed become witnesses and benefactors. Speculation: the text treats restored agency as the first miracle, and money as its quiet proof.
