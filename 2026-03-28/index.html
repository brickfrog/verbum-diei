Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

Ezekiel 37:21-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

21And thou shalt say to them: Thus saith the Lord God: Behold, I will take of the children of Israel from the midst of the nations whither they are gone: and I will gather them on every side, and will bring them to their own land.

☞R 21
Exile is assumed; return is performed by fiat, not by merit.

22And I will make them one nation in the land on the mountains of Israel, and one king shall be king over them all: and they shall no more be two nations, neither shall they be divided any more into two kingdoms.

☞R 22
Unity is political theology: one king cancels schism.

23Nor shall they be defiled any more with their idols, nor with their abominations, nor with all their iniquities: and I will save them out of all the places in which they have sinned, and I will cleanse them: and they shall be my people, and I will be their God.

☞R 23
Cleansing precedes belonging; “my people” is downstream of purification.

24And my servant David shall be king over them, and they shall have one shepherd: they shall walk in my judgments, and shall keep my commandments, and shall do them.

25And they shall dwell in the land which I gave to my servant Jacob, wherein your fathers dwelt, and they shall dwell in it, they and their children, and their children's children, for ever: and David my servant shall be their prince for ever.

26And I will make a covenant of peace with them, it shall be an everlasting covenant with them: and I will establish them, and will multiply them, and will set my sanctuary in the midst of them for ever.

☞R 26
“Covenant of peace” is not détente but sanctuary-installation: God moves in.

27And my tabernacle shall be with them: and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.

28And the nations shall know that I am the Lord the sanctifier of Israel, when my sanctuary shall be in the midst of them for ever.

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to John

John 11:45-56 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

45Many therefore of the Jews, who were come to Mary and Martha and had seen the things that Jesus did, believed in him.

46But some of them went to the Pharisees and told them the things that Jesus had done.

47The chief priests, therefore, and the Pharisees gathered a council and said: What do we, for this man doth many miracles?

48If we let him alone so, all will believe in him; and the Romans will come, and take away our place and nation.

☞G 48
They fear Rome more than God; “place and nation” are their idol.

49But one of them, named Caiphas, being the high priest that year, said to them: You know nothing.

50Neither do you consider that it is expedient for you that one man should die for the people and that the whole nation perish not.

☞G 50
Sacrifice is framed as management: murder rebranded as prudence.

51And this he spoke not of himself: but being the high priest of that year, he prophesied that Jesus should die for the nation.

☞G 51
Office speaks truer than the man: prophecy leaks through corruption.

52And not only for the nation, but to gather together in one the children of God that were dispersed.

53From that day therefore they devised to put him to death.

54Wherefore Jesus walked no more openly among the Jews: but he went into a country near the desert, unto a city that is called Ephrem. And there he abode with his disciples.

☞G 54
The Word withdraws; visibility becomes a tactical question.

55And the pasch of the Jews was at hand: and many from the country went up to Jerusalem, before the pasch, to purify themselves.

56They sought therefore for Jesus; and they discoursed one with another, standing in the temple: What think you that he is not come to the festival day? And the chief priests and Pharisees had given a commandment that, if any man knew where he was, he should tell, that they might apprehend him.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod unifies his people by installing his presence and shepherd-king; that unity is achieved in history through the death of the one man who gathers the dispersed into one.

In Lectionem

  • R 24,25The restoration is cast as a revived monarchy under “David,” yet the horizon is “for ever,” pressing beyond any historical Davidide. The text binds land, law, and ruler into a single regime ordered to permanence, implying a messianic administration rather than a temporary repair.
  • R 27,28The climax is missional: the sanctuary’s indwelling functions as public proof, converting Israel’s holiness into an international sign. God’s self-identification (“sanctifier”) is validated not by argument but by presence.

In Evangelium

  • G 45,46,47Belief produces a counter-movement of reporting; witness becomes evidence for prosecution. The council scene formalizes resistance: miracles are treated as a governance problem, not a revelation.
  • G 52,53,55,56John frames the coming death as the mechanism of a new unity—gathering the dispersed—while the narrative immediately tightens into pursuit and festival anticipation. Passover purification and the hunt for Jesus run in parallel, exposing a cultic system preparing itself while preparing to kill its true Pasch.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Ezekiel’s dream of unity is not tenderness; it is consolidation. The scattered are gathered like filings to a magnet, cleaned of their “abominations,” and fused into one body with one shepherd. Peace arrives as surveillance: sanctuary in the middle, tabernacle pitched inside the psyche, the holy as an occupying force that promises forever by abolishing exit.

John shows the same mechanism without the incense. The council’s fear is naked: belief is a political contagion, Rome the looming punishment. Caiaphas speaks the logic everyone tries not to say: kill the singular to save the collective, sacrifice as public health. His “prophecy” is not divine poetry but institutional reflex, the priesthood’s talent for turning murder into necessity.

The dread is that both texts want the same thing: one nation, one center, one story, one body. Desire for wholeness demands a victim, and the victim becomes the glue. Jesus withdraws into the desert not as mystic escape but as prey learning the map; meanwhile the temple crowd hunts for absence, needing him to appear so they can complete the unity that requires his removal.

Semina Verbi

Hinduism’s Purusha Sukta carries a stark kinship: the social body is knit together through a single sacrificial victim, unity purchased by dismemberment; speculation, but Caiaphas’ cold calculus rhymes with that archaic intuition that cohesion requires blood.

Greek tragedy offers the pharmakos, the scapegoat expelled or killed to cleanse the city; the council’s fear of Rome sounds like a polis managing contagion, turning a living person into a public remedy.

Buddhism’s sangha ideal and Ashoka’s edicts echo the dream of a dispersed people gathered into one disciplined community, yet without a throne; the “one shepherd” becomes, by analogy, a rule of life rather than a dynasty.

Daoism’s notion of de as an ordering presence resembles a sanctuary “in the midst” not as architecture but as a field that harmonizes; speculation, but the text’s center feels less like a building than an atmosphere that makes division psychologically harder.

Sufism’s language of the qutb, the spiritual pole around whom hearts align, parallels the longing for a single center that sanctifies; the danger, shared across traditions, is mistaking institutional survival for holiness and calling it peace.
