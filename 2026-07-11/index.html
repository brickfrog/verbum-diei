    IIILectioIsaiah 6:1-8

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Isaiah

    Isaiah 6:1-8 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1In the year that king Ozias died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and elevated: and his train filled the temple.

    ☞R 1
    A king dies; the real King is seen. Political vacancy becomes theophany.

    2Upon it stood the seraphims: the one had six wings, and the other had six wings: with two they covered his face, and with two they covered his feet, and with two they flew.

    ☞R 2
    Six wings: excess for creatures, yet still insufficient—so they spend it on concealment.

    3And they cried one to another, and said: Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God of hosts, all the earth is full of his glory,

    ☞R 3
    Thrice-holy: language pushed past emphasis into liturgy; earth already saturated with what eyes miss.

    4And the lintels of the doors were moved at the voice of him that cried, and the house was filled with smoke.

    5And I said: Woe is me, because I have held my peace; because I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people that hath unclean lips, and I have seen with my eyes the King the Lord of hosts.

    ☞R 5
    The prophet’s crisis is verbal: unclean lips, held peace. Vision indicts speech.

    6And one of the seraphims flew to me, and in his hand was a live coal, which he had taken with the tongs off the altar.

    ☞R 6
    Fire is mediated by tongs: holiness is not handled bare, even by seraphs.

    7And he touched my mouth, and said: Behold this hath touched thy lips, and thy iniquities shall be taken away, and thy sin shall be cleansed.

    ☞R 7
    Mouth touched; guilt removed. Purification is surgical, not sentimental.

    8And I heard the voice of the Lord, saying: Whom shall I send? and who shall go for us? And I said: Lo, here am I, send me.

    ☞R 8
    “Who shall go for us?”—plural counsel; the volunteer answers before terms are stated.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 10:24-33

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 10:24-33 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    24The disciple is not above the master, nor the servant above his lord.

    ☞G 24
    No promotion above the Master; the ceiling is likeness, not superiority.

    25It is enough for the disciple that he be as his master, and the servant as his lord. If they have called the good man of the house Beelzebub, how much more them of his household?

    ☞G 25
    If the householder is branded demonic, the household should expect inherited slander.

    26Therefore fear them not. For nothing is covered that shall not be revealed: nor hid, that shall not be known.

    ☞G 26
    Revelation is a pressure in history: coverings fail; secrets leak into daylight.

    27That which I tell you in the dark, speak ye in the light: and that which you hear in the ear, preach ye upon the housetops.

    28And fear ye not them that kill the body, and are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him that can destroy both soul and body in hell.

    ☞G 28
    Recalibrate fear: bodies are cheap to kill; the soul has a Judge.

    29Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and not one of them shall fall on the ground without your Father.

    ☞G 29
    Providence counts the negligible: a sparrow’s fall is not outside attention.

    30But the very hairs of your head are all numbered.

    31Fear not therefore: better are you than many sparrows.

    32Every one therefore that shall confess me before men, I will also confess him before my Father who is in heaven.

    ☞G 32
    Confession is reciprocal litigation: speak for Christ now; Christ speaks for you then.

    33But he that shall deny me before men, I will also deny him before my Father who is in heaven.

    ☞G 33
    Denial purchases silence at the cost of being disowned in court.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s holiness both exposes and purges, then drafts the cleansed into public witness. Final judgment turns on confessed allegiance to Christ, not on managed reputation.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4The scene is not private ecstasy but cosmic destabilization: thresholds shake, the house smokes. The temple becomes a site where created architecture registers the weight of the uncreated.
    • R 1,8The narrative moves from enthronement to commission: sovereignty issues orders. The call is not self-chosen vocation but conscription following encounter.

    In Evangelium

    • G 27,31The discourse turns disciples into public instruments: what is received in concealment must be externalized. The command to speak is grounded not in optimism but in a valuation of life under the Father’s surveillance.
    • G 24,32,33Identity is judicial and relational: alignment with the Master determines standing before the Father. The text frames discipleship as representation with consequences, not mere imitation.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Isaiah’s vision isn’t comfort; it’s regime change in the psyche. The king dies and immediately a larger King appears, filling the room like a suffocating fact. The seraphim cover face and feet because even they can’t bear the exposure of being seen by what sees everything. “Holy” is not praise but a warning siren; the thresholds shake, smoke pours in, and Isaiah’s first honest sentence is not devotion but panic. His “unclean lips” are not quaint guilt; they are the discovery that his whole life has been a practiced silence, a collaboration with the dirty language of his people. He is terrified not because he is small, but because he has been hiding.

    The coal is not gentle forgiveness; it is forced speech. It burns the mouth into an instrument, cauterizes the old muteness, and makes him usable. Then comes the chilling question: “Who will go for us?” God sounds like a court that needs a courier, a power that recruits. Isaiah’s “Send me” is not heroic volunteerism; it is the reflex of someone who has just been remade by fire and cannot return to ordinary life.

    Matthew continues the same coercive intimacy. You will be treated like the master, which means slander first, then danger. “Fear not” repeats like a spell because fear is the correct bodily response to what’s being asked. Everything hidden will be revealed: not liberation, but exposure. The disciples are commanded to drag the secret into daylight, to make private hearing into public risk.

    And the dread sharpens: do not fear the ones who can kill you; fear the one who can destroy you entirely. The sparrows and the numbered hairs are not sentimental; they are surveillance rendered as tenderness. You are watched down to the follicle, valued and owned at once. Confess me, and I will confess you; deny me, and I will deny you. Desire here is the desire to be recognized by the ultimate Witness, and the price is to become a mouth that cannot close.

    Semina Verbi

    Isaiah’s coal-on-the-lips looks like a cross-cultural technology for turning private awe into public speech: Vedic Agni as mouth of the gods, purifying and carrying utterance; the Buddhist bodhisattva’s “lion’s roar,” where fear is metabolized into compassionate proclamation; and the Sufi image of burning that refines the tongue into truth. Speculation: the seraph is the psyche’s own immune system for conscience, cauterizing shame so vocation can survive.

    Matthew’s insistence on speaking “in the light” echoes the Cynic parrhesia and Stoic indifference to reputation: truth-telling as training against social terror. The sparrows and numbered hairs rhyme with Daoist non-anxiety about the small and overlooked, and with Hindu bhakti’s intimacy where the divine attends to the least. Speculation: confession here is less doctrinal badge than the hardening of identity under pressure, the moment a self chooses coherence over safety.
