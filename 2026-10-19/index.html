    IIILectioEphesians 2:1-10

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 2:1-10 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And you, when you were dead in your offences and sins,

    ☞R 1
    “Dead” names incapacity, not mere guilt.

    2Wherein in time past you walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of this air, of the spirit that now worketh on the children of unbelief:

    ☞R 2
    Three tyrannies: world, air-prince, unbelief’s interior engine.

    3In which also we all conversed in time past, in the desires of our flesh, fulfilling the will of the flesh and of our thoughts, and were by nature children of wrath, even as the rest:

    ☞R 3
    Flesh and thoughts: desire is both appetite and imagination; wrath is “by nature.”

    4But God (who is rich in mercy) for his exceeding charity wherewith he loved us

    5Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together in Christ (by whose grace you are saved)

    ☞R 5
    Quickened “together”: resurrection is corporate before it is private.

    6And hath raised us up together and hath made us sit together in the heavenly places, through Christ Jesus.

    7That he might shew in the ages to come the abundant riches of his grace, in his bounty towards us in Christ Jesus.

    8For by grace you are saved through faith: and that not of yourselves, for it is the gift of God.

    ☞R 8
    Faith is the conduit, not the cause; the gift excludes self-origin.

    9Not of works, that no man may glory.

    10For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus in good works, which God hath prepared that we should walk in them.

    ☞R 10
    Workmanship: the artisan owns the artifact; “prepared” works precede our walking.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 12:13-21

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 12:13-21 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    13And one of the multitude said to him: Master, speak to my brother that he divide the inheritance with me.

    ☞G 13
    He wants a verdict; he gets a diagnosis.

    14But he said to him: Man, who hath appointed me judge or divider over you?

    ☞G 14
    Refusal of arbitration: Christ will not be conscripted into property’s liturgy.

    15And he said to them: Take heed and beware of all covetousness: for a man's life doth not consist in the abundance of things which he possesseth.

    ☞G 15
    Life is not an inventory; abundance is a false ontology.

    16And he spoke a similitude to them, saying: The land of a certain rich man brought forth plenty of fruits.

    17And he thought within himself, saying: What shall I do, because I have no room where to bestow my fruits?

    18And he said: This will I do: I will pull down my barns and will build greater: and into them will I gather all things that are grown to me and my goods.

    ☞G 18
    Bigger barns: expansion as spiritual anesthesia.

    19And I will say to my soul: Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years. Take thy rest: eat, drink, make good cheer.

    ☞G 19
    He preaches to his “soul” the gospel of delay.

    20But God said to him: Thou fool, this night do they require thy soul of thee. And whose shall those things be which thou hast provided?

    ☞G 20
    “This night”: time collapses; the soul is requisitioned, not surrendered.

    21So is he that layeth up treasure for himself and is not rich towards God.

    ☞G 21
    Two riches: toward self or toward God; neutrality is excluded.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisSalvation is God’s unilateral vivification of the dead into a prepared life, excluding all boasting. Covetousness is the attempt to secure life by possession, and it ends in dispossession at God’s demand.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4,6,7,9The pivot is divine initiative: mercy and love (4) act on the dead, then enthrone them “in the heavenly places” (6). The purpose clause (7) frames salvation as an eschatological display of grace, not a human success story. The exclusion of boasting (9) is not moral modesty but a structural demolition of merit as a category for standing before God.

    In Evangelium

    • G 16,17,20,21The parable’s logic is internal monologue: the rich man’s world is a closed circuit of land, storage, and self-address (4–5). God’s interruption (8) exposes the hidden premise that time is owned and securable. The final maxim (9) defines the real deficit: not wealth as such, but wealth’s direction—self-referential accumulation that cannot survive the summons.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul and Luke are describing the same addiction: the fantasy of self-possession. “Dead in offences” doesn’t mean inert; it means compulsively alive, marching “according to the course of this world,” possessed by an air-thin authority that feels like your own thoughts. The dread is that your desires are not yours. You “fulfil the will of the flesh and of your thoughts” and call it freedom.

    Grace, here, is not a moral upgrade but an expropriation. “Not of works” is not comfort; it is humiliation. You don’t get to author your rescue, so you don’t get to take credit for your goodness. Even your “good works” are pre-written, “prepared” in advance. Salvation arrives as a theft of autonomy, a forced relocation into “heavenly places” while you are still breathing, still wanting.

    The man in the crowd wants Jesus to arbitrate property because he believes life is divisible, measurable, ownable. Jesus refuses the role, then tells a horror story: a rich man who speaks tenderly to his own soul like a lover, promising rest, pleasure, years. The barns are his attempt to build a womb against time. God’s interruption is surgical: “this night they require thy soul.” Not “you will die,” but “they will collect,” as if existence is a debt and the body is collateral.

    The punchline is not that wealth is bad; it is that accumulation is a spell you cast to keep death from touching you, and it works until it doesn’t. “Rich towards God” means rich in what cannot be stored: dependence, surrender, the terrifying poverty of being held. The desire underneath greed is immortality; the gospel answers with a demand: let yourself be owned or you will be emptied.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “dead in offenses” reads like the yogic diagnosis of avidyā: a living organism functioning, yet inwardly anesthetized, mistaking compulsion for freedom. Grace as an unearned quickening has a close cousin in Zen’s kenshō, the sudden release from the self’s cramped story; speculation: both name an interruption that cannot be manufactured by effort, only received and then embodied.

    The rich man’s barns are the Buddhist storehouse consciousness turned outward: accumulation as a bid to stabilize a self that is already dissolving. Stoicism’s memento mori cuts the same nerve—death as the ultimate audit that exposes surplus as fantasy. The Qur’anic critique of takāthur, rivalry in piling up, likewise treats hoarding as a spiritual misrecognition, not merely a moral fault.

    “We are his workmanship” resonates with the Daoist sense of being shaped by the Dao when one stops forcing outcomes; speculation: “prepared good works” parallels karma purified into right action, not as self-justification but as the natural gait of a mind no longer possessed by hunger.

    “Rich toward God” has an echo in Hindu bhakti: wealth redefined as intimacy, not inventory. And in Jewish wisdom, especially Qohelet, the barn-builder’s tragedy is not that he planned, but that he tried to outbuild impermanence, confusing possession with being.
