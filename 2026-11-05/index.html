    IIILectioPhilippians 3:3-8

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Philippians

    Philippians 3:3-8 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    3For we are the circumcision, who in spirit serve God and glory in Christ Jesus, not having confidence in the flesh.

    ☞R 3
    “Circumcision” relocated: not cut flesh but reoriented worship; boasting permitted only with a new object.

    4Though I might also have confidence in the flesh. If any other thinketh he may have confidence in the flesh, I more:

    5Being circumcised the eighth day, of the stock of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, an Hebrew of the Hebrews. According to the law, a Pharisee:

    ☞R 5
    Credentials stacked like coins; identity becomes inventory.

    6According to zeal, persecuting the church of God: According to the justice that is in the law, conversing without blame.

    ☞R 6
    Zeal turns predatory; “without blame” can coexist with violence.

    7But the things that were gain to me, the same I have counted loss for Christ.

    8Furthermore, I count all things to be but loss for the excellent knowledge of Jesus Christ, my Lord: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things and count them but as dung, that I may gain Christ.

    ☞R 8
    Knowledge priced as dispossession; the ledger ends in “dung,” a deliberate profanity against prestige.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 15:1-10

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 15:1-10 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Now the publicans and sinners drew near unto him to hear him.

    2And the Pharisees and the scribes murmured, saying: This man receiveth sinners and eateth with them.

    ☞G 2
    Murmuring is the soundtrack of threatened purity; table-fellowship is the scandal.

    3And he spoke to them this parable, saying:

    4What man of you that hath an hundred sheep, and if he shall lose one of them, doth he not leave the ninety-nine in the desert and go after that which was lost, until he find it?

    ☞G 4
    Arithmetic of mercy: the many are left exposed for the one; risk is assumed, not explained.

    5And when he hath found it, lay it upon his shoulders, rejoicing?

    ☞G 5
    The lost is carried, not scolded; recovery precedes instruction.

    6And coming home, call together his friends and neighbours, saying to them: Rejoice with me, because I have found my sheep that was lost?

    7I say to you that even so there shall be joy in heaven upon one sinner that doth penance, more than upon ninety-nine just who need not penance.

    ☞G 7
    Heaven’s joy is asymmetrical; the “ninety-nine” are a foil, not a metric.

    8Or what woman having ten groats, if she lose one groat, doth not light a candle and sweep the house and seek diligently until she find it?

    ☞G 8
    Domestic liturgy: light, sweep, seek—salvation staged as ordinary diligence.

    9And when she hath found it, call together her friends and neighbours, saying: Rejoice with me, because I have found the groat which I had lost.

    10So I say to you, there shall be joy before the angels of God upon one sinner doing penance.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s righteousness dismantles fleshly capital and reconstitutes worth around Christ alone. Divine joy is triggered by the recovery of the lost, and it authorizes scandalous mercy over guarded respectability.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4,7Paul frames conversion as a hostile takeover of his former economy: the self once secured by comparative advantage (“I more”) is liquidated by a single valuation—“for Christ.” The point is not that the old markers were false, but that they are rendered non-ultimate by a superior claim.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,3,10Luke sets the parables inside an auditory conflict: sinners come to hear, the religious class speaks about him, and Jesus answers them rather than the crowd. The stories justify his practice by locating it in heaven’s court—joy “before the angels”—so the dispute is not etiquette but whose judgment governs the community.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul isn’t escaping “confidence in the flesh”; he’s staging a controlled self-demolition. He recites his pedigree like a man stroking a weapon he loved, then spits on it. “Dung” is not piety; it’s disgust, the recoil of someone who knows how intoxicating righteousness feels and how easily it turns predatory. The old Paul enjoyed being right. The new Paul still feels that hunger, so he solves it by making Christ the only permissible obsession. The renunciation is a conversion of appetite, not its extinction.

    Luke’s shepherd is not a gentle moralist; he’s a single-minded hunter of the missing. He abandons the ninety-nine in the desert because the one lost sheep has become the itch he can’t stop scratching. The parable admits what the respectable fear: the community’s order is never as compelling as the scandal of the stray. Heaven’s “joy” reads like relief after obsession, a party thrown to cover the terror that one can slip the count, vanish, and expose how fragile the whole arrangement is.

    The murmuring Pharisees sense the threat correctly. If the lost are the center of gravity, then the “just” are background noise, their virtue strangely irrelevant. Desire moves toward the wound, not the well-kept. Paul and the shepherd share the same dark knowledge: holiness is not cleanliness but pursuit, and pursuit always leaves someone unattended.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s contempt for “confidence in the flesh” reads like a hard psychological break with résumé-identity, close to Stoic prohairesis where worth relocates from externals to the ruling choice, and to the Bhagavad Gita’s renunciation of fruit and status in favor of a single allegiance; speculation: it also resembles Zen’s suspicion of merit-badges and spiritual pedigree as just another costume for the self.

    The shepherd and the woman enact a love that refuses statistical thinking, nearer to the bodhisattva vow that turns back for the one still caught, and to the Sufi image of the Beloved pursuing the stray heart; speculation: the “joy in heaven” has kinship with the Jewish midrashic delight in teshuvah, where return is not mere correction but a celebration that reorders the whole house.
