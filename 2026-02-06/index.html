21And when a convenient day was come, Herod made a supper for his birthday, for the princes, and tribunes, and chief men of Galilee.

22And when the daughter of the same Herodias had come in, and had danced, and pleased Herod, and them that were at table with him, the king said to the damsel: Ask of me what thou wilt, and I will give it thee.

23And he swore to her: Whatsoever thou shalt ask I will give thee, though it be the half of my kingdom.

24Who when she was gone out, said to her mother, What shall I ask? But her mother said: The head of John the Baptist.

25And when she was come in immediately with haste to the king, she asked, saying: I will that forthwith thou give me in a dish, the head of John the Baptist.

26And the king was struck sad. Yet because of his oath, and because of them that were with him at table, he would not displease her:

27But sending an executioner, he commanded that his head should be brought in a dish.

28And he beheaded him in the prison, and brought his head in a dish: and gave to the damsel, and the damsel gave it her mother.

29Which his disciples hearing came, and took his body, and laid it in a tomb.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 7,9,10The passage edits kingship into doxology: the public achievements are narrated as praise-acts, so rule is legitimated by attribution. The political arc bends toward cultic order; the king’s greatness is measured by whether glory is returned upward rather than hoarded.
On the Gospel
  • G 17,19,20,22,26,29The narrative dissects weak authority: Herod is governed by a triangle—illicit bond, domestic resentment, public spectators. He oscillates between fascination with the holy and fear of losing face, and the banquet seals the trap: an oath spoken for an audience becomes a law stronger than conscience. The disciples’ burial is the only coherent act left; it exposes the court’s spectacle as sterile power.

Doctrinal God’s anointed power is ordered toward thanksgiving and worship; worldly power, ruled by appetite and audience, converts truth-telling into a trophy and calls it necessity.

Heterodox Reading
Sirach polishes David into a usable myth: the boy who pets predators, the man who turns slaughter into song. But the music is not an afterthought; it is the technology that makes violence feel like vocation. “With his whole heart” he loves the maker who authorizes his appetite. The sweet melody is the seal over a mouth that has tasted blood. Mark shows what happens when power loses the ability to sanctify itself. Herod is not a tyrant of conviction but of nerves. He “hears willingly” because John’s voice gives him the sensation of being judged, and that sensation feels like life. He keeps the prophet like a caged conscience, a dangerous pet. Then desire enters as performance: a dance, a room of watching men, a vow spoken to preserve an image. The head on a dish is not simply cruelty; it is the banquet’s true course, the price of male bravado and female resentment, the conversion of speech into meat. David’s singers make killing sound holy; Herod’s guests make it look inevitable. In both, dread and desire collaborate to turn a human throat into an instrument—either for praise or for silence.
Semina Verbi
David’s lion-play is the archetype of trained ferocity: the psyche rehearses danger until it becomes intimate, the way a Zen swordsman or a Shaolin adept makes fear into a familiar tool. It is not innocence; it is aggression disciplined into protection. The “sweet melody” before the altar turns violence into order, like Orphic song taming the underworld or the Vedic idea that right chant stabilizes the world; speculation: art here is the sublimation that keeps power from rotting into mere appetite. Herod is a study in weak will enthroned. He “fears” the just man and enjoys hearing him, yet lets his image at a banquet murder his conscience; this is Plato’s tyrant-soul, enslaved by desire and the gaze of others. The birthday feast becomes a Dionysian court where eros and spectacle write law, and the prophet’s severed head is the grisly anti-sacrament, closer to the scapegoat mechanism René Girard traces in many cultures. The oath functions like dharma misread as face-saving: a vow treated as fate, when it is only vanity hardened into destiny.
