    IIILectioEphesians 3:14-21

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 3:14-21 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    14For this cause I bow my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ,

    ☞R 14
    Knees: theology begins in posture, not speculation.

    15Of whom all paternity in heaven and earth is named:

    ☞R 15
    Paternity is not metaphor; it is a naming act that orders realms.

    16That he would grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened by his Spirit with might unto the inward man:

    ☞R 16
    Strength aimed at the “inward man”: the battlefield is interior.

    17That Christ may dwell by faith in your hearts: that, being rooted and founded in charity,

    ☞R 17
    Indwelling is conditional: faith is the door; charity is the foundation.

    18You may be able to comprehend, with all the saints, what is the breadth and length and height and depth,

    ☞R 18
    Four dimensions for what cannot be mapped: measurement language for excess.

    19To know also the charity of Christ, which surpasseth all knowledge: that you may be filled unto all the fulness of God.

    ☞R 19
    To “know” what surpasses knowledge: cognition broken open into communion.

    20Now to him who is able to do all things more abundantly than we desire or understand, according to the power that worketh in us:

    ☞R 20
    More than desire and more than understanding: prayer outruns both appetite and intellect.

    21To him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus, unto all generations, world without end. Amen.

    ☞R 21
    Glory has a location: church and Christ; time is swallowed by “all generations.”

    IVEvangeliumLuke 12:49-53

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 12:49-53 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    49I am come to cast fire on the earth. And what will I, but that it be kindled?

    ☞G 49
    Fire is not decoration; it is an arrival that demands ignition.

    50And I have a baptism wherewith I am to be baptized. And how am I straitened until it be accomplished?

    ☞G 50
    Baptism here is pressure: a narrowing until completion.

    51Think ye, that I am come to give peace on earth? I tell you, no; but separation.

    ☞G 51
    Peace refused; separation named as mission, not accident.

    52For there shall be from henceforth five in one house divided: three against two, and two against three.

    ☞G 52
    The household becomes a tribunal: numbers split, neutrality abolished.

    53The father shall be divided against the son and the son against his father: the mother against the daughter and the daughter against her mother: the mother-in-law against the daughter-in-law and the daughter- in-law against her mother-in-law.

    ☞G 53
    Kinship lines turn into fault lines; the closest bonds are tested first.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod communicates his own fullness by indwelling, and that indwelling necessarily judges and reorders human bonds. Christ’s gift is not social calm but purified communion forged through division.

    In Lectionem

    • R 16,19,20Paul’s prayer moves from empowerment to saturation: Spirit strengthens, Christ inhabits, God fills. The logic is participatory rather than forensic—divine life communicated inwardly until the creature’s capacity is expanded beyond its native limits.
    • R 15,21The address to the Father frames the whole as ecclesial ontology: created relations take their name from him, and the doxology returns glory to God precisely in the corporate body across time. The church is not an audience to salvation but its public form.

    In Evangelium

    • G 49,51,52Jesus describes his advent as crisis: not a soothing presence but a catalytic one that forces disclosure. The “fire” and “separation” are the same event viewed as purification and as division—truth entering social space and sorting allegiances.
    • G 50,53The impending baptism signals that the division is purchased, not merely announced; it is driven by an accomplished ordeal. The resulting fractures in family are not random cruelty but the predictable cost of a new primary loyalty that relativizes blood.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s kneeling is not serenity; it’s a posture of someone bargaining with a force he cannot domesticate. “Father” names the source of every lineage, which is another way of saying: no one belongs to themselves. The prayer asks for an “inward man” made strong enough to host an occupant. Christ doesn’t visit; he dwells. Faith becomes a lease signed in the dark.

    The famous dimensions—breadth, length, height, depth—read like architecture for containment, a desperate measuring of what can’t be measured. “Charity” here is not niceness; it is an invasive love that “surpasseth all knowledge,” meaning it bypasses consent, argument, and self-explanation. To be “filled unto all the fulness of God” is not comfort but saturation, the self drowned in abundance.

    Then Luke tells the truth Paul’s prayer tries to soften: this indwelling arrives as fire. Jesus wants ignition, not balance. He is “straitened,” compressed by desire for completion, and that completion is called baptism—an ordeal that burns and washes at once. Peace is refused because peace would leave the old households intact.

    Separation is the real sacrament. The gospel’s domestic inventory is clinical: father/son, mother/daughter, in-law against in-law. The point is not that families are bad; it’s that love, when it becomes absolute, makes every other bond jealous. Christ in the heart is a rival presence, and once he moves in, the house must choose whom it will shelter. Paul prays for strength because what comes is not harmony but a holy civil war.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “inner man” strengthened for a love that exceeds knowledge brushes against Sufi ma‘rifa, where the heart is widened by an intimacy that outstrips concepts; speculation: the “dimensions” read like an interior cartography akin to Ibn ‘Arabi’s imaginal vastness without being reducible to it. The plea to be “filled” evokes Vedantic fullness (purnam) and Mahayana emptiness-as-plenitude, not as metaphysics here but as a psychological saturation where the self becomes porous to a larger life.

    Luke’s fire and division resembles the purifying burn of tapas in Hindu and Buddhist asceticism, and the alchemical nigredo-to-ignis motif: transformation that first fractures the old household of the psyche. In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna’s demand cleaves loyalties; in Zen, the sword cuts through familial and social identity; speculation: the “house divided” is the ego’s domestic order breaking under a new center of gravity. The baptism-as-pressure has a Stoic and Nietzschean contour too: necessity tightening into an ordeal that must be undergone, where the cost of truth is relational rupture before any deeper concord is possible.
