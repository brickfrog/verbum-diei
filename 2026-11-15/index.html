    IIILectioProverbs 31:10-13,19-20,30-31

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Proverbs

    Proverbs 31:10-13,19-20,30-31 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    10Who shall find a valiant woman? far, and from the uttermost coasts is the price of her.

    ☞R 10
    “Find” makes virtue rare; “price” makes her a treasure, not an ornament.

    11The heart of her husband trusteth in her, and he shall have no need of spoils.

    ☞R 11
    Trust replaces plunder; the household runs without predation.

    12She will render him good, and not evil all the days of her life.

    13She hath sought wool and flax, and hath wrought by the counsel of her hands.

    19She hath put out her hand to strong things, and her fingers have taken hold of the spindle.

    ☞R 19
    Hands trained on “strong things”: domestic labor cast as force-work, not delicacy.

    20She hath opened her hand to the needy, and stretched out her hands to the poor.

    30Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: the woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised.

    ☞R 30
    Charm and beauty are time-bound currencies; fear of the Lord is the only non-counterfeit.

    31Give her of the fruit of her hands: and let her works praise her in the gates.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 25:14-30

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 25:14-30 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    14For even as a man going into a far country called his servants and delivered to them his goods;

    15And to one he gave five talents, and to another two, and to another one, to every one according to his proper ability: and immediately he took his journey.

    ☞G 15
    Distribution is unequal, but not arbitrary: “according to ability” forecloses envy as an excuse.

    16And he that had received the five talents went his way and traded with the same and gained other five.

    17And in like manner he that had received the two gained other two.

    18But he that had received the one, going his way, digged into the earth and hid his lord's money.

    ☞G 18
    He buries the gift to keep it “safe”; the earth becomes a vault and a grave.

    19But after a long time the lord of those servants came and reckoned with them.

    20And he that had received the five talents coming, brought other five talents, saying: Lord, thou didst deliver to me five talents. Behold I have gained other five over and above.

    21His lord said to him: Well done, good and faithful servant, because thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will place thee over many things. Enter thou into the joy of thy lord.

    22And he also that had received the two talents came and said: Lord, thou deliveredst two talents to me. Behold I have gained other two.

    23His lord said to him: Well done, good and faithful servant: because thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will place thee over many things. Enter thou into the joy of thy lord.

    24But he that had received the one talent, came and said: Lord, I know that thou art a hard man; thou reapest where thou hast not sown and gatherest where thou hast not strewed.

    ☞G 24
    He theologizes his laziness: a “hard man” invented to justify paralysis.

    25And being afraid, I went and hid thy talent in the earth. Behold here thou hast that which is thine.

    26And his lord answering, said to him: Wicked and slothful servant, thou knewest that I reap where I sow not and gather where I have not strewed.

    27Thou oughtest therefore to have committed my money to the bankers: and at my coming I should have received my own with usury.

    ☞G 27
    Minimum fidelity exists: if you will not risk, at least do not sterilize the capital.

    28Take ye away therefore the talent from him and give it him that hath ten talents.

    29For to every one that hath shall be given, and he shall abound: but from him that hath not, that also which he seemeth to have shall be taken away.

    30And the unprofitable servant, cast ye out into the exterior darkness. There shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

    ☞G 30
    Exterior darkness is exile from the master’s economy; refusal of work becomes self-expulsion.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod entrusts real goods and demands their fruitful use over time. Reverent fear produces durable, public works; servile fear produces concealment and condemnation.

    In Lectionem

    • R 13,20,31The portrait is public and economic: production (wool/flax), distribution (to needy), and civic recognition (“in the gates”). Wisdom is not interior sentiment but a social order made visible in outputs.
    • R 12“All the days of her life” frames virtue as duration, not a single feat; the text measures holiness by sustained reliability.

    In Evangelium

    • G 14,19,21,28,29The parable is about delegated lordship: goods remain the master’s, time passes, then audit. Reward is not wages but promotion into governance (“over many things”), while judgment is reallocation—capital flows toward proven stewardship.
    • G 25,26The servant’s defense is accepted only as self-indictment: if his premise about the master were true, it would demand greater diligence, not concealment. Fear is exposed as a strategy, not a condition.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    These readings are not praise songs; they are recruitment posters for a brutal economy of worth.

    The “valiant woman” is priced “from the uttermost coasts,” a rare commodity whose virtue is measured as output: wool, flax, spindle, strong things. Even mercy is rendered as labor, hands opening like a factory line. “Favour is deceitful, beauty is vain” sounds pious, but it is also a purge of the merely pleasing; only the productive survives the audit. Let her works praise her “in the gates,” the public square where reputations are bought and sold.

    Matthew makes the same world explicit. The master is absent, unaccountable, and admits he harvests where he did not plant. The successful servants are not morally pure; they are daring. They accept the master’s predation and mirror it, multiplying capital, entering “joy” that reads like relief after terror.

    The one-talent servant is the only honest psychologist in the room. He names the master as hard, feels fear, and refuses the game. He preserves what was given, declining to turn life into profit. For that refusal he is called wicked, not because he stole, but because he would not desire what the master desires. The parable’s dread is that safety is punished: burying the gift is treated as an attack on the system itself.

    Put together, Proverbs supplies the ideal worker and Matthew supplies the sanction. Love, trust, praise, even piety become incentives to keep hands moving. The darkness is not for sinners; it is for those who stop producing.

    Semina Verbi

    The valiant woman and the trading servants share one spine: mature agency. Worth is not a glow on the surface but a practiced capacity to convert time, skill, and attention into shelter for others. Psychologically, this is the integrated self: desire harnessed, fear metabolized, competence made generous. The “gates” and the “reckoning” are the same public mirror where inner life becomes legible.

    The buried talent reads as anxiety disguised as morality. He calls the master harsh to justify paralysis; he prefers innocence to risk, safety to growth, and so becomes sterile. The punishment is not arbitrary; it is the natural end of a life that refused circulation. What isn’t invested in reality decays into darkness.

    Semina Verbi: Aristotle’s phronesis and aretē, where virtue is skillful action proven over time. Confucian ren and li, where trustworthiness is built through patterned work and care. The Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga, action without clinging, though here the text insists results matter too. In Buddhism, right effort and the critique of sloth, with the “burying” as a cousin of torpor; speculation: the “joy of the lord” resembles the ease that follows disciplined non-avoidance. In Islam, amanah as entrusted responsibility, and barakah as increase through faithful use. In Stoicism, oikeiōsis and duty: what is given must be exercised, not hoarded. In Jewish Pirkei Avot, “If I am only for myself, what am I?”—the hands that spin are the hands that open.
