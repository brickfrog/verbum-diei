    IIILectioRevelation 4:1-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Revelation

    Revelation 4:1-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1After these things I looked, and behold a door was opened in heaven, and the first voice which I heard, as it were, of a trumpet speaking with me, said: Come up hither, and I will shew thee the things which must be done hereafter.

    ☞R 1
    Open door; not escape but summons. Trumpet-voice: command disguised as sound.

    2And immediately I was in the spirit. And behold, there was a throne set in heaven, and upon the throne one sitting.

    ☞R 2
    “In the spirit” is transport, not metaphor. The throne appears before the sitter is described.

    3And he that sat was to the sight like the jasper and the sardine stone. And there was a rainbow round about the throne, in sight like unto an emerald.

    ☞R 3
    God rendered as minerals and light; refusal of portraiture. Emerald rainbow: mercy encircling sovereignty.

    4And round about the throne were four and twenty seats: and upon the seats, four and twenty ancients sitting, clothed in white garments. And on their heads were crowns of gold.

    5And from the throne proceeded lightnings and voices and thunders. And there were seven lamps burning before the throne, which are the seven Spirits of God.

    6And in the sight of the throne was, as it were, a sea of glass like to crystal: and in the midst of the throne, and round about the throne, were four living creatures, full of eyes before and behind.

    ☞R 6
    Sea turned solid: chaos vitrified. Eyes everywhere: creation as surveillance without sleep.

    7And the first living creature was like a lion: and the second living creature like a calf: and the third living creature, having the face, as it were, of a man: and the fourth living creature was like an eagle flying.

    8And the four living creatures had each of them six wings: and round about and within they are full of eyes. And they rested not day and night, saying: Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord God Almighty, who was and who is and who is to come.

    ☞R 8
    Six wings, no rest: liturgy as labor. Trisagion pins time—was/is/to come—under one Name.

    9And when those living creatures gave glory and honour and benediction to him that sitteth on the throne, who liveth for ever and ever:

    10The four and twenty ancients fell down before him that sitteth on the throne and adored him that liveth for ever and ever and cast their crowns before the throne, saying:

    ☞R 10
    Crowns are loaned goods; worship is their return. Authority collapses into adoration.

    11Thou art worthy, O Lord our God, to receive glory and honour and power. Because thou hast created all things: and for thy will they were and have been created.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 19:11-28

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 19:11-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    11As they were hearing these things, he added and spoke a parable, because he was nigh to Jerusalem and because they thought that the kingdom of God should immediately be manifested.

    ☞G 11
    The parable is a brake on imminence. Near Jerusalem, expectations run ahead of the King.

    12He said therefore: a certain nobleman went into a far country, to receive for himself a kingdom and to return.

    13And calling his ten servants, he gave them ten pounds and said to them: Trade till I come.

    ☞G 13
    “Trade” is imperative, not suggestion. The interval is for risk, not safekeeping.

    14But his citizens hated him and they sent an embassage after him, saying: We will not have this man to reign over us.

    ☞G 14
    Hatred speaks politically: refusal of reign. Embassy: rebellion formalized.

    15And it came to pass that he returned, having received the kingdom: and he commanded his servants to be called, to whom he had given the money, that he might know how much every man had gained by trading,

    16And the first came saying: Lord, thy pound hath gained ten pounds.

    17And he said to him: Well done, thou good servant, because thou hast been faithful in a little, thou shalt have power over ten cities.

    18And the second came, saying: Lord, thy pound hath gained five pounds.

    19And he said to him: Be thou also over five cities.

    20And another came, saying: Lord, behold here is thy pound, which I have kept laid up in a napkin.

    ☞G 20
    Napkin piety: preservation masquerading as prudence. The coin is buried in cleanliness.

    21For I feared thee, because thou art an austere man: thou takest up what thou didst not lay down: and thou reapest that which thou didst not sow.

    ☞G 21
    Fear invents a tyrant to justify inertia. He accuses the master of theft to excuse his own.

    22He saith to him: Out of thy own mouth I judge thee, thou wicked servant. Thou knewest that I was an austere man, taking up what I laid not down and reaping that which I did not sow.

    ☞G 22
    Judgment uses the servant’s theology against him. False speech becomes evidence.

    23And why then didst thou not give my money into the bank, that at my coming I might have exacted it with usury?

    24And he said to them that stood by: Take the pound away from him and give it to him that hath ten pounds.

    25And they said to him: Lord, he hath ten pounds.

    26But I say to you that to every one that hath shall be given, and he shall abound: and from him that hath not, even that which he hath shall be taken from him.

    ☞G 26
    The economy is asymmetrical: increase to the fruitful, subtraction from the sterile. Neutrality is a myth.

    27But as for those my enemies, who would not have me reign over them, bring them hither and kill them before me.

    ☞G 27
    Kingship ends in blood if refused. The parable will not let “no” remain abstract.

    28And having said these things, he went before, going up to Jerusalem.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s kingship is absolute and publicly enacted: heaven worships by returning all delegated glory, and earth is judged by whether it labors for the King during his apparent absence.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4,5The scene is a court, not a landscape: elders, lamps, and thunder establish governance, witness, and sentence. The sevenfold fire before the throne signals plenitude of divine agency; the elders’ white and gold mark delegated rule that exists only by proximity to the center.
    • R 7,9,11The living creatures supply a taxonomy of animate strength—wild, domestic, rational, aerial—so that praise is voiced by the whole order of life. The acclamation culminates in creation as the ground of worth: not rescue, not utility, but sheer divine will as the reason anything exists.

    In Evangelium

    • G 12,15,16,17,23,24The narrative hinges on delayed enthronement: the nobleman becomes king elsewhere, then returns to audit. Reward is not cash but jurisdiction (“cities”): money functions as a test of fitness to govern. Even minimal competence (bank/usury) is demanded; the master requires participation in his increase, not mere non-loss.
    • G 18,19,28The graded outcomes (ten/five) show proportionality rather than uniformity; the king’s generosity scales with demonstrated fidelity. The final ascent to Jerusalem frames the parable as prelude to a contested kingship: the audit and the march belong to the same royal claim.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Revelation opens like an invitation and feels like a summons. The door in heaven is not comfort but exposure: a throne-room built of hard minerals, a spectacle of power made jewel-bright so it can be stared at without being approached. The sea is glass, not water; nothing living can swim there. Everything is eyes. Nothing is allowed to look away.

    The elders’ white clothes and gold crowns read less like reward than like uniform. Their one act is surrender: they throw their crowns down on cue, rehearsing the liturgy of consent. Even praise becomes a mechanism, a perpetual chant that keeps the throne fed. Creation is framed as “for thy will,” which is the most chilling line here: existence justified not by love but by appetite.

    Luke’s parable pulls the same mask off the sacred. The nobleman goes away to get a kingdom, which means legitimacy is acquired elsewhere, by force or favor, and then imported home. The subjects’ hatred is not irrational rebellion; it is political recognition. He will reign, and he will take.

    The servants are not invited into joy; they are absorbed into administration. Profit becomes proof of devotion, and devotion is measured in cities to control. The one who hides the coin in a napkin is the only one who refuses to eroticize risk, the only one whose fear is accurate: the master is austere, extracting what he did not plant. The punishment is not for laziness but for refusing complicity.

    Then the ending says the quiet part aloud: bring the enemies and kill them in front of me. This is not a detour from the “kingdom”; it is the kingdom seen without incense. Revelation’s endless worship and Luke’s public execution belong to the same psychology: a regime that demands admiration and calls it holiness, that turns dread into praise, and desire for safety into collaboration.

    Semina Verbi

    Revelation’s throne-room reads like the psyche’s organizing center made visible: an imaginal mandala where attention is seized, scattered fear is gathered, and the self is re-ordered around an overwhelming axis. The jeweled radiance, the encircling rainbow, the crystalline sea, the tireless chorus, the many-eyed creatures: these are not “decorations” but the mind’s way of admitting that reality exceeds it, so it builds symbols of totality and vigilance to keep from dissolving. Speculation: this resembles the Hindu darśan of Viṣṇu’s cosmic form, the Buddhist jeweled net of Indra, and the Daoist celestial bureaucracy; in each, the point is not information but re-centering before what cannot be domesticated.

    Luke’s parable is the same psychic pressure in ethical form: delay exposes character. The nobleman’s absence is the long stretch where no final meaning arrives to rescue you; you must act anyway. The fearful servant wraps possibility in a napkin—an image of sterile self-protection—then projects his own inner harshness onto the master. He is condemned by his own story because his narrative is his cage. Speculation: this parallels the Bhagavad Gītā’s insistence on action without clinging, the Stoic demand to use what is in your power, and the rabbinic warning that burying a talent is a sin against the Giver; all treat unused capacity as a form of refusal.

    The violence at the end functions psychologically as the cost of enthronement: when a new center takes the throne, rival loyalties die. Speculation: like the purgative ferocity of Kali, the Zen sword that cuts delusion, or the Qur’anic image of the heart’s idols being shattered, it names the inner civil war required for a kingdom—any kingdom—to become real.
