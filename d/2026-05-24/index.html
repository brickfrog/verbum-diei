    IIILectioActs 2:1-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 2:1-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And when the days of the Pentecost were accomplished, they were all together in one place:

    ☞R 1
    Pentecost: a calendar ripens into an event; unity is staged before power descends.

    2And suddenly there came a sound from heaven, as of a mighty wind coming: and it filled the whole house where they were sitting.

    ☞R 2
    Heaven’s sound, not heaven’s sight; the invasion is auditory before it is visible.

    3And there appeared to them parted tongues, as it were of fire: and it sat upon every one of them.

    ☞R 3
    Fire divides without diminishing; one flame, many seats.

    4And they were all filled with the Holy Ghost: and they began to speak with divers tongues, according as the Holy Ghost gave them to speak.

    ☞R 4
    Speech is the first miracle: the Spirit chooses grammar, not spectacle.

    5Now there were dwelling at Jerusalem, Jews, devout men, out of every nation under heaven.

    6And when this was noised abroad, the multitude came together, and were confounded in mind, because that every man heard them speak in his own tongue.

    ☞R 6
    The crowd’s confusion is not noise but comprehension—each hears too clearly.

    7And they were all amazed, and wondered, saying: Behold, are not all these that speak Galilean?

    ☞R 7
    Galilee becomes the scandal: the message arrives from the wrong province.

    8And how have we heard, every man our own tongue wherein we were born?

    9Parthians and Medes and Elamites and inhabitants of Mesopotamia, Judea, and Cappadocia, Pontus and Asia,

    10Phrygia and Pamphylia, Egypt and the parts of Libya about Cyrene, and strangers of Rome,

    11Jews also, and proselytes, Cretes, and Arabians: we have heard them speak in our own tongues the wonderful works of God.

    ☞R 11
    The content is doxology—“works of God”—not self-advertisement.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 20:19-23

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 20:19-23 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    19Now when it was late the same day, the first of the week, and the doors were shut, where the disciples were gathered together, for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood in the midst and said to them: Peace be to you. The doors were shut... The same power which could bring Christ's whole body, entire in all its dimensions, through the doors, can without the least question make the same body really present in the sacrament; though both the one and the other be above our comprehension.

    ☞G 19
    Locked doors and a standing Christ: fear is spatial; presence ignores architecture.

    20And when he had said this, he shewed them his hands and his side. The disciples therefore were glad, when they saw the Lord.

    21He said therefore to them again: Peace be to you. As the Father hath sent me, I also send you.

    ☞G 21
    Peace precedes mission; sending without pacification would be mere dispersal.

    22When he had said this, he breathed on them; and he said to them: Receive ye the Holy Ghost.

    ☞G 22
    Breath, not thunder: creation’s gesture reused for a new humanity.

    23Whose sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven them: and whose sins you shall retain, they are retained. Whose sins, etc... See here the commission, stamped by the broad seal of heaven, by virtue of which the pastors of Christ's church absolve repenting sinners upon their confession.

    ☞G 23
    Forgive/retain: authority that can bind as well as loose; mercy with teeth.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe Holy Spirit constitutes the Church as a public, universal witness and as an authorized tribunal of reconciliation. Mission and absolution proceed from the risen Christ’s gift, not from human initiative.

    In Lectionem

    • R 5,8,9,10Luke frames Pentecost as a reversal of dispersion: the nations are listed not to exoticize but to show Jerusalem as a temporary world-center where diaspora and gentile proximity converge. The miracle does not erase difference; it traverses it, making the Church’s universality linguistic and public rather than imperial or coercive.

    In Evangelium

    • G 20,22,23John compresses Paschal ecclesiology into a sequence: recognition of the wounded body grounds the community; the Spirit is given as equipment for a specific juridical act; reconciliation is not an idea but an enacted judgment within history. The risen Jesus constitutes an authorized ministry, not merely an inspired audience.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Pentecost is not comfort; it is exposure. The room is “one place,” a sealed container of fear, and the wind is an invasion that turns private dread into public noise. Fire doesn’t warm them; it brands them. “Divers tongues” reads less like unity than like the psyche splitting under pressure, a forced extroversion where the self can no longer keep one voice. The crowd is “confounded,” not converted. Understanding arrives as disturbance: everyone hears intimacy in their own mother-language, as if the strangers have crawled inside their earliest memories and spoken from there.

    John’s locked room is the same architecture of panic. Peace is not a feeling; it is a command issued by a body that ignores boundaries. He shows wounds like credentials, proof that authority is purchased by damage. Then the breath: not gentle inspiration but a transfer of power that makes them dangerous. “Forgive” and “retain” is the real miracle—control over guilt, permission to reopen or keep shut the inner doors of other people. The Spirit here is not a dove; it is an instrument that turns frightened men into gatekeepers of shame and relief, and that is why the scene trembles with desire and dread at once.

    Semina Verbi

    Wind and flame as psyche’s weather: the self is seized by a force that is not “me,” then reorganized into speech. That looks like the Vedic Vayu and Agni as breath and fire that animate and clarify; like the Stoic pneuma as the warm current that makes reason audible; like Daoist qi moving through a sealed room without opening the door.

    Many tongues as a healing of social fragmentation: not one empire-language, but mutual intelligibility without erasure. That resembles the Buddhist ideal of skillful means, where one truth takes the accent of the hearer; and the rabbinic image (speculation) of Torah spoken in seventy languages, not to dilute it but to make it inhabitable.

    The closed doors and sudden presence stage trauma and safety: fear encloses, then “peace” enters without asking permission, as if the psyche learns a new boundary—porous to grace, firm against panic. Zen’s kenshō sometimes arrives like that, uninvited, through the locked habits of mind (speculation).

    Breath as transmission: not information but life passed mouth-to-mouth, like prāṇa in Yoga, ruach in Hebrew imagination, and the Sufi nafkh, the divine inbreathing that turns clay into a speaking person.

    The power to loose and bind reads as communal psychotherapy: naming guilt, releasing it, or holding it when denial would cheapen repair. That parallels Yom Kippur’s social-ethical accounting, and the Confucian stress on rectifying names so relationships can be made true again (speculation).
