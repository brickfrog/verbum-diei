    IIILectioGalatians 5:1-6

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Galatians

    Galatians 5:1-6 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Stand fast and be not held again under the yoke of bondage.

    ☞R 1
    Freedom is framed as posture: stand, do not stoop back into harness.

    2Behold, I Paul tell you, that if you be circumcised, Christ shall profit you nothing.

    ☞R 2
    A surgical sign becomes a total loss: one cut, and “profit” goes to zero.

    3And I testify again to every man circumcising himself that he is a debtor to do the whole law.

    ☞R 3
    The law is not sampled; it is a whole debt instrument.

    4You are made void of Christ, you who are justified in the law: you are fallen from grace.

    ☞R 4
    To seek justification in law is to evacuate Christ—grace is not a supplement.

    5For we in spirit, by faith, wait for the hope of justice.

    6For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision availeth any thing nor uncircumcision: but faith that worketh by Charity.

    ☞R 6
    The only currency named is operative faith; charity is its motion, not its ornament.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 11:37-41

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 11:37-41 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    37And as he was speaking, a certain Pharisee prayed him that he would dine with him. And he going in, sat down to eat.

    38And the Pharisee began to say, thinking within himself, why he was not washed before dinner.

    ☞G 38
    The accusation is silent; the judgment happens “within himself.”

    39And the Lord said to him: Now you, Pharisees, make clean the outside of the cup and of the platter: but your inside is full of rapine and iniquity.

    ☞G 39
    Outside polished, inside predatory: cleanliness as camouflage for theft.

    40Ye fools, did not he that made that which is without make also that which is within?

    ☞G 40
    Creation logic as indictment: the Maker’s jurisdiction includes the hidden.

    41But yet that which remaineth, give alms: and behold, all things are clean unto you.

    ☞G 41
    Alms are prescribed as remainder—what is left after self is dethroned becomes cleansing.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisJustification cannot be partitioned between Christ and law; to add a legal ground is to forfeit grace. God’s cleansing targets the interior and manifests outwardly as merciful action, not ritual display.

    In Lectionem

    • R 5Paul relocates eschatology: the community “waits” not for legal vindication but for a promised justice received in the Spirit. The argument is temporal as much as juridical—law-keeping tries to seize a verdict now; faith consents to God’s timing and mode of acquittal.

    In Evangelium

    • G 37,39,41The meal scene is a trap that becomes a courtroom. Jesus accepts the table, then reverses the host’s evaluative gaze: the Pharisee’s scrutiny of ritual becomes exposure of moral economy, and the remedy is not more water but a redistribution of goods that breaks the interior logic of rapine.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “freedom” is not comfort. It is the terror of being cut loose from the old machinery of innocence. Circumcision is not merely a ritual mark; it is the fantasy that the body can be edited into safety, that a small, sanctioned wound can purchase a life without dread. Paul calls it bondage because it turns desire into bookkeeping: do this, owe that, pay forever. The law becomes a creditor living inside you, and Christ “profits nothing” because you have chosen a system where profit is the only meaning.

    Luke stages the same sickness at a dinner table. The Pharisee’s shock at unwashed hands is not hygiene; it is the wish to keep violence respectable. Jesus names what the polish hides: rapine and iniquity, hunger dressed as manners. The cup is clean because it wants to be seen clean. The inside is dirty because it wants what it wants.

    Then the hard, heterodox turn: “Give alms” is not a pious add-on; it is a psychological hack against the self’s obsession with purity. Stop scrubbing the surface and bleed outward. Let your surplus leave you. Charity is not niceness but a controlled loss, a chosen diminishment that breaks the law’s addiction to control. Faith “working by charity” is faith that accepts contamination, refuses the fantasy of a sealed self, and finds cleanness not by washing but by surrender.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s freedom is not permission but a psychological exit from compulsive self-justification. Circumcision becomes the emblem of an anxious bargain with reality: if I perform the mark, I can stop fearing contingency. The trap is totalization; once you anchor worth in a rule, the rule metastasizes into a whole identity that must be defended, policed, and endlessly paid for. “Faith working through charity” reads as integration: trust that releases the self from scorekeeping, and love as the outward sign that the inner economy has changed.

    Luke stages the same conflict as hygiene theater. The Pharisee’s astonishment is the reflex of a mind trained to read virtue off surfaces. Jesus targets the split self: polished exterior, predatory interior. The shock is that purity is not achieved by scrubbing but by generosity; almsgiving is not a ritual add-on but a reorientation of desire away from grasping. Cleanliness becomes the byproduct of relinquishment.

    Semina Verbi: Stoicism’s distinction between externals and the ruling faculty parallels the move from washing to inner disposition, though this is speculation when mapped onto almsgiving. In Buddhism, sila without prajna can harden into self-making; the critique of clinging to forms echoes the warning that a mark can void the point. In Hindu bhakti, devotion expressed as seva resembles “faith working through love,” where action is not merit-hoarding but surrender. In Sufism, riya (performative piety) matches the polished cup, while zakat and futuwwa gesture toward cleansing by giving.
