Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 9:1,9:17,10:1The text stages election as a collision between pedigree (1), prophetic designation (5), and ritual installation (8). Benjamin supplies a plausible candidate; the Lord supplies the decisive identification; the anointing supplies the juridical transfer of authority. Kingship is mediated—God governs through sign, prophet, and rite—so the monarchy is never autonomous; it is a delegated administration over “inheritance,” not ownership.
On the Gospel
  • G 13,14,17Mark compresses discipleship into movement: Jesus teaches in public (1), then creates a follower by direct summons (2), then justifies his social strategy by redefining his mission as clinical intervention (5). The call is not moral vetting but reorientation: he forms a community whose boundary is his presence, not prior purity, and he asserts authority to define who counts as a proper object of divine attention.

DoctrinalGod establishes rulers and disciples by authoritative call and mediated sign, not by social standing or moral credential. Christ’s kingdom advances by reclaiming the disordered through communion under his command.

Heterodox Reading
Saul enters history the way a symptom enters a life: by accident, by loss, by a petty humiliation. He goes out to find donkeys and keeps not finding them, a pilgrimage of absence that trains him for rule, because kingship here is not a prize but a compensation. He is “choice and goodly,” a body made to be looked at, and that is precisely the danger: Israel wants a figure tall enough to quiet its panic. Samuel’s line, “I will…tell thee all that is in thy heart,” is not comfort; it is exposure. The oil is a seal and a trap. The kiss is intimate, coercive. Saul is drafted into being the answer to everyone else’s dread, before he even knows what he wants. Jesus does something that looks like mercy but feels like invasion. Levi is not persuaded, not improved, not argued with; he is claimed. The call is a clean cut, and then the feast is a scandal not because the guests are immoral but because the scene refuses the old bargain: purity as distance. “Physician” is not a compliment to the sick; it is a diagnosis that keeps them sick enough to need him. He gathers the compromised and makes their compromise the very doorway into belonging. Put together, the readings sketch a dark desire: to be chosen, even if choosing costs you yourself. Saul is anointed because he fits the people’s fantasy; Levi is summoned because he fits the role of the wounded. Both are pulled from ordinary shame into public meaning. And in both, the divine touch doesn’t soothe; it rearranges. To be found is to be taken.
Semina Verbi
Saul is found by being lost: the trivial errand turns into a summons, as if vocation rides on misplacement. That pivot has the feel of the Daoist unplanned turning, where usefulness arrives through wandering, and of the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence that one’s work, even when mundane, can be the corridor to destiny; speculation, but the psychology matches. The oil and kiss externalize a new self-image: an identity conferred from without that reorganizes the inner life. Levi is called at the point of compromise, not after reform. The meal makes belonging precede purity, like the Buddha eating with Angulimala after the terror, or the Sufi trope of the tavern where the broken are gathered; speculation, yet the pattern is recognizably therapeutic. “Physician” frames sin as sickness: not excused, but treated, shifting shame into cure and turning community from tribunal into clinic.
