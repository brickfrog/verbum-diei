12(And he saw a man named Ananias coming in and putting his hands upon him, that he might receive his sight.)

13But Ananias answered: Lord, I have heard by many of this man, how much evil he hath done to thy saints in Jerusalem.

14And here he hath authority from the chief priests to bind all that invoke thy name.

15And the Lord said to him: Go thy way: for this man is to me a vessel of election, to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel.

16For I will shew him how great things he must suffer for my name's sake.

17And Ananias went his way and entered into the house. And laying his hands upon him, he said: Brother Saul, the Lord Jesus hath sent me, he that appeared to thee in the way as thou camest, that thou mayest receive thy sight and be filled with the Holy Ghost.

18And immediately there fell from his eyes as it were scales: and he received his sight. And rising up, he was baptized.

19And when he had taken meat, he was strengthened. And he was with the disciples that were at Damascus, for some days.

20And immediately he preached Jesus in the synagogues, that he is the son of God.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 3,6,7The narrative moves from authorized coercion to commanded obedience. Saul’s initiative collapses into reception: he is halted, interrogated, then redirected into a mediated instruction—God chooses to convert the violent through a voice and to restore him through the Church.
  • R 10,11,13,14Ananias supplies the communal obstacle: memory, reputation, institutional warrants. The resolution is not reassurance but commission; the community’s fear is overridden by a divine claim on the offender, forcing ecclesial incorporation to run ahead of moral comfort.
  • R 19,20The endpoint is public speech in the very venues sought for arrests. The persecutor’s social capital is inverted into witness; the same synagogue network becomes the first stage of proclamation.
On the Gospel
  • G 52,55,57,59The discourse binds eternity to a specific mode of participation: life is not merely promised, it is ingested. The argument escalates from gift (flesh) to effect (resurrection) to union (abiding), then seals itself by contrasting manna’s mortality with a food that carries the living source.
  • G 53,56Objection targets plausibility; Christ answers with insistence on realism (‘indeed’) rather than explanation. The text prefers offense to dilution, making the hearer choose between literal scandal and literal dependence.

Doctrinal Christ identifies himself with his persecuted and converts by incorporation: into his Body through the Church and into his life through his flesh and blood. Election is ordered to mission and sealed by suffering, sustained by a real communion that grants resurrection.

Heterodox Reading
Saul isn’t converted; he’s captured. The light is an ambush that strips him of the one thing his violence depended on: the clean distance between “me” and “them.” “Why persecutest thou me?” is a psychological trap, forcing the persecutor to feel his target as his own body. His blindness is not punishment but the first honest state he’s had: the collapse of certainty into bare need. Three days without food reads like withdrawal, the body resetting after an addiction to righteousness. Ananias is the real moral center, because he feels the dread that the story wants to hurry past. He knows reputations, dossiers, the way men like Saul don’t change; he resists not out of stubbornness but out of self-preservation. Calling him “Brother” is not sweetness; it’s a high-risk act of imagination, the kind that can get you killed. The “vessel of election” line lands like cold bureaucracy: this instrument will be used, and the price is suffering. Saul is not spared; he’s repurposed. John’s bread speech is the same coercion in another register. It’s not a gentle invitation to believe; it’s a demand to ingest what horrifies you. Eat flesh, drink blood, or you have no life. The crowd’s disgust is the honest response to intimacy pushed past comfort into taboo. Eternal life is promised through an act that feels like violence turned inward: you don’t admire the sacred from a safe pew, you swallow it, let it enter the gut, let it undo your boundaries. The dread is the point. Desire is trained by being made to cross a line you can’t uncross. Taken together, the texts say: the divine doesn’t persuade, it invades. It takes the persecutor’s eyes, then feeds him a body. It creates disciples by dismantling autonomy and replacing it with a dependence so total it feels like possession. The miracle is not that Saul starts preaching; the miracle is that anyone, like Ananias, still dares to touch the man who once came to bind them.
Semina Verbi
Saul’s collapse under light reads like the psyche’s forced reversal: the aggressor meets the wounded Other and discovers the “enemy” lives inside his own moral body. That voice—addressing him by name, identifying with his victims—resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s moment when Arjuna’s violence is arrested by a revelation that turns duty into surrender, and it echoes Sufi accounts of qabd and bast, contraction then expansion, where certainty breaks the self so a larger allegiance can form. Speculation: the three-day blindness has the shape of an initiatory limen found in shamanic illness and in Tibetan “bardo” imagery, not as doctrine but as a psychological grammar for dying to an old identity. The bread-and-flesh insistence presses past metaphor into a demand for incorporation: life is not admired, it is eaten. That is close to the Vedic soma and the Dionysian sparagmos in Greek mystery religion, where a god is taken into the body so the body learns the god’s pulse; it also parallels the Jewish idea of internalizing Torah—Ezekiel’s scroll, later rabbinic language of words as food—except here the nourishment is not text but person. Speculation: Zen’s “eat when hungry” cuts the opposite way—no sacrament, only immediacy—yet it shares the same claim that reality must be taken in, not merely contemplated. Ananias calling him “brother” looks like restorative justice before it is a program: the community risks touch, and touch rehumanizes. That resembles the African ethic of ubuntu and the Buddhist practice of metta toward an enemy, not as sentiment but as a reweaving of the social self. The “scales” falling suggests the Gnostic motif of ignorance as a film over the eye, but here the cure is communal mediation, not secret knowledge.
