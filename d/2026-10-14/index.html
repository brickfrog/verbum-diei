    IIILectioGalatians 5:18-25

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Galatians

    Galatians 5:18-25 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    18But if you are led by the spirit, you are not under the law.

    ☞R 18
    “Led” displaces “ruled”: guidance replaces coercion; law loses jurisdiction.

    19Now the works of the flesh are manifest: which are fornication, uncleanness, immodesty, luxury,

    ☞R 19
    The flesh is not subtle: a catalog, public, enumerable; sin as inventory.

    20Idolatry, witchcrafts, enmities, contentions, emulations, wraths, quarrels, dissensions, sects,

    ☞R 20
    The flesh is not subtle: a catalog, public, enumerable; sin as inventory.

    21Envies, murders, drunkenness, revellings, and such like. Of the which I foretell you, as I have foretold to you, that they who do such things shall not obtain the kingdom of God.

    ☞R 21
    “Such like” widens the net; the warning is not about edge-cases but trajectory.

    22But the fruit of the Spirit is, charity, joy, peace, patience, benignity, goodness, longanimity,

    23Mildness, faith, modesty, continency, chastity. Against such there is no law.

    ☞R 23
    No law against fruit: virtue is not merely permitted; it is unprosecutable.

    24And they that are Christ's have crucified their flesh, with the vices and concupiscences.

    ☞R 24
    Crucifixion is not pruning; the self is executed with its appetites.

    25If we live in the Spirit, let us also walk in the Spirit.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 11:42-46

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 11:42-46 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    42But woe to you, Pharisees, because you tithe mint and rue and every herb and pass over judgment and the charity of God. Now these things you ought to have done, and not to leave the other undone.

    ☞G 42
    Herbs weighed; justice neglected. Precision becomes a decoy for omission.

    43Woe to you, Pharisees, because you love the uppermost seats in the synagogues and salutations in the marketplace.

    ☞G 43
    Piety as seating chart: honor sought where God is named.

    44Woe to you, because you are as sepulchres that appear not: and men that walk over are not aware.

    ☞G 44
    Invisible tombs: death disguised as normality; contamination without warning.

    45And one of the lawyers answering, saith to him: Master, in saying these things, thou reproachest us also.

    46But he said: Woe to you lawyers also, because you load men with burdens which they cannot bear and you yourselves touch not the packs with one of your fingers.

    ☞G 46
    Burden without touch: authority that commands but will not share weight.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe Spirit produces a life the law cannot indict, while legalistic religion produces burdens and hidden death. True righteousness is measured by judgment and the charity of God, not by meticulous display.

    In Lectionem

    • R 22,25Paul’s contrast is not law versus chaos but two economies of production: “works” that can be manufactured and displayed versus “fruit” that grows from an indwelling source. The list of vices culminates in exclusion from the kingdom, while the list of virtues culminates in a life-pattern (“walk”)—a sustained gait, not episodic compliance.

    In Evangelium

    • G 42,45,46The three woes move from distorted priorities (tithing without judgment) to distorted desire (status) to distorted ontology (hidden graves), then widen to the legal experts who systematize the distortion. The interruption in v.4 exposes the real concern: not truth, but being implicated; Jesus answers by naming the mechanism—religion turned into unlivable load administered at a distance.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “Spirit” is not serenity; it’s a jailbreak. “Not under the law” is the dangerous line, because it admits what everyone knows and no one says: law is a machine for managing desire, not curing it. So he draws up his lurid catalog of “flesh” like a prosecutor who can’t stop enjoying the evidence. The list is not just sex and drink; it’s social fever—enmities, factions, the sweet intoxication of being right. That’s the flesh: the self swollen into a crowd, craving mirrors and enemies.

    Then he offers “fruit,” and it reads like a new etiquette for a different body. Patience, mildness, continency: not haloed virtues, but survival skills for a person who has stopped feeding the inner riot. “Crucified their flesh” isn’t piety; it’s psychic violence turned inward, the cutting-off of the old pleasures of grievance and display. He knows it hurts. He calls it life.

    Jesus, meanwhile, attacks the same addiction from the other side: the addiction to moral accounting. The Pharisee tithes herbs because it feels like control—tiny, measurable righteousness, a daily fix. Judgment and love are messier; they can’t be counted, and they expose you. So the law becomes theater: best seats, public greetings, the body angled toward applause.

    “Unmarked graves” is the cruelest image: holiness as contamination. People step on you and don’t know they’re being infected by your fear. The lawyers are worse, because they turn dread into policy, loading burdens they never shoulder. This is the real “flesh” dressed as “law”: a way to keep desire intact by punishing it in others. Spirit, in both readings, is not niceness. It is the refusal to use rules as a substitute for love, and the terror of losing the identity built on rules.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “led by the spirit” reads like an interior jurisdiction replacing an exterior one: the self governed from within rather than managed by compliance. That split between “works” and “fruit” echoes Aristotle’s virtue as a stable disposition that ripens through habituation, not a checklist of acts; and it brushes close, speculatively, to Stoic oikeiosis, where the good life is an alignment with a rational nature that makes certain actions feel alien. The crucifixion image is psychological violence turned inward: not self-hatred, but the deliberate breaking of compulsive loops, a hard re-training of desire.

    Luke’s critique targets performative piety and prestige economy: the sacred used as social capital. That resembles the Buddha’s warnings against sila as mere display without prajna and karuna, and Confucius’s suspicion of li when it becomes empty ceremony without ren. The “unmarked graves” line has the bite of Jung’s shadow: corruption that is unconscious is most contagious. The lawyers’ burdens evoke Kafka’s bureaucratic torment, but also, more charitably, the Hindu insight (speculative) that dharma without compassion becomes a crushing mechanism; rule-keeping can be a way to avoid love, and love is the one thing no rule can substitute for.
