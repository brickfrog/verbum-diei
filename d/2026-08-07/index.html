    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Nahum

    Nahum 2:1,3; 3:1-3,6-7 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    2:1He is come up that shall destroy before thy face, that shall keep the siege: watch the way, fortify thy loins, strengthen thy power exceedingly.

    ☞R 2:1
    Imperatives to the doomed: vigilance as mockery; strength demanded at the moment it becomes useless.

    2:3The shield of his mighty men is like fire, the men of the army are clad in scarlet, the reins of the chariot are flaming in the day of his preparation, and the drivers are stupefied. Of his mighty men, etc... He speaks of the Chaldeans and Medes sent to destroy Ninive.-Ibid. Stupefied... consopiti. That is, they drive on furiously like men intoxicated with wine.

    3:1Woe to thee, O city of blood, all full of lies and violence: rapine shall not depart from thee.

    ☞R 3:1
    “City of blood”: identity reduced to its output; violence as civic liturgy.

    3:2The noise of the whip, and the noise of the rattling of the wheels, and of the neighing horse; and of the running chariot, and of the horsemen coming up,

    3:3And of the shining sword, and of the glittering spear, and of a multitude slain, and of a grievous destruction: and there is no end of carcasses, and they shall fall down on their dead bodies.

    3:6And I will cast abominations upon thee, and will disgrace thee, and will make an example of thee.

    ☞R 3:6
    Judgment as exposure: filth thrown not to stain but to unmask; disgrace weaponized.

    3:7And it shall come to pass that every one that shall see thee, shall flee from thee, and shall say: Ninive is laid waste: who shall bemoan thee? whence shall I seek a comforter for thee?

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 16:24-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    24Then Jesus said to his disciples: If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me.

    ☞G 24
    Discipleship begins with subtraction: self denied before any act is added.

    25For he that will save his life, shall lose it: and he that shall lose his life for my sake, shall find it.

    ☞G 25
    The calculus inverts: preservation destroys; surrender secures.

    26For what doth it profit a man, if he gain the whole world and suffer the loss of his own soul? Or what exchange shall a man give for his soul?

    ☞G 26
    The soul has no market price; “exchange” is the language of idolatry.

    27For the Son of man shall come in the glory of his Father with his angels: and then will he render to every man according to his works.

    28Amen I say to you, there are some of them that stand here, that shall not taste death, till they see the Son of man coming in his kingdom.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s judgment strips violent powers of honor and allies, and Christ’s call requires self-renunciation because final recompense is real and imminent.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2:3,3:2,3:3The passage stages a forensic spectacle: the conqueror’s apparatus (color, fire, sound) becomes the city’s indictment, and the sensory excess culminates in a landscape where bodies are mere debris. The point is not military reportage but the public demonstration that predatory power ends in anonymity and waste.
    • R 3:7The final taunt—no mourner, no comforter—marks the social death of an empire: when terror has been its only bond, collapse produces not pity but flight. Divine judgment here is also the withdrawal of human solidarity.

    In Evangelium

    • G 27,28The command to carry the cross is grounded in eschatological adjudication: works will be rendered, not intentions admired. The closing saying compresses horizon—kingdom is not only future but breaks into the present as a crisis of allegiance that reorders life before death arrives.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Nahum is not moral instruction; it is the eroticism of catastrophe. The text lingers on color and motion—scarlet, flaming reins, stupefied drivers—because violence is not only punishment, it is spectacle. Nineveh is “blood” and “lies,” yes, but the real horror is that the prophet can’t look away. The city is made into pornography of ruin: whip-crack, wheel-rattle, glittering spear, bodies without end. Then the final humiliation: not grief but abandonment. “Who shall bemoan thee?” means the worst sentence is to be un-mourned, stripped of even tragic dignity.

    Matthew answers with a colder violence turned inward. “Deny yourself” is not self-improvement; it is consent to dispossession, a controlled self-demolition. The cross is the instrument by which empire makes an example; Jesus asks the disciple to carry the sign of their own public erasure. The bargain is brutal: try to save your life and you lose it; lose it and you find it. This is desire trained through dread, a deliberate exchange of the world’s spectacle for an invisible kingdom that arrives like a threat—some will see it before they die. Between Nineveh’s disgrace and the disciple’s cross, the common thread is this: the self that wants to be seen, secured, mourned must be broken, because history only grants visibility by turning you into an example.

    Semina Verbi

    Nahum’s ruin-song for Nineveh has the hard clarity of Greek tragedy: the city becomes a character whose vice ripens into fate, like hubris drawing down nemesis. The relish in sound—whip, wheels, steel—feels Homeric, war as intoxicant and as verdict, and the final line, “who will mourn,” matches the Iliad’s cold truth that even greatness can become unmournable when it has fed on others.

    The Gospel’s self-denial and cross-bearing reads like a psychological inversion of imperial appetite: the ego’s survival strategy is exposed as its trap, and the only way out is voluntary loss. That turn has close kin in Buddhism’s anatta and dukkha—release by relinquishing the clinging self—and in Stoic askesis, where freedom is trained through consenting to hardship rather than bargaining with it. Speculation: the “some standing here” who will see the kingdom echoes apocalyptic urgency found in Zoroastrian and Second Temple Jewish imagination, but here it is internalized into a present-tense revaluation of what counts as profit, exchange, and life.
