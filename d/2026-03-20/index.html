Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Wisdom

Wisdom 2:1,12-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1For they have said, reasoning with themselves, but not right: The time of our life is short and tedious, and in the end of a man there is no remedy, and no man hath been known to have returned from hell:

☞R 1
Bad metaphysics births bad ethics: no return, therefore no restraint.

12Let us, therefore, lie in wait for the just, because he is not for our turn, and he is contrary to our doings, and upbraideth us with transgressions of the law, and divulgeth against us the sins of our way of life.

☞R 12
The just is a rebuke by mere existence; contradiction felt as insult.

13He boasteth that he hath the knowledge of God, and calleth himself the son of God.

☞R 13
“Son of God” is not argued against—only punished.

14He is become a censurer of our thoughts.

15He is grievous unto us, even to behold: for his life is not like other men's, and his ways are very different.

16We are esteemed by him as triflers, and he abstaineth from our ways as from filthiness, and he preferreth the latter end of the just, and glorieth that he hath God for his father.

☞R 16
Purity reads as contempt; filthiness is named by the abstainer.

17Let us see then if his words be true, and let us prove what shall happen to him, and we shall know what his end shall be.

☞R 17
They draft a test that forces God to perform on schedule.

18For if he be the true son of God, he will defend him, and will deliver him from the hands of his enemies.

☞R 19

20Let us condemn him to a most shameful death: for there shall be respect had unto him by his words.

IVEvangeliumJohn 7:1-2,10,25-30

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to John

John 7:1-2,10,25-30 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1After these things, Jesus walked in Galilee: for he would not walk in Judea, because the Jews sought to kill him.

☞G 1
Murder sets the itinerary; prudence under threat is not cowardice.

2Now the Jews feast of tabernacles was at hand.

10But after his brethren were gone up, then he also went up to the feast, not openly, but, as it were, in secret.

☞G 10
Hidden ascent: secrecy as strategy, not denial.

25Some therefore of Jerusalem said: Is not this he whom they seek to kill?

26And behold, he speaketh openly: and they say nothing to him. Have the rulers known for a truth that this is the Christ?

☞G 26
Power’s silence becomes evidence in the crowd’s courtroom.

27But we know this man, whence he is: but when the Christ cometh, no man knoweth, whence he is.

☞G 27
They weaponize a folklore criterion: Messiah must be untraceable.

28Jesus therefore cried out in the temple, teaching and saying: You both know me, and you know whence I am. And I am not come of myself: but he that sent me is true, whom you know not.

☞G 28
Known origin / unknown Sender: the scandal relocates from geography to God.

29I know him, because I am from him: and he hath sent me.

☞G 29
Known origin / unknown Sender: the scandal relocates from geography to God.

30They sought therefore to apprehend him: and no man laid hands on him, because his hour was not yet come.

☞G 30
Arrest fails on a clock: violence waits for permission it cannot see.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisThe righteous are hated because they reveal God, and the wicked attempt to turn providence into a manipulable test. Christ’s mission proceeds under threat, yet no violence can act beyond the Father’s appointed hour.

In Lectionem

  • R 14,15The text makes the persecutors confess the real provocation: the just man exposes interior life. Hatred begins as surveillance of thought, then seeks a body to break.
  • R 17,20The proposed “proof” is a theology of coercion: if God is real, he must validate the righteous by immediate rescue. When providence refuses their timetable, they call it falsification and proceed to sentence.
  • R 22Ignorance here is eschatological: loss of hope collapses moral imagination. Without the horizon of recompense, holiness becomes irrational and therefore intolerable.

In Evangelium

  • G 2,25,27Tabernacles frames the dispute as one about presence: God “dwelling” with his people versus a Messiah reduced to an identification puzzle. The crowd’s categories cannot hold a presence that is both visible and sent.
  • G 26,28Public teaching under threat exposes a split between official authority and popular speculation; the real issue is not whether he can be labeled, but whether God can be encountered outside their control.
  • G 30The narrative asserts an ordering principle over political force: history is not ruled by the strongest hands but by a fixed “hour.” Human plots operate, but they do not set the final conditions.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Wisdom admits what piety prefers to hide: the “just” is intolerable not because he is cruel, but because he is clean. His difference is an accusation you can’t answer, only silence with force. The mob’s philosophy is thin but honest—life is short, nothing comes back, so crush the witness and reclaim the room. Torture becomes a laboratory: if he’s real, let reality save him. It’s less hatred of God than hatred of the shame his existence awakens.

John stages the same sickness as political theater. Jesus doesn’t stride into danger; he slips in, testing the temperature of the crowd, letting desire and dread ferment. They want a Messiah who arrives like a rumor, originless, so no one has to revise their story. But he stands there with a known hometown and an unknown source, and that split—familiar body, alien authority—drives them mad. “His hour was not yet come” isn’t cosmic scheduling; it’s the crowd not yet unanimous. They can’t arrest him until they can agree on what, exactly, they are trying to kill: a man they recognize, or the judgment he carries into the open.

Semina Verbi

The “lie in wait for the just” mechanism reads like Girard’s scapegoat logic: a community stabilizes itself by hunting the one whose difference exposes its disorder. The irritant is not his argument but his mere existence as an accusing mirror, the superego made flesh, so the group converts shame into prosecution.

Their test—“if he is favored, let reality rescue him”—matches Job’s friends and, speculatively, the Qur’anic taunt scenes where opponents demand a sign as a pretext to dismiss the speaker. It also echoes Stoic and Buddhist motifs where the patient one is “proved” by torment, yet the tormentors mistake equanimity for defeat.

The secrecy and “hour” in John feels like Taoist wu-wei and Bhagavad Gita noncompulsion: agency that moves without advertising itself, timing that is moral rather than tactical. Their insistence on knowing his “whence” parallels Zen’s suspicion of pedigree and Hindu avatāra myths in reverse: the crowd thinks origin should be either fully mundane or utterly unknowable, and can’t tolerate a presence that is both locally familiar and inwardly unplaceable.

Malice as blindness has close kin in Plato’s cave and in the kleshas of Yoga and Buddhism: not ignorance as lack of data but as a motivated mis-seeing. Speculatively, it also resembles Freud’s defense mechanisms—projection and reaction-formation—where the persecutors punish in him what they cannot bear to name in themselves.
