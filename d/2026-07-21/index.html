    IIILectioMicah 7:14-15,18-20

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Micah

    Micah 7:14-15,18-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    14Feed thy people with thy rod, the flock of thy inheritance, them that dwell alone in the forest, in the midst of Carmel: they shall feed in Basan and Galaad, according to the days of old.

    ☞R 14
    Shepherding by rod: care that bruises; inheritance treated as livestock.

    15According to the days of thy coming out of the land of Egypt, I will shew him wonders.

    ☞R 15
    Exodus becomes a template: salvation remembered as repeatable spectacle.

    18Who is a God like to thee, who takest away iniquity, and passest by the sin of the remnant of thy inheritance? he will send his fury in no more, because he delighteth in mercy.

    19He will turn again, and have mercy on us: he will put away our iniquities: and he will cast all our sins into the bottom of the sea.

    ☞R 19
    Sin not managed but drowned; the sea as God’s oubliette.

    20Thou wilt perform the truth to Jacob, the mercy to Abraham: which thou hast sworn to our fathers from the days of old. THE PROPHECY OF NAHUM NAHUM, whose name signifies A COMFORTER, was a native of Elcese, or Elcesai, supposed to be a little town in Galilee. He prophesied, after the ten tribes were carried into captivity, and foretold the utter destruction of Ninive, by the Babylonians and Medes: which happened in the reign of JOSIAS.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 12:46-50

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 12:46-50 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    46As he was yet speaking to the multitudes, behold his mother and his brethren stood without, seeking to speak to him.

    ☞G 46
    Kinship waits outside while the word is mid-sentence; interruption tests priority.

    47And one said unto him: Behold thy mother and thy brethren stand without, seeking thee.

    48But he answering him that told him, said: Who is my mother, and who are my brethren? Who is my mother?... This was not spoken by way of slighting his mother, but to shew that we are never to suffer ourselves to be taken from the service of God, by any inordinate affection to our earthly parents: and that which our Lord chiefly regarded in his mother, was her doing the will of his Father in heaven. It may also further allude to the reprobation of the Jews, his carnal kindred, and the election of the Gentiles.

    ☞G 48
    A question that severs blood from claim; obedience, not proximity, is the credential.

    49And stretching forth his hand towards his disciples, he said: Behold my mother and my brethren.

    ☞G 49
    The hand redraws the family map around discipleship.

    50For whosoever shall do the will of my Father, that is in heaven, he is my brother, and sister, and mother.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s mercy is covenant-anchored and effectual, abolishing guilt rather than merely suspending penalty. In Christ, true kinship is reconstituted by obedience to the Father, not by blood.

    In Lectionem

    • R 18,20Micah’s climax is not Israel’s resolve but God’s self-description: wrath is not denied, it is bounded by a prior commitment. The oath to the fathers functions as the legal ground for pardon; mercy is covenantal, not sentimental.

    In Evangelium

    • G 47,50Matthew frames a jurisdictional transfer: the household of Jesus is constituted by the Father’s will, creating a public, teachable criterion for belonging. The scene legitimates a community that can outlast ethnic and domestic lines because its bond is performative—doing—rather than genealogical.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Micah’s mercy is not tenderness; it’s disposal. The flock “dwells alone,” penned by a rod, nostalgia fed like pasture. Egypt’s “wonders” are the old shock tactics of power, the kind that make a people feel chosen by making them afraid. Forgiveness arrives as erasure: sins hurled into the sea not to heal the wound but to silence it, to make a clean surface where no one has to remember what was done.

    Matthew stages the same logic inside a room. His mother waits outside like the past asking for access; he refuses the claim. He doesn’t broaden family so much as replace it, turning intimacy into a test of obedience. Desire is rerouted from blood to belonging, from the body that made you to the circle that approves you. The comfort offered is real, but it costs: you are saved by being unrecognizable to your own history.

    Semina Verbi

    Micah’s mercy that sinks wrongdoing into the sea has kinship with the Hindu image of karma burned to ash in jñāna, and with Pure Land Buddhism’s trust that the weight of failure can be carried by Other Power; speculation: the “sea” works like a psychic abyss where compulsive self-accusation is finally dropped rather than endlessly rehearsed. The Exodus “wonders” echo the Daoist sense of de as a quiet, reality-bending efficacy that arrives when force relaxes, and the Sufi motif of kashf where the world suddenly shows its hidden generosity.

    Matthew’s redefinition of family by obedience resembles the Stoic cosmopolis and Confucian ren: belonging grounded in lived virtue rather than blood; speculation: it names a mature attachment, where loyalty to the real displaces loyalty to the familiar. It also touches the Buddhist sangha as chosen kin, and the Sikh sangat as a community formed by practice, not lineage, without needing contempt for one’s household—only a refusal to let it be the ultimate claim.
