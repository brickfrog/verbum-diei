    IIILectioActs 18:23-28

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 18:23-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    23And after he had spent some time there, he departed and went through the country of Galatia and Phrygia, in order, confirming all the disciples.

    ☞R 23
    Itinerant consolidation: movement as governance; “confirming” is the verb of continuity.

    24Now a certain Jew, named Apollo, born at Alexandria, an eloquent man, came to Ephesus, one mighty in the scriptures.

    ☞R 24
    Alexandria exports rhetoric; “mighty in the scriptures” signals power before precision.

    25This man was instructed in the way of the Lord: and being fervent in spirit, spoke and taught diligently the things that are of Jesus, knowing only the baptism of John.

    ☞R 25
    Heat without fullness: Jesus on the tongue, John’s baptism in the hands.

    26This man therefore began to speak boldly in the synagogue. Whom when Priscilla and Aquila had heard, they took him to them and expounded to him the way of the Lord more diligently.

    ☞R 26
    Bold in public, corrected in private; the Church edits its own speakers.

    27And whereas he was desirous to go to Achaia, the brethren exhorting wrote to the disciples to receive him. Who, when he was come, helped them much who had believed.

    28For with much vigour he convinced the Jews openly, shewing by the scriptures that Jesus is the Christ.

    ☞R 28
    Scripture becomes a courtroom: “shewing” is evidence, “Christ” the verdict.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 16:23-28

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 16:23-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    23And in that day you shall not ask me any thing. Amen, amen, I say to you: if you ask the Father any thing in my name, he will give it you.

    ☞G 23
    A new access protocol: the Father is addressed, the Name is the key.

    24Hitherto, you have not asked any thing in my name. Ask, and you shall receive; that your joy may be full.

    ☞G 24
    Joy is not mood but completion; reception is the measure of asking.

    25These things I have spoken to you in proverbs. The hour cometh when I will no longer speak to you in proverbs, but will shew you plainly of the Father.

    ☞G 25
    Proverbs are a veil; “plainly” is disclosure, not mere clarity.

    26In that day, you shall ask in my name: and I say not to you that I will ask the Father for you.

    ☞G 26
    Intercession is reconfigured: not bypassed, but internalized into the disciple’s prayer.

    27For the Father himself loveth you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came out from God.

    28I came forth from the Father and am come into the world: again I leave the world and I go to the Father.

    ☞G 28
    The arc is exit and return; mission is a loop back to the Father.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChrist grants filial access to the Father, and the Church safeguards that access by correcting and commissioning its teachers so that Scripture’s witness lands on the true Christ.

    In Lectionem

    • R 27The episode stages a transfer of authority: letters of reception, communal exhortation, and the newcomer’s usefulness. Doctrine travels by networks, not charisma alone; the “help” given is ecclesial reinforcement of prior belief rather than a fresh foundation.

    In Evangelium

    • G 27The ground of prayer is relocated from human mediation to divine affection: love of the Son and belief in his origin establish filial standing. The discourse ties petition to Christology—who Jesus is determines what “in my name” can mean.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Acts stages a quiet violence: the community corrects its own prodigy. Apollos arrives polished, ardent, “mighty in the scriptures,” and yet incomplete, stalled at John’s baptism, trapped in an old threshold. Priscilla and Aquila do not applaud; they take him aside. The truth here is not a lightning bolt but an annexation. His eloquence is seized, retooled, and redeployed. What looks like guidance is also domestication: charisma brought under house discipline, desire redirected into acceptable channels. He becomes useful, and usefulness is the congregation’s favorite form of sanctity.

    John’s Jesus offers a different kind of control, more intimate and more frightening. “You shall not ask me anything,” he says, as if the relationship must survive his absence by becoming procedural: requests routed “in my name,” joy promised as a kind of compliance reward. He withdraws even from intercession—“I say not…that I will ask the Father for you”—and calls it love. The dread is that love is now direct exposure: no mediator, no buffer, just you and the Father’s gaze. The desire is to believe this exposure is safety.

    Both texts circle the same ache: the teacher is leaving, the disciple must be rewired. Apollos is rewired by friends; the disciples are rewired by language that turns longing into petition. The plainness promised is not comfort. It is the moment when the story stops being proverb and becomes a demand: speak correctly, ask correctly, belong correctly, and call the tightening of the net “full joy.”

    Semina Verbi

    Apollos reads like the gifted partial-knower: charisma outruns initiation, zeal outruns completion. The psyche here is porous and teachable; correction doesn’t humiliate him because his identity is anchored in the work, not in being right. Priscilla and Aquila practice a quiet pedagogy: they don’t crush public brilliance, they re-channel it through intimacy, like Zen dokusan where the master adjusts the student’s insight in private so the koan ripens without spectacle. Speculation: it also resembles the Sufi adab of companionship, where refinement comes by proximity more than argument.

    John’s promise of asking “in my name” shifts desire from bargaining to alignment. It’s less technique than a re-formed will: joy becomes the affect of congruence, not the reward of getting things. The movement from proverb to plainness parallels the Buddhist turn from skillful means to direct seeing, and the Vedantic move from symbols to aparoksha jnana, immediate knowledge. Speculation: the “Father himself loveth you” echoes bhakti’s claim that the divine response is already prior to the petition; prayer is consent to a love that precedes language.
