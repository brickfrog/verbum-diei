In Hebrew Eben-ezer; so called from the help which the Lord was pleased afterwards to give to his people Israel in that place, by the prayers of Samuel, chap. 7.12.

2And put their army in array against Israel. And when they had joined battle, Israel turned their backs to the Philistines: and there were slain in that fight, here and there in the fields, about four thousand men.

3And the people returned to the camp: and the ancients of Israel said: Why hath the Lord defeated us to day before the Philistines? Let us fetch unto us the ark of the covenant of the Lord from Silo, and let it come in the midst of us, that it may save us from the hand of our enemies.

4So the people sent to Silo, and they brought from thence the ark of the covenant of the Lord of hosts, sitting upon the cherubims: and the two sons of Heli, Ophni and Phinees, were with the ark of the covenant of God.

5And when the ark of the covenant of the Lord was come into the camp, all Israel shouted with a great shout, and the earth rang again.

6And the Philistines heard the noise of the shout, and they said: What is this noise of a great shout in the camp of the Hebrews? And they understood that the ark of the Lord was come into the camp.

7And the Philistines were afraid, saying: God is come into the camp. And sighing, they said:

8Woe to us: for there was no such great joy yesterday, and the day before: Woe to us. Who shall deliver us from the hand of these high Gods? these are the Gods that struck Egypt with all the plagues in the desert.

9Take courage, and behave like men, ye Philistines: lest you come to be servants to the Hebrews, as they have served you: take courage and fight.

10So the Philistines fought, and Israel was overthrown, and every man fled to his own dwelling: and there was an exceeding great slaughter; for there fell of Israel thirty thousand footmen.

11And the ark of God was taken: and the two sons of Heli, Ophni and Phinees, were slain.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 2,10The text stages a false sacramentalism: defeat provokes a ritual escalation, but the outcome worsens. Israel treats the covenant sign as leverage; God allows the sign to be stripped away to prove it was never a talisman and to accelerate judgment already hanging over Shiloh’s priesthood.
On the Gospel
  • G 41,44Mark juxtaposes sovereign immediacy (“I will”) with commanded mediation (priestly verification). The miracle asserts personal authority while still forcing the healed man back through the law’s public channels, so that Israel’s institutions are compelled to register what has arrived.

DoctrinalGod is not contained by his signs, and his presence cannot be seized for advantage. In Christ, divine holiness actively cleanses and then commands, binding mercy to obedience.

Heterodox Reading
Israel loses because it tries to turn dread into equipment. After four thousand corpses, the elders don’t ask what they desire; they ask for a device. The ark is dragged in like a talisman, a portable certainty meant to cancel the raw fact of defeat. The shout is panic disguised as faith, a mass attempt to drown out the suspicion that God is not obliged to their plan. The Philistines, oddly, understand the psychology better. They fear the story, not the object, then convert fear into a hard, masculine self-command. Israel does the opposite: it sanctifies its terror, then collapses. The result is the nightmare the crowd was trying to prevent: the holy thing captured, the priests’ sons dead, the symbol exposed as vulnerable. The text’s cruelty is precise: the ark doesn’t fail; Israel’s need to possess God does. The leper comes with the same hunger, but he doesn’t seize. He asks, “If you will,” and Jesus answers with touch, not theater. The miracle is intimate, almost private, then Jesus tries to keep it that way, insisting on restraint and ritual. But desire won’t stay contained. The healed man advertises the gift, and the gift becomes a problem: Jesus is pushed outside, into deserted places, as if healing itself creates a new contagion. Put together, the readings sketch a bleak economy of holiness: crowds use it to manage fear, and it slips their grip. When holiness is treated as property, it gets taken; when it is treated as intimacy, it turns into rumor and exile. Either way, no one gets to keep it safely in camp or city. The holy moves, and it leaves people displaced.
Semina Verbi
Israel loses and reaches for leverage: bring the Ark, turn holy presence into a battlefield instrument. The shout is mass suggestion, a morale drug, and the elders’ question—“why”—is answered with a substitute: not repentance, not reality-testing, just a powerful object and the right men to carry it. The irony bites: the place called “Stone of help” becomes the stage for helplessness. The Philistines, superstitious in their own way, mirror Israel’s mistake; both sides imagine the divine as a transferable advantage. The result is psychological and narrative justice: the fetish fails, the corrupt priests die, and the symbol is captured, exposing how thin the people’s trust has become. Mark turns the logic inside out. Here holiness is not hauled into camp; it walks toward the excluded. The leper asks for will, not mechanism: “If you will.” Jesus’ touch breaks the fear of contamination and restores a person to human contact, then sends him back through the public rites so reintegration is real, not private magic. Yet the healed man cannot keep the secret; gratitude becomes compulsion, and the crowd’s hunger pushes Jesus into the margins. Power that is given freely creates disorder, not because it is false but because it rearranges status, access, and desire. Speculation: the Ark episode resembles critiques of “using the gods” found in Greek tragedy, where sacrilege and overconfidence court ruin, and in Buddhist warnings against siddhi as spectacle rather than liberation. The leper scene echoes Taoist and Sufi motifs of the saint who transgresses purity boundaries with compassion, and Hindu bhakti’s insistence that grace is personal will, not ritual technology.
