IIILectioSirach 47:2-11

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Sirach

Sirach 47:2-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

2And as the fat taken away from the flesh, so was David chosen from among the children of Israel.

☞R 2
Election rendered as butchery: the chosen is what is cut away.

3He played with lions as with lambs: and with bears he did in like manner as with the lambs of the flock, in his youth.

☞R 3
Predation domesticated; the shepherd’s child plays at Eden with teeth still present.

4Did not he kill the giant, and take away reproach from his people?

5In lifting up his hand, with the stone in the sling he beat down the boasting of Goliath:

☞R 5
Tiny stone, huge mouth; boasting is a physical thing to be struck down.

6For he called upon the Lord the Almighty, and he gave strength in his right hand, to take away the mighty warrior, and to set up the horn of his nation.

☞R 6
Invocation precedes force; the “right hand” is lent, not owned.

7So in ten thousand did he glorify him, and praised him in the blessings of the Lord, in offering to him a crown of glory:

8For he destroyed the enemies on every side, and extirpated the Philistines the adversaries unto this day: he broke their horn for ever.

☞R 8
Horn-language: power is treated as an animal growth that can be broken.

9In all his works he gave thanks to the holy one, and to the most High, with words of glory.

10With his whole heart he praised the Lord, and loved God that made him: and he gave him power against his enemies:

11And he set singers before the altar, and by their voices he made sweet melody.

☞R 11
After blood, liturgy: violence resolves into ordered sound.

IVEvangeliumMark 6:14-29

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Mark

Mark 6:14-29 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

14And king Herod heard, (for his name was made manifest,) and he said: John the Baptist is risen again from dead, and therefore mighty works shew forth themselves in him.

☞G 14
Guilt manufactures miracles: the dead must return to explain the living.

15And others said: It is Elias. But others said: It is a prophet, as one of the prophets.

16Which Herod hearing, said: John whom I beheaded, he is risen again from the dead.

☞G 16
He names the deed plainly—“I beheaded”—and still calls it resurrection.

17For Herod himself had sent and apprehended John, and bound him prison for the sake of Herodias the wife of Philip his brother, because he had married her.

18For John said to Herod: It is not lawful for thee to have thy brother's wife.

☞G 18
Law is a sentence, not advice; it makes the bedroom a courtroom.

19Now Herodias laid snares for him: and was desirous to put him to death and could not.

20For Herod feared John, knowing him to be a just and holy man: and kept him, and when he heard him, did many things: and he heard him willingly. And kept him... That is, from the designs of Herodias; and for fear of the people, would not put him to death, though she sought it; and through her daughter she effected her wish.

21And when a convenient day was come, Herod made a supper for his birthday, for the princes, and tribunes, and chief men of Galilee.

☞G 21
“Convenient day”: time itself becomes an accomplice.

22And when the daughter of the same Herodias had come in, and had danced, and pleased Herod, and them that were at table with him, the king said to the damsel: Ask of me what thou wilt, and I will give it thee.

23And he swore to her: Whatsoever thou shalt ask I will give thee, though it be the half of my kingdom.

☞G 23
Half a kingdom offered to a dancer: sovereignty auctioned by impulse.

24Who when she was gone out, said to her mother, What shall I ask? But her mother said: The head of John the Baptist.

25And when she was come in immediately with haste to the king, she asked, saying: I will that forthwith thou give me in a dish, the head of John the Baptist.

☞G 25
Haste plus “forthwith”: desire wants speed more than reasons.

26And the king was struck sad. Yet because of his oath, and because of them that were with him at table, he would not displease her:

27But sending an executioner, he commanded that his head should be brought in a dish.

28And he beheaded him in the prison, and brought his head in a dish: and gave to the damsel, and the damsel gave it her mother.

☞G 28
The head circulates as a gift; murder converted into table-service.

29Which his disciples hearing came, and took his body, and laid it in a tomb.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s anointed power is ordered toward thanksgiving and worship; worldly power, ruled by appetite and audience, converts truth-telling into a trophy and calls it necessity.

In Lectionem

  • R 7,9,10The passage edits kingship into doxology: the public achievements are narrated as praise-acts, so rule is legitimated by attribution. The political arc bends toward cultic order; the king’s greatness is measured by whether glory is returned upward rather than hoarded.

In Evangelium

  • G 17,19,20,22,26,29The narrative dissects weak authority: Herod is governed by a triangle—illicit bond, domestic resentment, public spectators. He oscillates between fascination with the holy and fear of losing face, and the banquet seals the trap: an oath spoken for an audience becomes a law stronger than conscience. The disciples’ burial is the only coherent act left; it exposes the court’s spectacle as sterile power.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Sirach polishes David into a usable myth: the boy who pets predators, the man who turns slaughter into song. But the music is not an afterthought; it is the technology that makes violence feel like vocation. “With his whole heart” he loves the maker who authorizes his appetite. The sweet melody is the seal over a mouth that has tasted blood.

Mark shows what happens when power loses the ability to sanctify itself. Herod is not a tyrant of conviction but of nerves. He “hears willingly” because John’s voice gives him the sensation of being judged, and that sensation feels like life. He keeps the prophet like a caged conscience, a dangerous pet.

Then desire enters as performance: a dance, a room of watching men, a vow spoken to preserve an image. The head on a dish is not simply cruelty; it is the banquet’s true course, the price of male bravado and female resentment, the conversion of speech into meat. David’s singers make killing sound holy; Herod’s guests make it look inevitable. In both, dread and desire collaborate to turn a human throat into an instrument—either for praise or for silence.

Semina Verbi

David’s lion-play is the archetype of trained ferocity: the psyche rehearses danger until it becomes intimate, the way a Zen swordsman or a Shaolin adept makes fear into a familiar tool. It is not innocence; it is aggression disciplined into protection. The “sweet melody” before the altar turns violence into order, like Orphic song taming the underworld or the Vedic idea that right chant stabilizes the world; speculation: art here is the sublimation that keeps power from rotting into mere appetite.

Herod is a study in weak will enthroned. He “fears” the just man and enjoys hearing him, yet lets his image at a banquet murder his conscience; this is Plato’s tyrant-soul, enslaved by desire and the gaze of others. The birthday feast becomes a Dionysian court where eros and spectacle write law, and the prophet’s severed head is the grisly anti-sacrament, closer to the scapegoat mechanism René Girard traces in many cultures. The oath functions like dharma misread as face-saving: a vow treated as fate, when it is only vanity hardened into destiny.
