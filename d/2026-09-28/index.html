    IIILectioJob 1:6-22

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Job

    Job 1:6-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    6Now on a certain day, when the sons of God came to stand before the Lord, Satan also was present among them.

    ☞R 6
    The Accuser is not outside the court; he is admitted.

    7And the Lord said to him: Whence comest thou? And he answered and said: I have gone round about the earth, and walked through it.

    ☞R 7
    Restless patrol: evil as itinerant attention.

    8And the Lord said to him: Hast thou considered my servant, Job, that there is none like him in the earth, a simple and upright man, and fearing God, and avoiding evil?

    9And Satan answering, said: Doth Job fear God in vain?

    10Hast thou not made a fence for him, and his house, and all his substance round about, blessed the works of his hands, and his possession hath increased on the earth?

    ☞R 10
    Piety interpreted as hedged investment; blessing as bribe.

    11But stretch forth thy hand a little, and touch all that he hath, and see if he bless thee not to thy face.

    12Then the Lord said to Satan: Behold, all that he hath is in thy hand: only put not forth thy hand upon his person. And Satan went forth from the presence of the Lord.

    ☞R 12
    Permission with a boundary: delegated harm under constraint.

    13Now upon a certain day, when his sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine, in the house of their eldest brother,

    14There came a messenger to Job, and said: The oxen were ploughing, and the asses feeding beside them,

    15And the Sabeans rushed in, and took all away, and slew the servants with the sword; and I alone have escaped to tell thee.

    ☞R 15
    Catastrophe as litany; each report interrupts the last—no time to grieve, only to receive.

    16And while he was yet speaking, another came, and said: The fire of God fell from heaven, and striking the sheep and the servants, hath consumed them; and I alone have escaped to tell thee.

    ☞R 16
    Catastrophe as litany; each report interrupts the last—no time to grieve, only to receive.

    17And while he also was yet speaking, there came another, and said: The Chaldeans made three troops, and have fallen upon the camels, and taken them; moreover, they have slain the servants with the sword: and I alone have escaped to tell thee.

    ☞R 17
    Catastrophe as litany; each report interrupts the last—no time to grieve, only to receive.

    18He was yet speaking, and behold another came in, and said: Thy sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their eldest brother,

    19A violent wind came on a sudden from the side of the desert, and shook the four corners of the house, and it fell upon thy children, and they are dead: and I alone have escaped to tell thee.

    ☞R 19
    Catastrophe as litany; each report interrupts the last—no time to grieve, only to receive.

    20Then Job rose up, and rent his garments, and having shaven his head, fell down upon the ground, and worshipped,

    ☞R 20
    Ritualized ruin: tearing, shaving, prostration—then worship.

    21And said: Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away: as it hath pleased the Lord, so is it done: blessed be the name of the Lord.

    ☞R 21
    The grammar of loss: gave/took; pleasure, not explanation; blessing without reasons.

    22In all these things Job sinned not by his lips, nor spoke he any foolish thing against God.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 9:46-50

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 9:46-50 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    46And there entered a thought into them, which of them should be greater.

    ☞G 46
    Greatness begins as an interior calculation.

    47But Jesus seeing the thoughts of their hearts, took a child and set him by him,

    ☞G 47
    A child placed as an argument, not an ornament.

    48And said to them: Whosoever shall receive this child in my name receiveth me; and whosoever shall receive me receiveth him that sent me. For he that is the lesser among you all, he is the greater.

    ☞G 48
    Reception replaces rank; the small becomes a conduit for the Sender.

    49And John, answering, said: Master, we saw a certain man casting out devils in thy name: and we forbade him, because he followeth not with us.

    ☞G 49
    Orthodoxy policed by proximity: “not with us” as the charge.

    50And Jesus said to him: Forbid him not: for he that is not against you is for you.

    ☞G 50
    Jesus breaks the monopoly: the Name is not a private badge.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s sovereignty is not threatened by the Accuser’s challenge, and true righteousness is measured by faithful speech under loss. In Christ, greatness is participation in divine reception of the least, not possession of exclusive authority.

    In Lectionem

    • R 8,9,11,22The scene frames righteousness as contested evidence: God names Job exemplary, the Accuser proposes an alternative causal story, and the test aims at speech—whether worship collapses into accusation. The narrative’s interest is not in why calamity happens but in what kind of creature a just man is when the explanatory scaffolding is removed.

    In Evangelium

    • G 47,48,50Jesus answers the disciples’ rivalry by redefining mediation: access to him and to the Father is routed through receiving the least, not through securing status. The follow-up about the outsider exorcist exposes the same ambition in ecclesial form—control of efficacy—met by a rule that prioritizes alignment with Jesus’ work over factional boundaries.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Job is not a morality play; it’s a scene of divine boredom looking for friction. God points at Job the way a proud owner points at a purebred, and the Accuser names the obscene truth: virtue here is subsidized. The “fence” is the real god Job serves. Remove it and you find out what’s underneath.

    The disasters arrive like a liturgy of interruption, each messenger cutting off the last, language itself made into a conveyor belt for loss. Job’s famous sentence is not serenity; it’s dissociation under pressure, a controlled collapse into formula. “Blessed be the name” is what you say when grief is too large to fit in the mouth. He does not sin by his lips because his lips have become a mask.

    Luke shows the same hunger in miniature. The disciples’ desire to be greater is naked, and Jesus answers with a child, not as innocence but as leverage: the smallest body becomes the token that rearranges status. Then John tries to police power—exorcism as brand management. Jesus refuses the monopoly. The dread beneath both readings is that belonging is never secure: God can lift the fence, and the group can’t control who carries the name. Desire wants guarantees; the text offers exposure.

    Semina Verbi

    Job’s ordeal reads like the Zen koan of unearned catastrophe: the mind demands a moral ledger, reality refuses, and the practice becomes nonreactivity without numbness. Speculation: it also resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga stripped to the bone—action and loss severed from entitlement—except here the “battlefield” is domestic life collapsing.

    The child set beside the teacher echoes Daoist softness as strength: the uncarved block that disarms status-games by not playing them. Speculation: the outsider exorcist feels close to Ashoka’s edicts of religious toleration and to the rabbinic intuition that righteousness can exist beyond the in-group; power that heals is recognized by its fruit, not its badge.
