12Moreover, see and know, O my father, the hem of thy robe in my hand, that when I cut off the hem of thy robe, I would not put out my hand against thee. 13The Lord judge between me and thee and the Lord revenge me of thee: but my hand shall not be upon thee. Revenge me of thee... Or, as it is in the Hebrew, will revenge me. The meaning is, that he refers his whole cause to God, to judge and punish according to his justice: yet so as to keep himself in the mean time, from all personal hatred to Saul, or desire of gratifying his own passion, by seeking revenge. So far from it, that when Saul was afterwards slain, we find, that instead of rejoicing at his death, he mourned most bitterly for him.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 8,9David’s restraint does not end the conflict; it redefines its jurisdiction. He refuses to let political succession be achieved by sacrilege, and he stages that refusal publicly—exiting the cave to address the king under the eyes of the court-world Saul carries with him. The scene is a rehearsal for kingship as patient legitimacy rather than opportunistic seizure.
  • R 13,16,21The appeal to divine judgment is not piety-as-ornament but a relocation of vengeance outside the human hand. That relocation creates time: Saul can acknowledge what he “knows” about the future without David forcing the future through murder. The narrative links a stable kingdom to a delayed grasp.
On the Gospel
  • G 13,16,18The mountain is not scenery; it is a new Sinai where authority is reorganized around a named company. The list is the point: particular persons are drawn into a corporate identity that will outlast individual temperaments and reputations. Renaming marks transfer of allegiance and the making of a public body.
  • G 14,19The sending is constituted with an internal vulnerability: the mission is launched with a traitor inside the charter membership. The text refuses any fantasy of a pure founding; apostolic authority begins as a mixed field where fidelity and betrayal grow adjacent.

DoctrinalGod’s rule advances by legitimate calling and delegated authority, not by seizing power through violence. The people of God are constituted with real offices and real corruption, yet the mission proceeds under judgment reserved to God.

Heterodox Reading
The cave is not a chapel; it’s a body. Saul enters to empty himself, kingly power reduced to a private need, and David is already inside him, hidden in the “inner part,” like the thought you can’t admit you’re thinking. The cut hem is a fetish and a threat: not mercy, but possession. David takes a piece of Saul and then calls it innocence. His heart “strikes” him because he has tasted what it feels like to dominate without being seen, and he wants that taste without the guilt. David refuses to kill because killing would end the erotic tension. A living Saul is the perfect witness, the perfect antagonist, the perfect father to bow before while quietly holding proof of castration. “My father” is not tenderness; it’s conquest by intimacy. He hands vengeance to God the way a careful man hands a weapon to someone else, so his hands stay clean while the outcome stays his. On the mountain, Jesus does something colder than it sounds: he calls “whom he would.” The twelve are not chosen for virtue but for use, to be with him and then to be sent, power routed through them like current. He names them thunder, rock—roles, not selves—and he includes Judas as part of the design, the necessary fracture inside the circle. The dread here is that betrayal isn’t an accident; it’s the price of forming a body. Like Saul in the cave, the group is most vulnerable at its most human, and that vulnerability is exactly where authority is made.
Semina Verbi
David’s restraint in the cave has the flavor of ahimsa in Jain and Hindu ethics: power proven by non-use, victory defined as self-mastery; speculation, but the “hem” reads like a token of conquered impulse rather than conquered man. Saul’s sudden weeping resembles Greek tragic anagnorisis, the moment the pursuer recognizes himself as the true threat and the hunted as the measure of justice. The mountain choosing of twelve echoes Buddhist saṅgha formation and the guru’s selective call in several yogic lineages: not a crowd-movement but a crafted vessel; the nicknames feel like Zen-style naming that frames a disciple’s temperament as both gift and hazard. Judas within the circle parallels the necessary shadow-member in Sufi and Tantric stories, where betrayal is the dark instrument that forces the teacher’s work to complete itself; speculation, but it treats failure as structurally included, not merely accidental.
