18And they said: Come, and let us invent devices against Jeremias: for the law shall not perish from the priest, nor counsel from the wise, nor the word from the prophet: come, and let us strike him with the tongue, and let us give no heed to all his words.

19Give heed to me, O Lord, and hear the voice of my adversaries.

20Shall evil be rendered for good, because they have digged a pit for my soul? Remember that I have stood in thy sight, to speak good for them, and to turn away thy indignation from them. Remember, etc... This is spoken in the person of Christ, persecuted by the Jews, and prophetically denouncing the evils that should fall upon them in punishment of their crimes.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 19The prophet appeals over the heads of the crowd to the only competent judge; the case is not rhetorical but juridical—witness, adversary, verdict.
  • R 18,20Jeremiah’s scene establishes the pattern: the community claims continuity (“law… counsel… word”) while silencing the living word; the result is a counterfeit tradition that preserves offices by eliminating interruption.
On the Gospel
  • G 17,18,19,23The ascent to Jerusalem frames two orders of will: human plotting and divine preparation. Jesus locates betrayal, condemnation, and Gentile cruelty inside a providential itinerary, and then refuses to treat “places” as spoils, assigning them to the Father’s prior appointment.
  • G 25,26,27,28The contrast with Gentile rule is not merely ethical advice; it is ecclesial constitution. The community that follows the crucified Messiah must organize power around ministry because its founder’s act is substitutionary self-gift, not domination.

Doctrinal God’s kingdom overturns status by rooting authority in sacrificial service, and it exposes religious violence as the attempt to preserve office by silencing the mediator. The Church is ordered by the chalice: participation in Christ’s suffering, not proximity to prestige.

Heterodox Reading
Jeremiah’s enemies aren’t defending truth; they’re defending the system that makes truth unnecessary. “The law shall not perish… nor counsel… nor the word…” is the smug creed of a culture that can survive without listening. If the priest still has law and the wise still have counsel, then the prophet is surplus. So they “strike him with the tongue”: assassination by speech, the clean violence of reputations, the pit dug for the soul. Jeremiah’s plea isn’t pious; it’s the wounded insistence of someone who tried to save people who now need him silenced to keep their conscience intact. Jesus walks toward Jerusalem with the script of his own humiliation already spoken aloud, and right after that prophecy comes the rawest human impulse in the room: a mother bargaining for proximity to power. The kingdom is imagined as a throne room, not a grave. Her desire is not abnormal; it’s the honest form of what the others are thinking, proven by their indignation. They’re not scandalized by ambition; they’re jealous of its audacity. The “chalice” is not a metaphor for generic hardship; it is the taste of being used, spat out, and publicly erased. They say, “We can,” because desire always overestimates its courage. Jesus doesn’t romanticize them or reject them; he predicts they will indeed drink it, as if the only way to cure their hunger for status is to let them discover how bitter it is. And his alternative to domination is not sweetness but dread: serve, be last, be spent. The ransom line lands like an unasked-for diagnosis: the world runs on exchange, blood for freedom, bodies turned into payment. The good news, here, is also the terrible news: the only authority that won’t corrupt you is the kind that feels like losing everything.
Semina Verbi
The prophet’s enemies protect their status by claiming the institutions will survive without him; it is the psychology of gatekeeping dressed as piety. In Confucian terms it resembles the court that invokes li and tradition to silence the remonstrating minister, treating criticism as disorder rather than loyalty. “Strike him with the tongue” is social execution: rumor as weapon, belonging as cudgel. This parallels the Athenian dynamic around Socrates, where the city defends its self-image by prosecuting the irritant who names its contradictions. The request for thrones beside the doomed king is ambition clinging to a fantasy of victory, a family’s understandable attempt to translate love into leverage. In Buddhist terms it echoes the subtle grasping for spiritual rank; the craving survives even in the presence of mortality. Greatness redefined as service aligns with the Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga: action without grasping at the fruit, authority expressed as duty rather than entitlement. Speculation: the “cup” functions like the Stoic lot—what must be borne—accepted not as romance but as the price of integrity. Ransom language touches a widespread intuition: a life can be spent to free others from fear and debt. One can hear an analogue in the bodhisattva vow, where liberation is pursued by taking on the weight of others, not escaping it.
