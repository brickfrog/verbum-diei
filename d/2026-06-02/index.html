Who lives and reigns for ever and ever.

Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    IIILectio2 Peter 3:12-15,17-18

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Second Letter of St. Peter

    2 Peter 3:12-15,17-18 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12Looking for and hasting unto the coming of the day of the Lord, by which the heavens being on fire shall be dissolved, and the elements shall melt with the burning heat?

    ☞R 12
    Eschaton as solvent: fire is not ornament but chemistry.

    13But we look for new heavens and a new earth according to his promises, in which justice dwelleth.

    ☞R 13
    “Justice dwelleth”: the new creation is zoned for righteousness, not novelty.

    14Wherefore, dearly beloved, waiting for these things, be diligent that you may be found before him unspotted and blameless in peace.

    ☞R 14
    “Found before him”: judgment imagined as inspection; peace is a condition, not a mood.

    15And account the longsuffering of our Lord, salvation: as also our most dear brother Paul, according to the wisdom given him, hath written to you:

    17You therefore, brethren, knowing these things before, take heed, lest being led aside by the error of the unwise, you fall from your own steadfastness.

    ☞R 17
    Foreknowledge increases liability; error is a current that carries the unmoored.

    18But grow in grace and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. To him be glory both now and unto the day of eternity, Amen. THE FIRST EPISTLE OF ST. JOHN THE APOSTLE The same vein of divine love and charity towards our neighbour, which runs throughout the Gospel written by the beloved disciple and Evangelist, St. John, is found also in his Epistles. He confirms the two principal mysteries of faith: The mystery of the Trinity and the mystery of the incarnation of Jesus Christ the Son of God. The sublimity and excellence of the evangelical doctrine he declares: And this commandment we have from God, that he, who loveth God, love also his brother (chap. 4,21). And again: For this is the charity of God, that we keep his commandments, and: His commandments are not heavy (chap. 5,3). He shews how to distinguish the children of God from those of the devil: marks out those who should be called Antichrists: describes the turpitude and gravity of sin. Finally, he shews how the sinner may hope for pardon. It was written, according to Baronius' account, sixty-six years after our Lord's Ascension.

    IVEvangeliumMark 12:13-17

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Mark

    Mark 12:13-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    13And they sent to him some of the Pharisees and of the Herodians: that they should catch him in his words.

    ☞G 13
    A coalition of enemies forms when truth threatens revenue.

    14Who coming, say to him: Master, we know that thou art a true speaker and carest not for any man; for thou regardest not the person of men, but teachest the way of God in truth. Is it lawful to give tribute to Caesar? Or shall we not give it?

    ☞G 14
    Flattery as bait: they praise impartiality to weaponize it.

    15Who knowing their wiliness, saith to them: Why tempt you me? Bring me a penny that I may see it.

    16And they brought it him. And he saith to them: Whose is this image and inscription? They say to him, Caesar's.

    ☞G 16
    Image and inscription: sovereignty stamped into metal, theology hiding in currency.

    17And Jesus answering, said to them: Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's and to God the things that are God's. And they marvelled at him.

    ☞G 17
    Two claims, two realms; the marvel is the refusal to be conscripted by their binary.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s coming judgment makes present holiness non-negotiable, and God’s sovereignty sets a boundary the state cannot cross. The faithful render temporal dues without surrendering the self that bears God’s image.

    In Lectionem

    • R 15,18The passage frames the delay of the end as purposeful time: “longsuffering” is interpreted as salvation, and apostolic teaching is presented as a single, coherent witness (Peter invoking Paul) that stabilizes the community against drift. The closing doxology relocates the horizon from catastrophe to worship: the end is not merely an event to survive but a telos that orders growth now.

    In Evangelium

    • G 15,17Jesus forces the questioners to supply the evidence of imperial claim by producing the coin; the trap collapses because the interlocutors already participate in Caesar’s economy. The answer does not sacralize the state; it limits it by defining what properly belongs to God, a category the state cannot mint. The scene is a public unmasking: political theology is decided by ownership and obligation, not by slogans.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Peter’s fire is not meteorology but libido for erasure. “Hasting” the day is a confession that the present is intolerable, that the self wants to be liquefied into something “unspotted,” even if the price is the world’s skin burning off. The delay is framed as mercy, but it reads like suspense: a long, grinding interval where desire is forced to call itself patience.

    “Unspotted and blameless in peace” is the fantasy of being finally unaccusable, beyond gossip, beyond one’s own memory. The warning about being “led aside” sounds less like doctrinal caution than fear of contagion: other people’s looseness might legitimize your own, and then you lose the one thing holding you together—steadfastness as a kind of clenched fist.

    Jesus with the coin refuses the trap by splitting the soul. The image belongs to Caesar because it already branded you; the tribute is just admitting the mark. But “to God the things that are God’s” turns ominous, because what bears God’s image is not a coin but you. Pay the tax, yes, but the real demand is total: your face, your breath, your inward life. They marvel because he dodges their politics and exposes their dread—the state can take money; God wants the person.

    Semina Verbi

    The melting heavens and the call to be found “in peace” touch the Stoic ekpyrosis: a world periodically burned clean, with the wise training now for the only stable good, virtue. Speculation: the Christian fire keeps the moral urgency but refuses the Stoic cycle by aiming at a genuinely new order where justice is not merely internal composure but a shared habitation.

    The delay that is “salvation” resonates with Mahayana Buddhism’s patience and bodhisattva vow: time is stretched by compassion so others can cross. Speculation: where Buddhism frames delay as skillful means within impermanence, this text frames it as personal address—history as mercy, not mechanism.

    “Image and inscription” meets the Hindu idea of the atman as bearing the mark of the real, and the Jewish sense of the human as tzelem Elohim: the coin’s image returns to its ruler, the person’s deeper imprint returns to its source. Speculation: the saying works like a psychological sorting of loyalties—external obligations paid without surrendering the inner self’s belonging.

    The trap-question and the untrapped answer echo Daoist wu-wei and Zen non-attachment to fixed positions: the refusal to be dragged into binary slogans, answering from a freer level. Speculation: the marvel is the shock of encountering speech that cannot be weaponized because it is not defending an ego or a faction.

    “Grow in grace and knowledge” parallels Sufi tazkiyah and ma‘rifa: purification and knowing that ripen together, not as information but as transformation. Speculation: the warning against being “led aside” reads like a diagnosis of suggestibility—how easily the self borrows its convictions from the loudest mind nearby unless it is steadily re-formed from within.
