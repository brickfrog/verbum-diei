    IIILectio2 Kings 17:5-8,13-15,18

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Second Book of Kings

    2 Kings 17:5-8,13-15,18 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    5And he went through all the land: and going up to Samaria, he besieged it three years.

    ☞R 5
    Three-year siege: judgment is slow, not sudden; time to repent squandered.

    6And in the ninth year of Osee, the king of the Assyrians took Samaria, and carried Israel away to Assyria: and he placed them in Hala, and Habor, by the river of Gozan, in the cities of the Medes.

    ☞R 6
    Exile is geography as theology: names of rivers and cities become a litany of loss.

    7For so it was that the children of Israel had sinned against the Lord, their God, who brought them out of the land of Egypt, from under the hand of Pharao, king of Egypt; and they worshipped strange gods.

    ☞R 7
    The exodus is the baseline; idolatry is not ignorance but betrayal of rescue.

    8And they walked according to the way of the nations which the Lord had destroyed in the sight of the children of Israel, and of the kings of Israel: because they had done in like manner.

    13And the Lord testified to them in Israel, and in Juda, by the hand of all the prophets and seers, saying: Return from your wicked ways, and keep my precepts, and ceremonies, according to all the law which I commanded your fathers: and as I have sent to you in the hand of my servants the prophets.

    14And they hearkened not, but hardened their necks like to the neck of their fathers, who would not obey the Lord, their God.

    15And they rejected his ordinances, and the covenant that he made with their fathers, and the testimonies which he testified against them: and they followed vanities, and acted vainly: and they followed the nations that were round about them, concerning which the Lord had commanded them that they should not do as they did.

    ☞R 15
    “Vanities” breeds “vainly”: worship reshapes the worshipper into its emptiness.

    18And the Lord was very angry with Israel, and removed them from his sight, and there remained only the tribe of Juda.

    ☞R 18
    Removed “from his sight”: the worst punishment is being un-seen by God.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 7:1-5

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 7:1-5 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Judge not, that you may not be judged.

    ☞G 1
    Prohibition is pragmatic: judgment rebounds; the sentence returns to the speaker.

    2For with what judgment you judge, you shall be judged: and with what measure you mete, it shall be measured to you again.

    ☞G 2
    Measure-for-measure: the courtroom is portable; you carry it in your hand.

    3And why seest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye; and seest not the beam that is in thy own eye?

    ☞G 3
    Mote/beam: moral optics fail first at home; proximity blinds.

    4Or how sayest thou to thy brother: Let me cast the mote out of thy eye; and behold a beam is in thy own eye?

    5Thou hypocrite, cast out first the beam out of thy own eye, and then shalt thou see to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye.

    ☞G 5
    “First” is the order of surgery: self-amputation precedes correction of another.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s judgments are covenantal and reciprocal: the worshipper becomes what he serves, and the judge is measured by his own measure. Therefore self-judgment is the necessary precondition for any true judgment of another.

    In Lectionem

    • R 8,13The narrative frames conquest as covenant litigation: imitation of the nations is treated as a legal breach, and the prophets function as formal summonses. The fall of Samaria is not merely military defeat but the execution of a long-contested claim—YHWH’s right to define Israel’s worship and life.
    • R 14,18The text isolates the mechanism of collapse: refusal to hear hardens into incapacity to hear, until separation becomes the only remaining form of instruction. What survives (Judah) is not praised; it is simply what remains when the northern kingdom is cut away.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,2Jesus relocates judgment from public tribunal to personal practice: the standard you deploy becomes the standard that governs you. The warning is not against moral discernment but against assuming the role of final assessor while remaining unexamined.
    • G 4,5The command targets the posture of the would-be healer: correction offered from an uncorrected self is violence disguised as care. Only purified sight can claim the authority to intervene in another’s fault.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Samaria falls after three years of siege, but the real siege is internal: a people tired of being singular, craving the easier anesthesia of “the nations round about.” The text calls it vanity, but vanity here is desire for normalcy, for a god you can see, a life that doesn’t ask you to be strange. The prophets arrive like unwanted therapists, insisting on return, and the neck stiffens not from pride alone but from fear of what obedience will cost.

    “Removed them from his sight” reads less like a tantrum than a withdrawal of attention. The worst punishment is not fire; it’s being left to your own mirrors, exiled into the bland, where nothing looks back. Assyria becomes the exterior form of an interior choice: if you want other gods, you will get the world that comes with them.

    Then Matthew tightens the screw. “Judge not” isn’t moral gentleness; it’s a warning about projection. The beam is the hidden engine of your outrage, the secret appetite that needs a culprit. You want to fix your brother’s eye because his flaw gives shape to your dread. The command is not to become pure before you speak, but to stop using judgment as a way to avoid yourself.

    Put together, the readings suggest a grim symmetry: the nation is exiled for chasing vanities, and the individual is trapped for chasing verdicts. Both are forms of refusal—refusal to face the costly truth of one’s own desire. The only escape is the humiliating work of seeing clearly, which feels at first like losing everything, because it is losing the lie that kept you comfortable.

    Semina Verbi

    There’s a shared psychology here: exile begins as imitation. A people borrows the surrounding gods because they promise immediacy, and the borrowed promise slowly hollows out memory until identity can be carried off like furniture. The “hardened neck” is not mere stubbornness; it’s the body refusing to turn toward any voice that would interrupt the trance of the crowd.

    The warning about judging lands in the same inner territory. Judgment is a way to keep the self unexamined: you outsource your conscience into surveillance of others. The “measure” is the hidden ruler you live by; it returns because it is already shaping your perception. Hypocrisy is not moral failure so much as a visual disorder: the beam is the self’s unowned need, and it distorts the entire field until the neighbor becomes a convenient screen.

    Semina Verbi: the exile-as-consequence parallels the Hindu and Buddhist idea of karma as the maturation of habituated action, and the Stoic thought that living by the crowd’s opinions is a kind of inner banishment. The “beam and mote” resembles Zen’s insistence that delusion is first-person, and Sufi adab about guarding the tongue from fault-finding; speculation: both texts assume that attention is moral fate—what you repeatedly look at becomes what you become.
