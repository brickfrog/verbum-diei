    IIILectio1 Corinthians 12:12-14,27-31

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 12:12-14,27-31 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12For as the body is one and hath many members; and all the members of the body, whereas they are many, yet are one body: So also is Christ.

    ☞R 12
    “So also is Christ”: the metaphor snaps shut—Christ is not merely head but whole.

    13For in one Spirit were we all baptized into one body, whether Jews or Gentiles, whether bond or free: and in one Spirit we have all been made to drink.

    ☞R 13
    One Spirit: baptism and drinking—initiation and sustenance share a single source.

    14For the body also is not one member, but many.

    27Now you are the body of Christ and members of member.

    ☞R 27
    “members of member”: awkward grammar, sharp point—belonging is nested, not solitary.

    28And God indeed hath set some in the church; first apostles, secondly prophets, thirdly doctors: after that miracles: then the graces of healings, helps, governments, kinds of tongues, interpretations of speeches.

    29Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all doctors?

    30Are all workers of miracles? Have all the grace of healing? Do all speak with tongues? Do all interpret?

    31But be zealous for the better gifts. And I shew unto you yet a more excellent way.

    ☞R 31
    “better gifts” relativized by “more excellent way”: charisma is demoted beneath a path.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 19:25-27

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 19:25-27 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    25Now there stood by the cross of Jesus, his mother and his mother's sister, Mary of Cleophas, and Mary Magdalen.

    26When Jesus therefore had seen his mother and the disciple standing whom he loved, he saith to his mother: Woman, behold thy son.

    ☞G 26
    “Woman”: not tenderness but title; kinship is being re-assigned under the cross.

    27After that, he saith to the disciple: Behold thy mother. And from that hour, the disciple took her to his own.

    ☞G 27
    “from that hour”: a new household begins at a fixed moment, not by sentiment.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe Spirit incorporates many into one Christ, and the crucified Lord constitutes a new household that is truly ecclesial. Gifts and natural ties are subordinated to divinely ordered communion.

    In Lectionem

    • R 28,29,30Paul moves from ontology (one body) to polity (appointments). The list is not a ladder of personal holiness but an ordering of functions for the church’s operation; the barrage of rhetorical questions breaks any attempt to universalize a single role as normative. Unity is maintained not by sameness but by coordinated inequality under divine placement.

    In Evangelium

    • G 25John frames the scene with witnesses—women named, the beloved disciple unnamed—so the new relation is anchored in testimony rather than bloodline. At the cross, Jesus exercises authority while apparently powerless: he disposes of persons, establishing a durable social form that outlives the event.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “one body” is not a warm metaphor; it’s an annexation. Difference is permitted only as function. Jews and Greeks, slaves and free are not reconciled so much as metabolized into a single organism that decides what each limb is for. The hierarchy he names—apostles first—sounds like anatomy, but it’s also a chain of command. His repeated questions aren’t invitations; they are restraints. You will not be everything. Desire is redirected: be zealous, but only for gifts that serve the body’s survival. The “more excellent way” arrives like a promise and a threat: love as the discipline that makes inequality feel holy.

    At the cross, the body is failing, and the only thing left is reassignment. “Woman” is cold, almost clinical, as if mother must be stripped of her private claim. Jesus creates kinship by decree: a new household born from loss, not romance. The beloved disciple takes her “to his own,” and the phrase tastes like possession. Grief becomes infrastructure. The church begins not in triumph but in the management of absence, where longing is given a role so it won’t tear the community apart.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s many-membered body has a close cousin in Plato’s Republic, where the city is healthiest when each part does its own work without envy; the psychological wager is the same: identity stabilizes when difference is given a dignified function. A speculative parallel sits in the Bhagavad Gita’s svadharma: not everyone is Arjuna, and the refusal to be one’s own kind of instrument breeds inner fracture.

    The cross-scene’s creation of kinship by deliberate address echoes Confucian role-ethics: relation is not merely found, it is enacted through naming and obligation. Speculatively, it also resembles Buddhist refuge as re-parenting of the self—taking shelter in a living community when the old supports collapse—except here the bond is forged through grief and entrusted care rather than doctrine.
