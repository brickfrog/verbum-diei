    IIILectioNumbers 21:4-9

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Numbers

    Numbers 21:4-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    4And they marched from mount Hor, by the way that leadeth to the Red Sea, to compass the land of Edom. And the people began to be weary of their journey and labour:

    ☞R 4
    Detour becomes discipline: “compass Edom” breeds fatigue before any enemy appears.

    5And speaking against God and Moses, they said: Why didst thou bring us out of Egypt, to die in the wilderness? There is no bread, nor have we any waters: our soul now loatheth this very light food.

    ☞R 5
    Complaint is liturgy inverted: they indict the Exodus as a murder plot; “light food” becomes contempt for gift.

    6Wherefore the Lord sent among the people fiery serpents, which bit them and killed many of them.

    ☞R 6
    The punishment matches the speech: venom answers venom; death enters through the mouth’s rebellion.

    7Upon which they came to Moses, and said; We have sinned, because we have spoken against the Lord and thee: pray that he may take away these serpents from us. And Moses prayed for the people.

    8And the Lord said to him: Make a brazen serpent, and set it up for a sign: whosoever being struck shall look on it, shall live.

    ☞R 8
    Cure is a scandal: the image of the wound is enthroned; looking, not fighting, is commanded.

    9Moses therefore made a brazen serpent, and set it up for a sign: which when they that were bitten looked upon, they were healed.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 3:13-17

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 3:13-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    13And no man hath ascended into heaven, but he that descended from heaven, the Son of man who is in heaven.

    ☞G 13
    Heaven is not climbed; it is entered by descent. Authority is vertical, not achieved.

    14And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of man be lifted up:

    ☞G 14
    “Lifted up” fuses exaltation and execution; glory is hung in public.

    15That whosoever believeth in him may not perish, but may have life everlasting.

    16For God so loved the world, as to give his only begotten Son: that whosoever believeth in him may not perish, but may have life everlasting.

    ☞G 16
    Love is defined as giving the Only; the world is loved without being flattered.

    17For God sent not his Son into the world, to judge the world: but that the world may be saved by him.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod heals by a lifted sign that exposes the wound and demands faith. In Christ lifted up, salvation comes by believing reception of God’s gift, not by human ascent or self-cure.

    In Lectionem

    • R 7,9Intercession is granted, but not by removing the threat. The economy is pedagogical: sin is confessed, prayer is made, yet the community must live with the consequence and learn a new mode of obedience—healing mediated through a commanded sign rather than immediate relief.

    In Evangelium

    • G 15,17The passage frames salvation as the divine intent toward the perishing world: belief is the appointed means, and the mission’s stated end is rescue rather than courtroom verdict. Judgment is not denied; it is postponed by a saving initiative that creates a present possibility of life.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    The people don’t just complain; they gag on “light food.” The wilderness isn’t a backdrop but a pressure chamber where desire turns rancid. They want Egypt back not because it was good, but because it was thick: predictable misery, solid chains, real meals. Freedom feels like starvation, and they would rather die in a known prison than live in an open ache.

    The serpents are not random punishment; they are the body made honest. What was already inside them—heat, venom, dread—breaks the skin. Then comes the strangest medicine: not removal, not soothing, but an image. Healing requires looking straight at the thing that is killing you, cast into metal and lifted where everyone can’t avoid it. The cure is attention, a compelled confrontation, desire forced to see its own fangs.

    John intensifies the scandal. Salvation arrives not by escaping the world but by being hoisted up inside it, exposed, made into a sign like the serpent: a public object for desperate eyes. “Belief” here is less assent than the willingness to stare at what you fear and want at once. Love does not spare; it lifts the wound into view and says: live by looking, not by fleeing.

    Semina Verbi

    The cure that looks like the wound has cousins elsewhere. In Greek medicine the rod of Asclepius makes the serpent a sign of healing rather than mere threat, as if the psyche can only be freed by facing what terrifies it. In Buddhism the medicine is often the poison itself, transmuted by clear seeing; the bitten one is saved by attention, not flight. In Hindu imagery the nāga is both danger and guardian of life-force, a reminder that the same energy that kills can also awaken when rightly held. In Jung’s language, the lifted serpent is the shadow made visible, no longer acting from below. Speculation: John’s “lifted up” echoes this cross-cultural logic of transformation through exposure, where salvation is not escape from the world’s venom but a reorientation of gaze that changes what the venom means.
