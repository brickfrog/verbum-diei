    IIILectioJob 42:1-3,5-6,12-17

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Job

    Job 42:1-3,5-6,12-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Then Job answered the Lord, and said:

    2I know that thou canst do all things, and no thought is hid from thee.

    ☞R 2
    Omnipotence paired with omniscience: no refuge in “misunderstanding.”

    3Who is this that hideth counsel without knowledge? Therefore I have spoken unwisely, and things that above measure exceeded my knowledge.

    ☞R 3
    The question is God’s; Job answers by indicting his own speech.

    5With the hearing of the ear, I have heard thee, but now my eye seeth thee.

    ☞R 5
    Hearing is secondhand; seeing collapses litigation into encounter.

    6Therefore I reprehend myself, and do penance in dust and ashes.

    ☞R 6
    Dust and ashes: the body becomes the argument.

    12And the Lord blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning. And he had fourteen thousand sheep, and six thousand camels, and a thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand she-asses.

    13And he had seven sons, and three daughters.

    14And he called the name of one Dies, and the name of the second Cassia, and the name of the third Cornustibii.

    15And there were not found in all the earth women so beautiful as the daughters of Job: and their father gave them inheritance among their brethren.

    ☞R 15
    Beauty noted, then inheritance granted: the text refuses to keep daughters ornamental.

    16-17And Job lived after these things, a hundred and forty years, and he saw his children, and his children's children, unto the fourth generation, and he died an old man, and full of days.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 10:17-24

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 10:17-24 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    17And the seventy-two returned with joy, saying: Lord, the devils also are subject to us in thy name.

    18And he said to them: I saw Satan like lightning falling from heaven.

    ☞G 18
    Victory is narrated as a vision: the fall precedes the mission’s report.

    19Behold, I have given you power to tread upon serpents and scorpions and upon all the power of the enemy: and nothing shall hurt you.

    20But yet rejoice not in this, that spirits are subject unto you: but rejoice in this, that your names are written in heaven.

    ☞G 20
    Power is demoted; registry is promoted.

    21In that same hour, he rejoiced in the Holy Ghost and said: I confess to thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hidden these things from the wise and prudent and hast revealed them to little ones. Yea, Father, for so it hath seemed good in thy sight.

    ☞G 21
    Revelation is not meritocratic; it is a concealment and a gift.

    22All things are delivered to me by my Father. And no one knoweth who the Son is, but the Father: and who the Father is, but the Son and to whom the Son will reveal him.

    ☞G 22
    Knowledge of God is gated by persons, not by techniques.

    23And turning to his disciples, he said: Blessed are the eyes that see the things which you see.

    24For I say to you that many prophets and kings have desired to see the things that you see and have not seen them; and to hear the things that you hear and have not heard them.

    ☞G 24
    Desire of prophets meets its limit: history strains toward what it cannot produce.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s self-revelation, not human competence, is the ground of true knowledge and joy. Creaturely restoration and spiritual authority are subordinate signs of a prior divine initiative that humbles the wise and secures the elect in heaven.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,12,16-17The book closes by shifting the axis from disputation to settlement: Job’s reply ends the courtroom, then the narrative reasserts ordinary time—livestock, children, years. Restoration is not an explanation of suffering but a reinstallation of creaturely life under God’s unanswerable freedom.
    • R 12,13,15The recompense is deliberately excessive and concrete, yet it is not a simple reversal: the text lingers on names and legal standing, suggesting that blessing is not merely quantity but order—household, memory, and inheritance reconstituted after chaos.

    In Evangelium

    • G 17,19,23The mission report tempts a triumphalist reading, but Jesus frames their success inside a larger cosmic defeat and then redirects attention to perception: the blessedness is not efficacy but participation in a disclosed reality.
    • G 21,22The prayer and the saying about mutual knowledge establish the epistemic regime of the kingdom: God is known only by God’s self-disclosure, and that disclosure bypasses the prestige economy. Authority and intimacy are fused in the Son’s prerogative to reveal.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Job’s “repentance” is not moral contrition; it is capitulation before a spectacle. He doesn’t discover his sin, he discovers his smallness. “Now my eye seeth thee” lands like a coerced intimacy: the bullied finally meets the bully in person and calls it revelation. Dust and ashes are less humility than self-erasure, the price of being allowed to live.

    Then the book tries to buy silence with abundance. New herds, new children, exquisite daughters named like perfume and ornament. It reads like replacement, not restoration: grief is not healed, it is covered. The dread is that the universe can be appeased but never explained, and the reward is simply the continuation of breath under an unanswerable power.

    In Luke, the seventy-two taste the same seduction: power over devils, immunity, the thrill of being dangerous. Jesus cuts it off, not because power is false, but because it is intoxicating. “Your names are written in heaven” is the deeper narcotic: not mastery over spirits, but being chosen, registered, kept.

    “I saw Satan fall like lightning” sounds less like victory than a glimpse of how fast the cosmos can flip. What is “hidden from the wise” is not a sweet secret but a destabilizing one: reality belongs to the childlike because they can endure dependence. The wise want reasons; the little ones accept the gaze. Both readings end in the same uneasy desire: to be seen by the One who refuses to explain himself.

    Semina Verbi

    Job’s turn is the psyche surrendering its lawsuit against reality: the intellect stops demanding a courtroom and accepts the vastness that won’t fit inside explanation. That shift from “hearing” to “seeing” is not new data but a new mode of consciousness, the kind that makes self-importance collapse into ash.

    The disciples’ exhilaration at power over spirits is the ego’s first intoxication with efficacy; Jesus redirects them to identity rather than performance. The real miracle is belonging, not mastery. The “little ones” are not the ignorant but the unarmored, the ones whose attention isn’t barricaded by status or technique.

    Semina Verbi: this resembles Zen’s kenshō after the failure of discursive striving, and the Bhagavad Gītā’s movement from anxious agency to surrendered action; speculative, but the psychological contour matches. It also echoes Sufi fanāʾ, where the self’s claims burn away and a quieter joy remains. The warning against rejoicing in power parallels Buddhist cautions about siddhis and Stoic suspicion of external victories: the cleanest joy is inward alignment, not control.
