    IIILectioJob 9:1-12,14-16

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Job

    Job 9:1-12,14-16 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And Job answered, and said:

    2Indeed I know it is so, and that man cannot be justified, compared with God.

    ☞R 2
    Justification collapses when the comparator is God; the scale is the verdict.

    3If he will contend with him, he cannot answer him one for a thousand.

    ☞R 3
    Litigation imagery: the ratio is not unfairness but incommensurability—one question, a thousand silences.

    4He is wise in heart, and mighty in strength: who hath resisted him, and hath had peace?

    5Who hath removed mountains, and they whom he overthrew in his wrath, knew it not.

    6Who shaketh the earth out of her place, and the pillars thereof tremble.

    7Who commandeth the sun, and it riseth not: and shutteth up the stars, as it were, under a seal:

    ☞R 7
    Cosmos as courtroom evidence: sun subpoenaed, stars sealed—creation obeys without explanation.

    8Who alone spreadeth out the heavens, and walketh upon the waves of the sea,

    ☞R 8
    “Walketh upon the waves”: sovereignty rendered as casual gait over chaos.

    9Who maketh Arcturus, and Orion, and Hyades, and the inner parts of the south.

    10Who doth things great and incomprehensible, and wonderful, of which there is no number.

    11If he come to me, I shall not see him: if he depart, I shall not understand.

    ☞R 11
    The terror is not absence but untrackable presence: arrival unseen, departure unintelligible.

    12If he examine on a sudden, who shall answer him? or who can say: Why dost thou so?

    14What am I then, that I should answer him, and have words with him?

    15I, who although I should have any just thing, would not answer, but would make supplication to my judge.

    16And if he should hear me when I call, I should not believe that he had heard my voice.

    ☞R 16
    Even answered prayer is epistemically unstable; hearing does not become certainty.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 9:57-62

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 9:57-62 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    57And it came to pass, as they walked in the way, that a certain man said to him: I will follow thee whithersoever thou goest.

    58Jesus said to him: The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air nests: but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head.

    ☞G 58
    Discipleship offered homelessness: animals have places; the Son of Man refuses the security of a “where.”

    59But he said to another: Follow me. And he said: Lord, suffer me first to go and to bury my father.

    60And Jesus said to him: Let the dead bury their dead: but go thou and preach the kingdom of God.

    ☞G 60
    Kinship inverted: the living are conscripted for proclamation; the dead are assigned the dead.

    61And another said: I will follow thee, Lord; but let me first take my leave of them that are at my house.

    62Jesus said to him: No man putting his hand to the plough and looking back is fit for the kingdom of God.

    ☞G 62
    Agrarian eschatology: the kingdom is a furrow—progress requires a neck that will not swivel.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod is not just; he is Judge in a category beyond contest, and therefore human righteousness cannot litigate him. The kingdom’s call is total: it displaces security, custom, and even sacred duties in favor of unbroken obedience.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,4,5,6,9,10Job’s speech is not a theodicy but a demolition of procedural religion. He stacks cosmic acts—geologic, seismic, astral—not to admire power but to show that God’s governance is not answerable to human categories of due process. The point is jurisdiction: the Creator is not a party in a case but the condition under which any case can be heard.
    • R 12,15The sudden “examine” reframes suffering as inspection without notice; the human role becomes petition rather than cross-examination. Supplication here is not piety but realism: speech survives only as appeal, not as argument that could bind God.

    In Evangelium

    • G 57,59,61Three would-be followers negotiate terms: place, time, and social closure. Each proposal tries to domesticate vocation—attach it to shelter, to filial duty, to household etiquette—so that following becomes an add-on rather than a transfer of allegiance.
    • G 58,60,62Jesus answers by stripping away the normal anchors of identity: no settled location, no priority of burial rites, no backward glance. The kingdom is treated as an absolute claim that reorders obligations; it does not compete with lesser goods but reclassifies them.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Job isn’t humbled; he’s terrorized into erotic awe. God is described like a cosmic mugger, rearranging mountains and sealing up stars, not to teach but to demonstrate that reality is owned. The dread is intimate: if God comes, Job can’t see him; if God leaves, Job can’t track him. Presence and absence both wound. Prayer becomes a gamble so humiliating Job says even if answered he wouldn’t believe it—because the real injury is not silence but arbitrariness.

    Luke’s Jesus carries that same atmosphere into the street. The homelessness isn’t quaint asceticism; it’s a demand to live without refuge, without the animal comforts even foxes get. The refusals are not about virtue but about severing the last cords that make a self: family, mourning, farewells, the right to look back and still be whole. “Let the dead bury their dead” is the voice of a kingdom that treats ordinary love as a sedative. Desire is recruited, then stripped of its alibis, until following becomes a kind of chosen dispossession—an answer to Job’s terror that doesn’t solve it, only joins it.

    Semina Verbi

    Job’s awe before an unanswerable power has kin in the Bhagavad Gita’s vision of Krishna’s vast form, where speech collapses into trembling and consent; speculation: both stage the psyche’s encounter with the inhuman scale of reality that makes self-justification feel absurd. The hiddenness—“if he come to me, I shall not see him”—echoes Daoist reserve in the Daodejing, where the source acts without presenting itself for interrogation; speculation: this is less theology than a discipline against compulsive explanation.

    Luke’s hard summons resembles the Buddhist renunciant cut, leaving household ties to end the round of clinging; speculation: “let the dead bury their dead” reads like a ruthless compassion that refuses to let grief become identity. The plough image matches Stoic prohairesis in Epictetus: once you choose the work, you stop bargaining with the past; speculation: the kingdom here functions like a telos that reorganizes desire so completely that nostalgia becomes sabotage.
