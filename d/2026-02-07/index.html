IIILectio1 Kings 3:4-13

Lectio Prima

A reading from the First Bok of Kings

1 Kings 3:4-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

4He went therefore to Gabaon, to sacrifice there: for that was the great high place: a thousand victims for holocausts, did Solomon offer upon that altar, in Gabaon.

☞R 4
A “high place” for worship: devotion still wearing Canaanite architecture.

5And the Lord appeared to Solomon in a dream by night, saying: Ask what thou wilt that I should give thee.

☞R 5
God’s blank check is a test, not a gift.

6And Solomon said: Thou hast shewed great mercy to thy servant David, my father, even as he walked before thee in truth, and justice, and an upright heart with thee: and thou hast kept thy great mercy for him, and hast given him a son to sit on his throne, as it is this day.

7And now, O Lord God, thou hast made thy servant king instead of David, my father: and I am but a child, and know not how to go out and come in;

☞R 7
“Child” is political speech: weakness confessed to obtain strength.

8And thy servant is in the midst of the people which thou hast chosen, an immense people, which cannot be numbered nor counted for multitude.

9Give therefore to thy servant an understanding heart, to judge thy people, and discern between good and evil. For who shall be able to judge this people, thy people, which is so numerous?

☞R 9
Wisdom defined as discrimination—good/evil, not cleverness.

10And the word was pleasing to the Lord, that Solomon had asked such a thing.

☞R 10
The Lord is “pleased” by the object of desire; prayer is moralized.

11And the Lord said to Solomon: Because thou hast asked this thing, and hast not asked for thyself long life nor riches, nor the lives of thy enemies, but hast asked for thyself wisdom to discern judgment;

12Behold I have done for thee according to thy words, and have given thee a wise and understanding heart, in so much that there hath been no one like thee before thee, nor shall arise after thee.

13Yea, and the things also which thou didst not ask, I have given thee; to wit, riches and glory: so that no one hath been like thee among the kings in all days heretofore.

IVEvangeliumMark 6:30-34

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Mark

Mark 6:30-34 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

30And the apostles coming together unto Jesus, related to him all things that they had done and taught.

31And he said to them: Come apart into a desert place, and rest a little. For there were many coming and going: and they had not so much as time to eat.

☞G 31
The kingdom’s labor produces hunger; mission consumes even meals.

32And going up into a ship, they went into a desert place apart.

33And they saw them going away, and many knew: and they ran flocking thither foot from all the cities, and were there before them.

☞G 33
The crowd outpaces the boat: human need outruns strategy.

34And Jesus going out saw a great multitude: and he had compassion on them, because they were as sheep not having a shepherd, and he began to teach them many things.

☞G 34
Compassion becomes instruction; the remedy begins as teaching, not bread.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod approves the petition that seeks justice for others, and he equips rulers and apostles by giving wisdom and teaching rather than indulgence. True shepherding is measured by ordered judgment and doctrine, not by the multiplication of comforts.

In Lectionem

  • R 6,11,12,13The exchange is covenantal and dynastic: Solomon appeals to Davidic mercy (3), then God interprets the request as a reordering of kingship’s aims (8). The promised “heart” is not private enlightenment but a governing organ; the superlative claim (“none like…,” 9) establishes legitimacy. The added riches/glory (10) expose a paradox: renunciation is rewarded with the very temptations renounced, so the narrative quietly sets up future judgment of how gifts are borne.

In Evangelium

  • G 30,32,34Apostolic report (1) frames ministry as accountable work. The attempted withdrawal (3) is not failure but a contested rhythm: rest is commanded, yet the teacher re-enters the public field and answers with formation (5). The scene defines leadership as shepherding through doctrine—ordering a scattered people by speech before any other provision.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Solomon buys a night’s intimacy with God by burning a thousand bodies. The “wisdom” he asks for isn’t purity; it’s management. He calls himself a child as a tactic, a way to make power look like burden, to make ambition sound like service. The dream is a contract: he requests an “understanding heart,” the organ that can feel and calculate at once, and he gets it—along with the unasked-for spoil. The text pretends this is a reward for restraint, but it reads like a seduction: virtue as a mask that lets desire take everything without admitting hunger.

In Mark, the disciples report their work like exhausted contractors, and Jesus offers a desert not as holiness but as triage: a place where the body can finally eat, where the machine can stop. The crowd ruins it. They sprint to the shoreline, refusing to let the workers become private people again. Jesus’s compassion is not soft; it’s the grim recognition that need is endless and leaderless, and it will invade every attempted silence. So he teaches—because teaching is the only feeding that can scale when the appetite is infinite. The dread underneath both scenes is the same: you can’t rule without sacrifice, and you can’t rest without abandoning someone. Desire for wisdom and desire for solitude are both answered, and both answers come with a multitude attached.

Semina Verbi

Solomon’s request sounds like the Confucian ideal of rulership by cultivated discernment rather than appetite: the junzi who governs by ren and li, where legitimacy is measured by moral perception. It also brushes against Plato’s philosopher-king, where the first political act is an interior reordering toward the Good; the “understanding heart” is a psyche trained to see proportion. Speculation: the dream-audience functions like an inner tribunal, akin to the Upanishadic move from external sacrifice to the subtler ask of buddhi—clarity that adjudicates desire.

The desert withdrawal and the crowd’s pursuit resembles the Buddha’s oscillation between solitude and teaching: renunciation not as escape but as the condition for clean attention. “Sheep without a shepherd” parallels Daoist suspicion of frantic striving: people stream because they are unmoored; the sage’s compassion is not sentiment but orientation, a re-centering. Speculation: the inability “to eat” reads like modern burnout; the teacher insists on a rhythm that protects the mind so it can serve without turning predatory or numb.
