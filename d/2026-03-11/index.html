From the Gospel according to Matthew

Matthew 5:17-19
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

17Do not think that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets. I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. To fulfil... By accomplishing all the figures and prophecies; and perfecting all that was imperfect.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 5,8Moses frames Israel’s legal order as a national distinctiveness intelligible to other nations: statutes, ceremonies, judgments—an integrated regime. The rhetoric is comparative (“what other nation”), arguing that revelation is not merely internal rule but a diplomatic sign, a culture with a legislator who is also a God.
On the Gospel
  • G 17,19Jesus’ thesis is continuity without stasis: fulfillment introduces a criterion for interpreting commandments rather than nullifying them. The warning about teaching makes interpretation a moral act; authority in the kingdom is measured by fidelity enacted and communicated, binding exegesis to practice.

Doctrinal Divine law remains binding in its substance and reaches its completion in Christ; fidelity is proved by doing and teaching, and judged as such.

Heterodox Reading
Deuteronomy sells law as survival technology: do this and you live, forget and you die. “Wisdom” isn’t enlightenment; it’s the crafted image a frightened people needs to project so the nations hesitate before devouring them. God “near” to petitions reads like intimacy, but it’s surveillance made tender: keep yourself, keep your soul, keep the memory inside your children so it can’t escape. The land is the prize and the threat; possession requires a mind that polices itself. Matthew sharpens the vise. “Fulfil” is not liberation but closure: the law’s loose ends are pulled tight around a human life until nothing wiggles free, not a jot, not a tittle. The dread is bureaucratic eternity—heaven and earth can pass, but the rule stays. Desire enters as ambition: greatness in the kingdom comes from performing obedience and teaching it, reproducing the system. The least are not the sinful but the ones who puncture the spell by breaking even the smallest commandment and making others imagine they can breathe.
Semina Verbi
This is law not as bureaucracy but as lived memory: a people becomes itself by rehearsing what it has seen, until it sits in the body as reflex. The psyche here is trained against dissociation; forgetting is not ignorance but self-loss, and teaching the next generation is how the self stays continuous across time. The claim of a “near” God reads like an interiorized intimacy rather than mere geography: presence is measured by answered petition, by responsiveness, by the felt permeability between need and meaning. In that light “fulfil” is not demolition or mere compliance; it is the deepening of form until the smallest stroke carries weight, the way a mature character can’t treat a minor promise as minor. Speculation: this kinship with Dharma in Hindu and Buddhist traditions is strong, where order is both cosmic and ethical and becomes wisdom in public view. Confucian li and yi echo the same social psychology: ritual and righteousness as the grammar that makes a people legible and trustworthy. Islamic sharia, at its best construal, similarly frames law as a path that binds memory, practice, and communal identity under a sense of divine nearness. The Jewish idea of Torah as an ever-present instruction also resonates with the emphasis on transmission as fidelity of the heart, not just the archive.
