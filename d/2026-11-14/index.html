    IIILectio3 John 1:5-8

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Third Letter of John

    3 John 1:5-8 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    5Dearly beloved, thou dost faithfully whatever thou dost for the brethren: and that for strangers,

    ☞R 5
    Fidelity measured in deeds, not sentiment; “brethren” includes “strangers.”

    6Who have given testimony to thy charity in the sight of the church. Whom thou shalt do well to bring forward on their way in a manner worthy of God:

    ☞R 6
    Charity becomes public record; “worthy of God” is the scale, not mere decency.

    7Because, for his name they went out, taking nothing of the Gentiles.

    ☞R 7
    Mission refuses Gentile patronage; the Name will not be subsidized by outsiders.

    8We therefore ought to receive such: that we may be fellow helpers of the truth.

    ☞R 8
    Hospitality is epistemic: to receive them is to take a side in “truth.”

    IVEvangeliumLuke 18:1-8

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 18:1-8 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And he spoke also a parable to them, that we ought always to pray and not to faint,

    2Saying: There was a judge in a certain city, who feared not God nor regarded man.

    ☞G 2
    A judge without vertical or horizontal fear: law severed from worship and shame.

    3And there was a certain widow in that city; and she came to him, saying: Avenge me of my adversary.

    ☞G 3
    The widow has no leverage but speech; justice pursued by repetition.

    4And he would not for a long time. But afterwards he said within himself: Although I fear not God nor regard man,

    5Yet because this widow is troublesome to me, I will avenge her, lest continually coming she weary me.

    ☞G 5
    He grants not from righteousness but from fatigue; justice as nuisance-management.

    6And the Lord said: Hear what the unjust judge saith.

    7And will not God revenge his elect who cry to him day and night? And will he have patience in their regard?

    8I say to you that he will quickly revenge them. But yet the Son of man, when he cometh, shall he find, think you, faith on earth?

    ☞G 8
    The last question cuts: delay is not the only danger—faith may evaporate before vindication arrives.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod binds truth to concrete support of his messengers, and he binds prayer to endurance under delay. Divine vindication is certain; persevering faith is the contested field.

    In Lectionem

    • R 6,8The passage builds a chain of communion: the church’s witness authorizes the traveler, and the host’s reception makes him a co-worker. Support is not philanthropy but incorporation into a single work with a single standard.
    • R 7By refusing outside funding, the mission preserves the gospel’s freedom to accuse and to bless; dependence would domesticate the message. The community therefore bears the material cost of its own speech.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,6,7The parable argues from lesser to greater: if a corrupt official can be moved, divine justice is not in doubt. The real issue is the interval between cry and answer, and what that interval does to the one who prays.
    • G 7,8“Quickly” is set beside the question of faith: vindication is promised, but perseverance is not assumed. The eschatological audit is not of God’s reliability but of human endurance.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    These readings don’t praise purity; they praise leverage.

    In the letter, hospitality is not kindness but recruitment. The “strangers” are professionals of the Name, traveling light so their need can press on the conscience of the faithful. “Taking nothing of the Gentiles” isn’t noble poverty; it’s a strategy: refuse the outsider’s money so the group must fund you, must prove itself by feeding you. Gaius’s charity becomes a test he cannot refuse, because to refuse is to step outside “truth.”

    The parable sharpens the same nerve. The widow doesn’t win because the judge becomes good; she wins because she becomes unbearable. Prayer here is not serene communion; it is relentless pressure applied to a power that does not care. God is introduced through the mask of an unjust official, and the dread is that this is not merely a comparison but a disclosure: the world answers not to virtue but to persistence, to those who won’t go away.

    So “faith” at the end is not belief; it is stamina. The question is whether anyone will still have the appetite to keep knocking when the door feels like it’s staffed by indifference. Desire keeps the whole system moving: the missionaries desire support, the community desires to be counted righteous, the widow desires revenge, and heaven is imagined as a place that can be worn down. The terror is that it works.

    Semina Verbi

    Hospitality to strangers for the sake of the Name has close kin in the Buddhist dāna offered to wandering monastics, where giving is both practical support and participation in a path larger than the giver. It also resembles the Sikh langar, food as a refusal to let devotion become private property, and the Sufi adab of hosting the traveler as a guest of God rather than a consumer.

    The widow’s relentless petition reads like the Zen insistence on returning, again and again, to the cushion despite boredom and refusal, and like Hindu tapas: heat generated by sustained asking when the world stays cold. Speculatively, the “unjust judge” functions like the Taoist image of a non-moral cosmos that still yields to persistence; not because it is righteous, but because reality has seams that steady pressure can open.

    “Fellow helpers of the truth” echoes the bodhisattva vow and the Jewish idea of tikkun olam: you don’t merely admire truth, you bankroll it with your home, your time, your reputation. The final question about faith lands like the Bhagavad Gita’s warning about fatigue in practice and the Islamic theme of sabr: endurance is not sentiment but the capacity to keep showing up when no one applauds.
