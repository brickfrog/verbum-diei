    IIILectioGalatians 3:1-5

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Galatians

    Galatians 3:1-5 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1O senseless Galatians, who hath bewitched you that you should not obey the truth: before whose eyes Jesus Christ hath been set forth, crucified among you?

    ☞R 1
    “Bewitched”: error as enchantment, not mere mistake; the crucified is public, not private.

    2This only would I learn of you: Did you receive the Spirit by the works of the law or by the hearing of faith?

    ☞R 2
    Two economies posed as a single question: law-works versus faith-hearing; reception, not achievement.

    3Are you so foolish that, whereas you began in the Spirit, you would now be made perfect by the flesh?

    ☞R 3
    Perfection by “flesh” is regression disguised as progress; the finish line becomes a treadmill.

    4Have you suffered so great things in vain? If it be yet in vain.

    ☞R 4
    Suffering becomes evidence only if it has an object; “if it be yet in vain” leaves a crack for return.

    5He therefore who giveth to you the Spirit and worketh miracles among you: doth he do it by the works of the law or by the hearing of the faith?

    ☞R 5
    Miracles as audit trail: the giver’s method exposes the recipient’s theory.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 11:5-13

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 11:5-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    5And he said to them: Which of you shall have a friend and shall go to him at midnight and shall say to him: Friend, lend me three loaves,

    ☞G 5
    Midnight prayer: need arrives at the wrong hour; dependence is inconvenient by nature.

    6Because a friend of mine is come off his journey to me and I have not what to set before him.

    7And he from within should answer and say: Trouble me not; the door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed. I cannot rise and give thee.

    ☞G 7
    The closed door is not denial but resistance; domestic order set against sudden charity.

    8Yet if he shall continue knocking, I say to you, although he will not rise and give him because he is his friend; yet, because of his importunity, he will rise and give him as many as he needeth.

    ☞G 8
    Importunity wins without intimacy; persistence substitutes for merit.

    9And I say to you: Ask, and it shall be given you: seek, and you shall find: knock, and it shall be opened to you.

    10For every one that asketh receiveth: and he that seeketh findeth: and to him that knocketh it shall be opened:

    11And which of you, if he ask his father bread, will he give him a stone? Or a fish, will he for a fish give him a serpent?

    ☞G 11
    Stone/serpent: the terror of misrecognition—gift turned counterfeit.

    12Or if he shall ask an egg, will he reach him a scorpion?

    13If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father from heaven give the good Spirit to them that ask him?

    ☞G 13
    “Being evil” is baseline anthropology; the comparison shames doubt and elevates the Father’s giving.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod grants the Spirit by faith’s reception, not by legal performance, and he ordains persistent asking as the creaturely mode of that reception. To seek perfection by flesh is to deny both the cross’s disclosure and the Father’s pledged generosity.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,2,5Paul prosecutes a category mistake: treating the Spirit as a wage paid by legal performance. The cross was “set forth” as the definitive disclosure of God’s action; therefore the community’s present experience of Spirit and power must be interpreted as divine initiative rather than human leverage.
    • R 3,4The argument turns on temporal logic: beginning and completion must belong to the same principle. To switch from Spirit to flesh is to change the kind of story their lives are—turning endurance into wasted expenditure unless they reattach it to the original promise.

    In Evangelium

    • G 9,10The parable is not about persuading a reluctant God but about training desire into speech and motion: ask/seek/knock names a sustained posture, not a single request. The universality (“every one”) frames prayer as a rule of the kingdom rather than an exception for the devout.
    • G 11,12,13Jesus argues from lesser to greater: even compromised human fatherhood tends toward fitting gifts; divine fatherhood cannot be outdone by its own shadow. The climactic gift is not relief but the “good Spirit,” making prayer the appointed means by which God gives God.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s insult is a diagnosis: they’ve traded the raw risk of being seized by something beyond them for the narcotic of technique. “Works of the law” isn’t morality; it’s control, the fantasy that the self can finish what the Spirit began. “Who hath bewitched you” lands like shame because it names a desire they won’t admit: they want the crucified Christ as an image they can manage, not a wound that keeps opening.

    Luke’s midnight scene makes the same point without piety. Prayer is not polite; it is pressure. The door is shut, the house is dark, the friend is irritated, and the asker is desperate enough to keep knocking until love looks like coercion. The gift comes not because the one inside is kind, but because refusal becomes unbearable.

    So “ask, seek, knock” is not a promise of serenity but a permission to be relentless. Even the comparison with fathers is edged: “you, being evil,” still feed your children; goodness here is not purity but instinct. The Spirit is given the way bread is given at midnight—because hunger will not be argued with, and because the only faith that survives is the one that keeps making a nuisance of itself.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s jab at “bewitchment” lands close to Plato’s worry about doxa: the mind can be spellbound by inherited forms and mistake compliance for truth. The pivot is not moral effort but a different mode of knowing, nearer to the Upanishadic contrast between ritual performance and direct insight into what is given (speculation). Luke’s midnight knocking rhymes with Zen’s “great doubt” and relentless koan-pressure: the door yields not to merit but to sustained, naked need. The father who gives good gifts echoes the Bhagavad Gita’s assurance that what is sincerely sought is met, not always as requested but as fitting; and the Sufi insistence that persistence in asking is itself a polishing of desire until it can receive (speculation). The “Spirit” as the gift behind gifts parallels prana/ruach as animating breath across traditions, an inward endowment rather than an external wage.
