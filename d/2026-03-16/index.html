Isaiah 65:17-21
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

17For behold I create new heavens, and a new earth: and the former things shall not be in remembrance, and they shall not come upon the heart.

18But you shall be glad and rejoice for ever in these things, which I create: for behold I create Jerusalem a rejoicing, and the people thereof joy.

19And I will rejoice in Jerusalem, and joy in my people, and the voice of weeping shall no more be heard in her, nor the voice of crying.

20There shall no more be an infant of days there, nor an old man that shall not fill up his days: for the child shall die a hundred years old, and the sinner being a hundred years old shall be accursed.

21And they shall build houses, and inhabit them; and they shall plant vineyards, and eat the fruits of them.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 19God’s own affect is repositioned: divine “rejoicing” is attached to a people, so the end is not mere repair of conditions but a reconstitution of covenantal relation in which sorrow has no public voice.
On the Gospel
  • G 45,46,54The narrative turns on misrecognition: they “receive” him on the basis of festival marvels, yet the evangelist counts the act as a “second miracle,” pressing a shift from notoriety to authority—what matters is not that power occurs, but that it is bound to the speaker’s identity and can be trusted without proximity.
  • G 49,53The father’s trajectory is from demand for presence to reliance on utterance; belief is narrated as expanding outward—first personal, then domestic—making the household the first unit of incorporation into the sign’s meaning.

Doctrinal God’s renewal is not cosmetic but ontological: he re-creates the world by reordering memory, sorrow, and ordinary life. Christ’s word effects what it names, and faith is measured by adherence to that word rather than by witnessed spectacle.

Heterodox Reading
Isaiah’s “new heavens” isn’t a gentle upgrade; it’s a forced amnesia. The former things won’t be remembered because remembering would indict the architect. The promise of no weeping comes with a chill clause: the child can still die, just later, and the “sinner” is branded even at a hundred. Utopia keeps a gallows in the backyard. Houses and vineyards read like therapy after catastrophe, domestic labor as a spell against the return of loss. John stages desire as humiliation. The father begs, and Jesus snaps: you people only believe if dazzled. It’s cruelty with a purpose: to strip the man down to naked need, to make him swallow a word without a scene. “Go” is the real miracle—permission to leave the pleading posture. The healing happens at a distance, like power refusing intimacy, like love that will not come down. Put together, the texts confess the same dread: a world remade by decree and a child saved by command both require submission to an absent authority. Joy is promised, but it’s joy on conditions—forget, obey, build your small life where you’re told, and call it faith when the terror finally loosens its grip.
Semina Verbi
Isaiah’s “new heavens and new earth” is psyche-work: a future that heals by erasing the compulsive replay of injury. It is not mere consolation; it is a reconditioning of memory so grief stops running the body. That resembles the Buddhist move from clinging to a re-made world into non-attachment, and the Stoic reordering of impressions so old harms no longer seize the heart—speculation, but close in emotional mechanics. John’s healing at a distance makes faith a disciplined trust in speech rather than in spectacle. The father’s anxiety demands presence and proof; he is refused the drug of immediacy and given a sentence to carry home. That is remarkably like Sufi tawakkul, reliance that walks on a word, and like the Taoist confidence in efficacy without grasping at control—speculation, but the shared pivot is letting the visible lag behind the true.
