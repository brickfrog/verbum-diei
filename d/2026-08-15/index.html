    IIILectioRevelation 11:19; 12:1-6,10

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Revelation

    Revelation 11:19; 12:1-6,10 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    11:19And the temple of God was opened in heaven: and the ark of his testament was seen in his temple. And there were lightnings and voices and an earthquake and great hail.

    ☞R 11:19
    Heaven’s sanctuary opens; covenant is not lost but exposed. Theophany arrives as weather.

    12:1And a great sign appeared in heaven: A woman clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars. A woman... The church of God. It may also, by allusion, be applied to our blessed Lady. The church is clothed with the sun, that is, with Christ: she hath the moon, that is, the changeable things of the world, under her feet: and the twelve stars with which she is crowned, are the twelve apostles: she is in labour and pain, whilst she brings forth her children, and Christ in them, in the midst of afflictions and persecutions.

    ☞R 12:1
    Cosmic regalia on a woman: light worn, mutability trampled, apostolic number enthroned. Sign, not portrait.

    12:2And being with child, she cried travailing in birth: and was in pain to be delivered.

    12:3And there was seen another sign in heaven. And behold a great red dragon, having seven heads and ten horns and on his heads seven diadems.

    12:4And his tail drew the third part of the stars of heaven and cast them to the earth. And the dragon stood before the woman who was ready to be delivered: that, when she should be delivered, he might devour her son.

    ☞R 12:4
    The predator waits at the threshold of birth: violence aims at the heir, not the mother.

    12:5And she brought forth a man child, who was to rule all nations with an iron rod. And her son was taken up to God and to his throne.

    ☞R 12:5
    The child’s destiny is juridical (“iron rod”); the rescue is vertical—snatched to throne, not spared by truce.

    12:6And the woman fled into the wilderness, where she had a place prepared by God, that there they should feed her, a thousand two hundred sixty days.

    ☞R 12:6
    Wilderness is not abandonment but rationed providence; time is measured, not endless.

    12:10And I heard a loud voice in heaven, saying: Now is come salvation and strength and the kingdom of our God and the power of his Christ: because the accuser of our brethren is cast forth, who accused them before our God day and night.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 1:39-56

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 1:39-56 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    39And Mary rising up in those days, went into the hill country with haste into a city of Juda.

    ☞G 39
    Haste: charity moves faster than decorum. The hill country becomes a liturgical stage.

    40And she entered into the house of Zachary and saluted Elizabeth.

    41And it came to pass that when Elizabeth heard the salutation of Mary, the infant leaped in her womb. And Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Ghost.

    ☞G 41
    A fetus performs recognition; the Spirit makes the unborn a witness.

    42And she cried out with a loud voice and said: Blessed art thou among women and blessed is the fruit of thy womb.

    43And whence is this to me that the mother of my Lord should come to me?

    ☞G 43
    “Mother of my Lord”: the title precedes explanation; theology erupts as greeting.

    44For behold as soon as the voice of thy salutation sounded in my ears, the infant in my womb leaped for joy.

    45And blessed art thou that hast believed, because those things shall be accomplished that were spoken to thee by the Lord.

    46And Mary said: My soul doth magnify the Lord.

    47And my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour.

    48Because he hath regarded the humility of his handmaid: for behold from henceforth all generations shall call me blessed. Shall call me blessed... These words are a prediction of that honour which the church in all ages should pay to the Blessed Virgin. Let Protestants examine whether they are any way concerned in this prophecy.

    ☞G 48
    Blessedness is public and durable—generation after generation, not a private compliment.

    49Because he that is mighty hath done great things to me: and holy is his name.

    50And his mercy is from generation unto generations, to them that fear him.

    51He hath shewed might in his arm: he hath scattered the proud in the conceit of their heart.

    52He hath put down the mighty from their seat and hath exalted the humble.

    53He hath filled the hungry with good things: and the rich he hath sent empty away.

    ☞G 53
    Reversal has an edge: the rich are not improved, they are dismissed empty.

    54He hath received Israel his servant, being mindful of his mercy.

    55As he spoke to our fathers: to Abraham and to his seed for ever.

    56And Mary abode with her about three months. And she returned to her own house.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s kingdom advances by covenant fidelity and decisive judgment: the accuser is expelled, the Messiah enthroned, and the lowly made the public locus of divine action.

    In Lectionem

    • R 12:3,12:10The passage is a courtroom and a battlefield at once: the dragon’s insignia mimics sovereignty, but the decisive act is expulsion of the accuser. Evil is defined less by raw force than by prosecutorial speech—“day and night”—and its defeat is the loss of standing before God.
    • R 12:1,12:2,12:5The woman functions as a corporate figure whose labor is historical: the messianic rule appears, then immediately the narrative jumps to enthronement. The text compresses incarnation, conflict, and exaltation into a single apocalyptic gesture, treating history as the outworking of a decree.

    In Evangelium

    • G 46,50,54,55The Magnificat is not sentiment but a political-theological manifesto: God’s action is narrated as a pattern across generations, anchored in Israel’s covenant memory. Mary speaks as Israel’s voice at the hinge where promise becomes event.
    • G 40,45,56The scene is structured by visitation and return: greeting, recognition, confession, then withdrawal. Faith is presented as the condition for fulfillment, and the narrative closes with ordinary time (“three months”) to insist that the extraordinary enters domestic chronology without dissolving it.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    The temple opens and the ark flashes into view like a forbidden memory surfacing. Heaven is not serene; it convulses. What appears next is not comfort but spectacle: a woman dressed in impossible light, crowned, yet screaming in labor. Glory here is a costume for pain.

    The dragon is not an external monster so much as the world’s appetite, the devouring gaze that waits at the moment of emergence. Birth is immediately met by consumption. The child is snatched upward—less a happy ascension than an evacuation—while the mother is left to run, exiled into the wilderness where “prepared” sounds like managed scarcity. Salvation is announced as a legal victory: the accuser thrown out, the courtroom cleared, but the body still bleeding.

    Luke stages the same dread in domestic quiet. Mary moves “with haste,” not with piety but with urgency, as if she needs a witness before the story turns violent. Elizabeth’s child leaps: desire recognizes desire, two pregnancies vibrating like tuned instruments. Blessing in this scene is contagious, almost erotic in its intensity, yet it is also a social weapon: from now on all generations will call her blessed, meaning her life will be taken from her and turned into a public sentence.

    The Magnificat is not a lullaby; it is revolutionary speech from a girl who has already been drafted into a cosmic struggle. She imagines the mighty thrown down and the rich emptied because she can feel the dragon’s mouth at the edge of her future. Praise becomes the only way to metabolize terror: if you name the world upside down, maybe it won’t swallow you whole.

    Semina Verbi

    The sun-clothed woman and the devouring dragon feel like the psyche’s birth-scene under siege: new life arriving while an old, ravenous power tries to intercept it. That dragon is the inner prosecutor, the voice that turns every emerging vocation into a case against you; its defeat is not sentiment but a change of courtroom, accusation losing jurisdiction.

    Mary’s haste is the body trusting its own yes. The leap in Elizabeth is recognition before argument, a pre-verbal joy that precedes proof; two pregnancies become a shared field where faith is contagious, not private. The Magnificat is a revolutionary song that begins as intimacy and ends as social inversion: pride as self-enclosure, humility as permeability to the real.

    Semina Verbi: the woman crowned with stars echoes Isis with Horus pursued by Set, and Leto birthing Apollo while Python waits; speculation, but the pattern is maternal radiance versus predatory chaos. The wilderness refuge resembles Hagar’s survival and also the Buddhist forest as a protected liminal space where the self is remade. The casting down of the accuser rhymes with the Zoroastrian sense of a cosmic slanderer opposed by truth, and with the Qur’anic motif of Satan as whisperer rather than final judge. The Magnificat’s reversal has kinship with the Daoist praise of the lowly valley and with the Hindu bhakti insistence that grace chooses the small.
