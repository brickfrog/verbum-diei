    IIILectio1 Corinthians 12:31-13:13

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 12:31-13:13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12:31But be zealous for the better gifts. And I shew unto you yet a more excellent way.

    ☞R 12:31
    Gifts are bait; the “way” is the trapdoor under them.

    13:1If I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, and have not charity, I am become as sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal.

    ☞R 13:1
    Angelic speech without love: liturgy reduced to percussion.

    13:2And if I should have prophecy and should know all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I should have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing.

    ☞R 13:2
    Mountain-moving faith can still be a zero.

    13:3And if I should distribute all my goods to feed the poor, and if I should deliver my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing.

    ☞R 13:3
    Almsgiving and martyrdom can be self-investment with no return.

    13:4Charity is patient, is kind: charity envieth not, dealeth not perversely, is not puffed up,

    ☞R 13:4
    Love is described by negations: it is known by what it refuses.

    13:5Is not ambitious, seeketh not her own, is not provoked to anger, thinketh no evil:

    13:6Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth with the truth:

    13:7Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.

    13:8Charity never falleth away: whether prophecies shall be made void or tongues shall cease or knowledge shall be destroyed.

    ☞R 13:8
    Charity outlives the spectacular; the permanent judges the temporary.

    13:9For we know in part: and we prophesy in part.

    13:10But when that which is perfect is come, that which is in part shall be done away.

    13:11When I was a child, I spoke as a child, I understood as a child, I thought as a child. But, when I became a man, I put away the things of a child.

    13:12We see now through a glass in a dark manner: but then face to face. Now I know in part: but then I shall know even as I am known.

    ☞R 13:12
    “Known” precedes knowing; the subject is already read.

    13:13And now there remain faith, hope, and charity, these three: but the greatest of these is charity.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 7:31-35

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 7:31-35 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    31And the Lord said: Whereunto then shall I liken the men of this generation? And to what are they like?

    32They are like to children sitting in the marketplace and speaking one to another and saying: We have piped to you, and you have not danced: we have mourned, and you have not wept.

    ☞G 32
    They demand a controllable Messiah: dance on cue, weep on cue.

    33For John the Baptist came neither eating bread nor drinking wine. And you say: He hath a devil.

    ☞G 33
    Asceticism is pathologized: holiness rebranded as possession.

    34The Son of man is come eating and drinking. And you say: Behold a man that is a glutton and a drinker of wine, a friend of publicans and sinners.

    ☞G 34
    Feasting is criminalized: mercy rebranded as vice.

    35And wisdom is justified by all her children.

    ☞G 35
    Wisdom’s defense is not argument but offspring—results.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisCharity is the eschatological measure that renders every gift, feat, and posture accountable. Christ exposes a generation that judges by mood, while God judges by the fruit of wisdom.

    In Lectionem

    • R 13:9,13:10Paul relocates authority from present competencies to eschatological completion: partial knowledge cannot found ultimacy, so the community’s hierarchy of “better gifts” is relativized by the coming of the perfect.
    • R 13:11,13:13The child/man contrast is not sentiment but epistemic discipline: maturity is measured by what one discards, and the triad that remains is ordered toward a single summit.

    In Evangelium

    • G 31,35Jesus frames the generation as a problem of judgment, not information: they refuse any form that would indict them, and the only vindication offered is the long audit of wisdom’s outcomes.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “more excellent way” reads like an intervention for addicts of greatness. Tongues, prophecy, knowledge, even martyrdom: the whole spiritual achievement economy. He doesn’t deny the rush; he calls it noise. Without charity the self stays intact, and that intact self is the real idol—burning, giving, speaking, dazzling, all to keep the interior throne occupied.

    Charity here is not sweetness; it is ego-death stretched over time. Patient, kind, not ambitious: a portrait of someone refusing the narcotic of being right, admired, or indispensable. “Beareth all things” sounds holy until you hear the dread in it: love as the capacity to remain present when your story of yourself is collapsing.

    Luke’s scene is the same sickness in public. The generation wants religion as a performance keyed to their mood: play the flute, stage the funeral, give us the correct affect. John’s refusal of pleasure is demonized; Jesus’ embrace of pleasure is demonized. The complaint is identical because the desire is identical: control. They don’t want wisdom; they want a god who dances on command.

    So “wisdom is justified by all her children” lands like a cold verdict. Wisdom doesn’t win arguments; it produces lives. Charity is the only “gift” that can’t be used to posture, because the moment you use it that way it turns back into brass and cymbal. The text isn’t consoling; it’s stripping you of every impressive refuge until only the terrifying simplicity remains: love, or nothing.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “more excellent way” reads like a psychological claim: the self can perform brilliance and sacrifice as a kind of armor, but only love dissolves the performer and makes the act real. That aligns with the Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga, where action without grasping at its fruit purifies the agent; with Mahayana Buddhism’s bodhicitta and the bodhisattva vow, where insight and powers are secondary to compassion; and with Sufism’s insistence that ecstatic gifts mean little without adab and the heart’s tenderness.

    “Through a glass, darkly” has a close cousin in Plato’s cave and in the Upanishadic neti neti: cognition is partial, maturity is the slow surrender of childish certainty. Speculation: it also resembles Zen’s distrust of spiritual fireworks—kōan practice as a stripping of cleverness until direct seeing.

    Luke’s children in the marketplace echoes the Tao Te Ching’s refusal to be managed by expectations: the sage won’t dance on command or mourn on cue. It also touches Stoic independence from the crowd’s verdict and the Zen image of “no rank,” where both ascetic severity and convivial ease get misread by the same hungry cynicism.

    “Wisdom is justified by her children” parallels the pragmatic test in Confucianism—virtue known by its fruits in conduct and harmony—and, speculatively, William James’s measure of truth by lived consequences rather than by applause.
