Reading

A reading from the Book of Isaiah

Isaiah 49:1-6
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

1Give ear, ye islands, and hearken, ye people from afar. The Lord hath called me from the womb, from the bowels of my mother he hath been mindful of my name.

2And he hath made my mouth like a sharp sword: in the shadow of his hand he hath protected me, and hath made me as a chosen arrow: in his quiver he hath hidden me.

3And he said to me: Thou art my servant Israel, for in thee will I glory.

4And I said: I have laboured in vain, I have spent my strength without cause and in vain: therefore my judgment is with the Lord, and my work with my God.

5And now saith the Lord, that formed me from the womb to be his servant, that I may bring back Jacob unto him, and Israel will not be gathered together: and I am glorified in the eyes of the Lord, and my God is made my strength.

6And he said: It is a small thing that thou shouldst be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to convert the dregs of Israel. Behold, I have given thee to be the light of the Gentiles, that thou mayst be my salvation even to the farthest part of the earth.

From the Gospel according to John

John 13:21-33,36-38
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

21When Jesus had said these things, he was troubled in spirit; and he testified, and said: Amen, amen, I say to you, one of you shall betray me.

22The disciples therefore looked one upon another, doubting of whom he spoke.

23Now there was leaning on Jesus' bosom one of his disciples, whom Jesus loved.

24Simon Peter therefore beckoned to him and said to him: Who is it of whom he speaketh?

25He therefore, leaning on the breast of Jesus, saith to him: Lord, who is it?

26Jesus answered: He it is to whom I shall reach bread dipped. And when he had dipped the bread, he gave it to Judas Iscariot, the son of Simon.

27And after the morsel, Satan entered into him. And Jesus said to him: That which thou dost, do quickly. That which thou dost, do quickly... It is not a license, much less a command, to go about his treason: but a signification to him that Christ would not hinder or resist what he was about, do it as soon as he pleased: but was both ready and desirous to suffer for our redemption.

28Now no man at the table knew to what purpose he said this unto him.

29For some thought, because Judas had the purse, that Jesus had said to him: Buy those things which we have need of for the festival day: or that he should give something to the poor.

30He therefore, having received the morsel, went out immediately. And it was night.

31When he therefore was gone out, Jesus said: Now is the Son of man glorified; and God is glorified in him.

32If God be glorified in him, God also will glorify him in himself: and immediately will he glorify him.

33Little children, yet a little while I am with you. You shall seek me. And as I said to the Jews: Whither I go you cannot come; so I say to you now.

36Simon Peter saith to him: Lord, whither goest thou? Jesus answered: Whither I go, thou canst not follow me now: but thou shalt follow hereafter.

37Peter saith to him: Why cannot I follow thee now? I will lay down my life for thee.

38Jesus answered him: Wilt thou lay down thy life for me? Amen, amen, I say to thee, the cock shall not crow, till thou deny me thrice.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 3,5The servant is named “Israel” yet tasked to recover Israel; the figure concentrates the people into one representative who bears their vocation and their failure. The paradox (“Israel will not be gathered”) places success in divine glorification rather than communal response, reframing mission as God’s self-display through a contested instrument.
On the Gospel
  • G 22,28,29,31,32,33The narrative stages a controlled disclosure: the community cannot read the sign, misinterprets motives, and so remains blind while the plot advances. Only after separation does Jesus interpret events as “glory,” relocating meaning from betrayal’s psychology to the Father-Son economy; the address “little children” marks a new pedagogical moment formed by imminent absence.

Doctrinal God’s glory advances through a servant whose mission exceeds Israel and through a Messiah who neither is surprised by betrayal nor derailed by it. Salvation is enacted as deliberate divine self-manifestation in and through human failure.

Heterodox Reading
Isaiah’s servant is not a hero but a weapon that has been kept in the dark too long. Called from the womb, named before consent, he is honed into a mouth that cuts, an arrow that waits. His first honest speech is exhaustion: I have laboured in vain. The grand vocation feels like wasted life, a performance for a glory that isn’t his. Then the task expands obscenely—Israel isn’t even gathered, so he’s pushed outward to strangers, a light for people who never asked for him. Salvation here is not comfort; it is overexposure, the self drafted into a project too large to be lived without resentment. John stages the same dread at a table. Jesus is “troubled in spirit” because he knows love cannot be kept clean. The sign of intimacy is a dipped morsel placed directly into Judas, and the result is possession: Satan enters. Betrayal is not outside the circle; it is fed by the hand that blesses. “Do quickly” is not strategy but desire for the wound to open, for the suspense to end. Night falls the moment Judas leaves because the community needs someone to carry its forbidden will. Peter’s devotion is the other face of betrayal. He wants to follow now, to turn love into spectacle, to make the story about his courage. Jesus refuses him not as a moralist but as a diagnostician: you cannot bear what you claim to want. The cockcrow is the sound of the self snapping back into survival. These readings press one insight: being chosen means being used, and the ache underneath holiness is the craving to be free of the role—even if freedom arrives only through failure.
Semina Verbi
The “called from the womb” voice has kinship with the Hindu sense of svadharma: a life-task that precedes choice and still burns through disappointment; the “sharp sword” mouth matches the Bhagavad Gita’s unflinching speech that cuts illusion (speculation). “Hidden in the quiver” feels like Taoist wu-wei: power kept sheathed, timed, conserved, acting by season rather than impulse (speculation). “Light to the Gentiles” resonates with Mahayana bodhisattva vows to turn outward beyond one’s own clan and toward all beings, not by conquest but by radiance; the widening of mission after felt futility also echoes the Zen pattern where failure breaks the small self and opens the larger one (speculation). The dipped bread handed to the betrayer has a parallel in Sufi readings of intimacy as ordeal: nearness intensifies what is already in the heart, so love can become either surrender or revolt; Judas is not tempted from outside so much as disclosed from within (speculation). “It was night” aligns with the Dark Night in St. John of the Cross and with the Buddhist image of ignorance as darkness, where the decisive act is made under diminished sight. Peter’s vow and collapse resemble the Stoic discovery that intention is not yet virtue; willpower breaks at the first real pressure, and only training makes steadiness. The threefold denial recalls the three poisons in Buddhism—grasping, aversion, delusion—cycling through the same fear in different masks (speculation).
