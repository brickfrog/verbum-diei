Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    SextAd Sextam12:00

Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    IIILectioJeremiah 13:1-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

    Jeremiah 13:1-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Thus saith the Lord to me: Go, and get thee a linen girdle, and thou shalt put it about thy loins, and shalt not put it into water.

    ☞R 1
    Linen: priestly fabric; command includes a prohibition—no cleansing, no reset.

    2And I got a girdle according to the word of the Lord, and put it about my loins.

    3And the word of the Lord came to me the second time, saying:

    4Take the girdle which thou hast got, which is about thy loins, and arise, go to the Euphrates, and hide it there in a hole of the rock.

    ☞R 4
    Euphrates: border-water, exile-water; intimacy buried at the empire’s edge.

    5And I went, and hid it by the Euphrates, as the Lord had commanded me.

    6And it came to pass after many days, that the Lord said to me: Arise, go to the Euphrates, and take from thence the girdle, which I commanded thee to hide there.

    7And I went to the Euphrates, and digged, and took the girdle out of the place where I had hid it and behold the girdle was rotten, so that it was fit for no use.

    ☞R 7
    Rot is time made visible; what was worn becomes evidence.

    8And the word of the Lord came to me, saying:

    9Thus saith the Lord: After this manner will I make the pride of Juda, and the great pride of Jerusalem to rot.

    10This wicked people, that will not hear my words, and that walk in the perverseness of their heart, and have gone after strange gods to serve them, and to adore them: and they shall be as this girdle, which is fit for no use.

    11For as the girdle sticketh close to the loins of a man, so have I brought close to me all the house of Israel, and all the house of Juda, saith the Lord: that they might be my people, and for a name, and for a praise, and for a glory: but they would not hear.

    ☞R 11
    Covenant figured as clothing: closeness meant for display, not concealment; refusal turns glory into waste.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 13:31-35

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 13:31-35 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    31Another parable he proposed unto them, saying: The kingdom of heaven is like to a grain of mustard seed, which a man took and sowed in his field.

    ☞G 31
    Kingdom begins as a unit of near-nothing; sown, not engineered.

    32Which is the least indeed of all seeds; but when it is grown up, it is greater than all herbs, and becometh a tree, so that the birds of the air come, and dwell in the branches thereof.

    ☞G 32
    Herb becomes “tree”: deliberate disproportion; shelter arrives as a side-effect.

    33Another parable he spoke to them: The kingdom of heaven is like to leaven, which a woman took and hid in three measures of meal, until the whole was leavened.

    ☞G 33
    Leaven works by hiding; the agent is small, the outcome total.

    34All these things Jesus spoke in parables to the multitudes: and without parables he did not speak to them.

    35That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet, saying: I will open my mouth in parables, I will utter things hidden from the foundation of the world.

    ☞G 35
    “Hidden from the foundation”: parable is not decoration but disclosure of an older secrecy.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod binds a people to himself for public glory; refusal of his word turns that bond into corruption. The kingdom advances by hidden, disproportionate power that remakes the whole and is disclosed only to those who truly hear.

    In Lectionem

    • R 9,10The sign-act is a lawsuit in fabric: pride is treated as a material that can decay, and idolatry is framed as functional failure—unusable for its intended purpose. The judgment is not annihilation but degradation: a people meant to signify becomes incapable of signifying.

    In Evangelium

    • G 34The parables establish a regime of indirect speech: the kingdom is not delivered as a program but as a riddle that forces hearing to become participation. The medium enacts the message—growth and permeation occur without spectacle, and comprehension itself becomes a kind of fermentation.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Jeremiah stages intimacy as coercion. The girdle is underwear, a private band meant to hold the body together, and God claims it as his own: pressed to the loins, warmed by sweat, made a badge of belonging. Then he orders it exiled to the Euphrates, the borderland of empire and fantasy, and left there to fester. The rot is not just punishment; it is the predictable outcome of being claimed too tightly, then abandoned too far. Pride decays because the relationship is designed to produce decay: closeness turned into surveillance, distance turned into shame.

    Matthew answers with a quieter dread. The kingdom is not a clean monument but an infection: a speck that becomes a tree big enough for birds—those airborne opportunists—to roost, and leaven hidden in flour until everything is altered. Growth here is covert takeover. The “hidden from the foundation” is not comfort but the admission that the real work has always been subterranean, changing texture, scent, and taste while you think you’re still yourself.

    Put together, the texts confess a desire that cannot tolerate consent. One voice wants a people stuck to its body; the other describes a reign that slips inside and remakes you. Either way, the self you were—useful, intact, proud—doesn’t survive. The only question is whether you rot in the rock or rise, swollen and strange, like dough that no longer remembers it was flour.

    Semina Verbi

    The ruined linen reads like a psyche that refused intimacy with its own source. What was meant to cling becomes a prop for pride; cut off from the living current, it decays into performative identity, “fit for no use.” The mustard seed and the leaven answer with the opposite movement: the real work is small, hidden, patient, and it changes the whole from inside, not by display.

    In Taoism, the mustard logic resembles wu-wei: the least, unforced beginning that nonetheless outgrows the loud. In Zen, the leaven is like silent practice working through the dough of habit until the self is altered without spectacle. In Sufism, the spoiled girdle echoes the nafs swelling with self-regard until it rots; the leaven parallels dhikr as an unseen fermentation of the heart. Speculation: in Jungian terms, the girdle is a persona meant to bind life to meaning; when it serves inflation, it decomposes, while the seed and leaven image individuation as slow, subterranean integration.
