IIILectioJeremiah 11:18-20

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

Jeremiah 11:18-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

18But thou, O Lord, hast shewn me, and I have known: then thou shewedst me their doings.

☞R 18
Revelation is forensic: God shows; the prophet “knows” because evidence is disclosed.

19And I was as a meek lamb, that is carried to be a victim: and I knew not that they had devised counsels against me, saying: Let us put wood on his bread, and cut him off from the land of the living, and let his name be remembered no more.

☞R 19
“Meek lamb” meets bureaucratic murder: strategy, not passion; innocence is logistical.

20But thou, O Lord of Sabaoth, who judgest justly, and triest the reins and the hearts, let me see thy revenge on them: for to thee have I revealed my cause. Sabaoth... That is, of hosts or armies, a name frequently given to God in the scriptures.-Ibid. Thy revenge... This was rather a prediction of what was to happen, with an approbation of the divine justice, than an imprecation.

☞R 20
“Reins and hearts”: interior organs as courtroom exhibits; nothing private remains private to judgment.

IVEvangeliumJohn 7:40-53

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to John

John 7:40-53 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

40Of that multitude therefore, when they had heard these words of his, some said: This is the prophet indeed.

41Others said: This is the Christ. But some said: Doth the Christ come out of Galilee?

☞G 41
Messiah reduced to postcode: the scandal is geography, not doctrine.

42Doth not the scripture say: That Christ cometh of the seed of David and from Bethlehem the town where David was?

43So there arose a dissension among the people because of him.

☞G 43
Dissension is the immediate sign: his words do not harmonize a crowd; they split it.

44And some of them would have apprehended him: but no man laid hands upon him.

☞G 44
Desire to seize, inability to seize: power meets an unseen restraint.

45The ministers therefore came to the chief priests and the Pharisees. And they said to them: Why have you not brought him?

46The ministers answered: Never did man speak like this man.

☞G 46
Style becomes testimony: speech itself functions as evidence when credentials are contested.

47The Pharisees therefore answered them: Are you also seduced?

48Hath any one of the rulers believed in him, or of the Pharisees?

49But this multitude, that knoweth not the law, are accursed.

☞G 49
Contempt masquerades as theology: “accursed” is a shortcut around hearing.

50Nicodemus said to them (he that came to him by night, who was one of them):

51Doth our law judge any man, unless it first hear him and know what he doth?

☞G 51
Minimal justice proposed: hear first—law invoked against the legalists.

52They answered and said to him: Art thou also a Galilean? Search the scriptures, and see that out of Galilee a prophet riseth not.

53And every man returned to his own house.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s judgment pierces hidden counsel, and human courts, when they refuse to hear, condemn themselves. Christ provokes division because truth cannot be processed by prejudice without breaking it.

In Lectionem

  • R 19,20Jeremiah’s complaint runs on two axes: targeted destruction of the person and the attempted cancellation of the prophet’s future voice. The appeal to the Lord of hosts frames the conflict as asymmetrical—private plots answered by a public tribunal whose reach is inward and whose verdict cannot be evaded by killing the witness.

In Evangelium

  • G 40,42,45,47,48,52John stages a juridical theatre: crowds render competing identifications; officials demand an arrest; subordinates return with an unintentionally damning acquittal. The authorities’ argument is not exegesis but credentialism—appeal to rank (“rulers”), locality (“Galilee”), and group purity—while Nicodemus introduces procedure, exposing that their certainty depends on refusing the very hearing they claim the law requires.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Jeremiah’s “meek lamb” is not innocence; it’s the cultivated posture of someone who knows he is being watched and wants the watching to curdle into guilt. “Wood on his bread” is intimate sabotage, violence smuggled into nourishment, the conspiracy entering through the mouth. He is “shown” their doings and that revelation is erotic in a bleak way: to be hated is to be confirmed, to be singled out. His prayer for revenge is the moment he admits the desire beneath the prophecy: he doesn’t want merely to survive them; he wants to see them break, to watch the world agree with his wound.

John’s crowd is the same mechanism in daylight. They don’t hear a person; they hear an origin story and panic when it won’t settle. Galilee versus Bethlehem is a bureaucratic obsession that keeps desire at a safe distance. The officers fail to arrest because language has already arrested them: “Never did man speak like this man.” The Pharisees call it seduction because they feel it as seduction; authority is jealous of what it can’t command.

Nicodemus tries to insert procedure, the last refuge of someone who wants to postpone a choice. The answer is a sneer, not an argument: identity-policing as moral certainty. Then everyone goes home. That ending is the coldest line in the passage: the drama drains away into domesticity, leaving the targeted figure outside the houses, outside the law, outside the warm lie of normal life. The dread is that nothing needs to happen for violence to be agreed upon; it is enough that people prefer their own house to the risk of listening.

Semina Verbi

Jeremiah’s “wood on his bread” reads like the psyche’s horror at contamination: nourishment turned weapon, intimacy turned ambush. That image has the same dark charge as the Greek pharmakon—medicine that is also poison—and as the Vedic sense that soma can be blessing or ruin depending on who handles it. Speculation: it also brushes the Zen theme of being “cooked” by circumstance, where the very ordinary meal becomes the site of injury or awakening.

John’s scene is group psychology under pressure: status guarding, credentialism, and the weaponized appeal to expertise (“rulers,” “law”) against lived speech that disarms the enforcers. Nicodemus embodies procedural conscience inside an anxious institution; he resembles the Confucian remonstrator who insists on due hearing, and the Buddhist ideal of right view that refuses verdict without direct seeing. Speculation: the line “never did man speak like this man” echoes the Sufi conviction that certain speech carries an immediacy that outruns legalism without despising it.
