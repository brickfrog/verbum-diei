A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

Jeremiah 20:10-13
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

10For I heard the reproaches of many, and terror on every side: Persecute him, and let us persecute him: from all the men that were my familiars, and continued at my side: if by any means he may be deceived, and we may prevail against him, and be revenged on him.

11But the Lord is with me as a strong warrior: therefore they that persecute me shall fall, and shall be weak: they shall be greatly confounded, because they have not understood the everlasting reproach, which never shall be effaced.

12And thou, O Lord of hosts, prover of the just, who seest the reins and the heart: let me see, I beseech thee, thy vengeance on them: for to thee I have laid open my cause. Let me see, etc... This prayer proceeded not from hatred or ill will, but zeal of justice.

13Sing ye to the Lord, praise the Lord: because he hath delivered the soul of the poor out of the hand of the wicked.

From the Gospel according to John

John 10:31-42
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

31The Jews then took up stones to stone him.

32Jesus answered them: Many good works I have shewed you from my Father. For which of those works do you stone me?

33The Jews answered him: For a good work we stone thee not, but for blasphemy: and because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God.

40And he went again beyond the Jordan, into that place where John was baptizing first. And there he abode.

41And many resorted to him: and they said: John indeed did no sign.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 13The passage pivots from surveillance and threat to liturgy: deliverance is declared publicly, not merely endured privately.
  • R 10,11Jeremiah’s conflict is a contest of interpretation: enemies presume he can be “deceived,” while the Lord’s presence exposes their misreading and makes their victory structurally impossible.
On the Gospel
  • G 31,33,36The scene is a juridical collision: they prosecute a man for claiming divinity, and he reframes the charge in terms of consecration and mission—identity grounded in the Father’s act, not self-assertion.
  • G 40,41,42Withdrawal beyond the Jordan is strategic: the narrative returns to John’s original site so testimony, not spectacle, becomes the basis of faith; the absence of “sign” highlights the sufficiency of truthful witness.

Doctrinal God’s vindication is not a mood but a judgment enacted in history. Christ’s claim stands or falls with the Father’s works and word, and those works expose unbelief as willful, not evidential.

Heterodox Reading
Jeremiah isn’t a saint serenely persecuted; he’s a man addicted to being right. He hears “terror on every side” and it thrills him, because it proves he matters. The betrayal of “familiars” is the real wound: intimacy curdled into surveillance. God as “strong warrior” is less comfort than borrowed muscle, a way to transmute humiliation into victory. And the rawest line is the request to watch vengeance happen. He wants the world to hurt back, and calls it justice to keep his self-image clean. John’s scene is the same drama in a cooler key. The crowd isn’t outraged by goodness; they’re outraged by the collapse of distance. “Being a man, makest thyself God” is the panic of ordinary people hearing their hierarchy dissolve. Jesus replies by widening the scandal: if scripture can name humans “gods,” then the charge is not impiety but envy. He doesn’t retreat into innocence; he presses the wound, making belief hinge on works rather than permission. Then the strangest move: he escapes. Not triumphant martyrdom, but tactical disappearance. Desire remains unsatisfied—no final clash, no cathartic verdict—only retreat beyond the Jordan, back to the place of beginnings, where testimony without spectacle is enough. The faithful gather there not because certainty has won, but because the tension has: a man who refuses both capture and closure, leaving his opponents with stones in their hands and no body to end the story.
Semina Verbi
Jeremiah’s “terror on every side” is the social psychology of scapegoating: the circle tightens, intimates turn informant, and the victim becomes the community’s disinfectant. You can hear René Girard’s mimetic crisis (speculation), but also the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence that action be stripped of personal rancor: vengeance is framed as impersonal justice rather than private satisfaction, like karma returning through a lawful order rather than a bruised ego. John’s scene turns on a familiar riddle across traditions: the human being accused of overreaching into the divine, then defended by pointing to a deeper anthropology. The “you are gods” move resembles the Upanishadic shock that Atman is not-other than Brahman (speculation), and it rhymes with Sufi language of annihilation and subsistence, where “I” thins until only the Real acts. The appeal to “believe the works” also echoes Zen’s distrust of metaphysical argument: don’t debate the moon, watch the hand that actually heals.
