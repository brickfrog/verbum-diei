From the Gospel according to Matthew

Matthew 7:7-12
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

7Ask, and it shall be given you: seek, and you shall find: knock, and it shall be opened to you.

8For every one that asketh, receiveth: and he that seeketh, findeth: and to him that knocketh, it shall be opened.

9Or what man is there among you, of whom if his son shall ask bread, will he reach him a stone?

10Or if he shall ask him a fish, will he reach him a serpent?

11If you then being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children: how much more will your Father who is in heaven, give good things to them that ask him?

12All things therefore whatsoever you would that men should do to you, do you also to them. For this is the law and the prophets.

On the Gospel
  • G 7,11,12The passage binds prayer and ethics: confident asking (1,5) is inseparable from a commanded pattern of conduct (6), so piety that does not issue in just treatment is internally inconsistent.

Doctrinal God’s fatherly generosity is the ground of bold prayer and the measure of human duty. Those who seek gifts from heaven are bound to become givers on earth by the same rule.

Heterodox Reading
This isn’t a promise of cosmic customer service. It’s a training in appetite. Ask, seek, knock: verbs of lack, not of virtue. The point is to keep desire moving, to keep the self from sealing shut. The reward is not the thing you want; it’s the disciplined restlessness that keeps you alive. The father image is not comfort but accusation. You are “evil,” yet you can still manage bread over stone, fish over serpent. The heaven of this passage is a mirror held to your failures: if even your compromised love can avoid cruelty, what excuse do you have for the small sadisms you call realism? The Golden Rule lands like a trapdoor. After all the talk of getting, you’re told the only law is giving what you crave. Your prayers are exposed as rehearsals for how you treat others. Knock long enough and the door that opens is your own obligation.
Semina Verbi
This is a psychology of desire trained into trust. The self is told to keep moving—ask, seek, knock—so longing doesn’t curdle into bitterness. The father-image works as a corrective to the inner cynic: the world is not an adversary handing stones for bread. The “evil” line refuses sentimentality; it banks the argument on ordinary, compromised human decency, then stretches it toward a greater mercy. The Golden Rule grounds the whole movement in reciprocity: prayer without ethical mirroring becomes fantasy, ethics without inward petition becomes grit. Desire is validated, but disciplined by empathy—what you want back is what you must send out. Semina Verbi (speculative): Judaism’s Hillel compresses the ethic into refusing harm to the other; Confucian shu frames it as measured reciprocity; the Mahayana bodhisattva vow extends it as compassion that acts before being asked; Sufism’s tawakkul reads like radical trust after effort; Stoic oikeiosis and Seneca’s counsel to treat others as fellow-citizens echo the same interior expansion from self-concern to kinship.
