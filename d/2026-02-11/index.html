IIILectio1 Kings 10:1-10

Lectio Prima

A reading from the First Book of Kings

1 Kings 10:1-10 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1And the queen of Saba having heard of the fame of Solomon in the name of the Lord, came to try him with hard questions.

☞R 1
Fame “in the name of the LORD”: renown is treated as a theological fact, not PR.

2And entering into Jerusalem with a great train, and riches, and camels that carried spices, and an immense quantity of gold, and precious stones, she came to king Solomon, and spoke to him all that she had in her heart.

☞R 2
She brings caravan-wealth and also “all that she had in her heart”: tribute and testimony yoked.

3And Solomon informed her of all the things she proposed to him: there was not any word the king was ignorant of, and which he could not answer her.

☞R 3
Omniscience by proxy: the king is “ignorant of” nothing asked—wisdom as total coverage.

4And when the queen of Saba saw all the wisdom of Solomon, and the house which he had built,

5And the meat of his table, and the apartments of his servants, and the order of his ministers, and their apparel, and the cupbearers, and the holocausts, which he offered in the house of the Lord, she had no longer any spirit in her;

☞R 5
The inventory culminates in worship (“holocausts”): liturgy is part of the argument. Awe becomes collapse: “no longer any spirit.”

6And she said to the king: The report is true, which I heard in my own country,

7Concerning thy words, and concerning thy wisdom. And I did not believe them that told me, till I came myself, and saw with my own eyes, and have found that the half hath not been told me: thy wisdom and thy works exceed the fame which I heard.

☞R 7
Empiricism converts hearsay: the half-report motif shames distance and secondhand religion.

8Blessed are thy men, and blessed are thy servants, who stand before thee always, and hear thy wisdom.

9Blessed be the Lord thy God, whom thou hast pleased, and who hath set thee upon the throne of Israel, because the Lord hath loved Israel for ever, and hath appointed thee king, to do judgment and justice.

☞R 9
Her blessing maps politics to ethics: throne justified only by “judgment and justice.”

10And she gave the king a hundred and twenty talents of gold, and of spices a very great store, and precious stones: there was brought no more such abundance of spices as these which the queen of Saba gave to king Solomon.

☞R 10
Gifts answer splendor with surplus; the spice note is excess quantified—astonishment turned into offering.

IVEvangeliumMark 7:14-23

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Mark

Mark 7:14-23 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

14And calling again the multitude unto him, he said to them: Hear ye me all and understand.

15There is nothing from without a man that entering into him can defile him. But the things which come from a man, those are they that defile a man.

☞G 15
Defilement reversed: outside does not stain; the self is the source of its own uncleanness.

16If any man have ears to hear, let him hear.

17And when he was come into the house from the multitude, his disciples asked him the parable.

18And he saith to them: So are you also without knowledge? Understand you not that every thing from without entering into a man cannot defile him:

19Because it entereth not into his heart but goeth into his belly and goeth out into the privy, purging all meats?

☞G 19
Blunt physiology (“privy”) demystifies taboo: purity rules are reduced to digestion.

20But he said that the things which come out from a man, they defile a man.

21For from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts, adulteries, fornications, murders,

☞G 21
The heart is a factory: interiority produces acts before acts appear.

22Thefts, covetousness, wickedness, deceit, lasciviousness, an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, foolishness.

☞G 22
Catalogue theology: sin named in plural, social and private; “evil eye” makes envy visible.

23All these evil things come from within and defile a man.

☞G 23
Verdict repeated: “from within” is both origin and accusation—no scapegoat in food or contact.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s gifts can be publicly legible as ordered wisdom, but impurity is not imported; it is generated by the heart and therefore judged at the source.

In Lectionem

  • R 4,6,8The scene is international verification of Israel’s order: architecture, administration, and speech cohere into a single public witness. The foreign queen’s confession functions as external accreditation—Israel’s God is confessed by an outsider precisely through Israel’s visible coherence.

In Evangelium

  • G 14,17,18Jesus splits audience and disciples to expose a literacy gap: public aphorism, then private interrogation, then rebuke. The teaching relocates moral gravity from boundary-maintenance to interior governance, collapsing the authority of tradition when it mistakes ritual perimeter for moral center.

Lectio Heterodoxa

The Queen of Saba comes with “hard questions,” but the text keeps slipping from inquiry into appetite. She arrives as a convoy of scent and weight, and when she finally “spoke to him all that she had in her heart,” Solomon answers everything, not like a sage but like a perfect mirror. What undoes her is not truth but total coherence: the house, the table, the uniforms, the choreography, even the holocausts. Order so complete it steals her breath. She has “no longer any spirit in her” because the system leaves no crack for her own interior to stay hers.

She blesses his God and pays him, but it reads like tribute to the spell, not conversion. The spices are the giveaway: desire packaged as piety, intimacy converted into commodity. Solomon’s wisdom is a machine that can digest whatever a foreign heart confesses and return it as admiration.

Jesus cuts against that spectacle. He drags defilement away from foods and rituals and locates it where Solomon’s empire dares not look: the heart that manufactures its own sewage. Nothing “without” can stain you, he says, because the real contamination is the private production line of thoughts, lusts, greed, and the “evil eye”—envy’s hungry stare. The queen is dazzled by a holiness you can see and smell; Jesus insists the dread is invisible, the rot is domestic, and the enemy is not the strange thing you take in but the familiar thing that comes out of you.

Semina Verbi

The queen arrives as an intelligent outsider who refuses secondhand awe. She tests, watches, then loses her breath—not from mysticism but from coherence: speech, architecture, ritual, labor, and justice all fit together. Admiration here is psychological surrender to integration; she meets a mind whose inner order has successfully turned into public order.

Mark’s diagnosis turns the same lens inward. The real impurity is not contagion but emission; what stains you is what your heart manufactures. The scene is an argument against scapegoating the world for one’s own interior disarray. External splendor can impress, but the true court is inside, and its ministers are thoughts.

Speculation: this convergence echoes Stoic ethics where virtue is the only real good and vice is an inner failure, not a thing caught. It also resembles Buddhist emphasis on mind as the source of suffering and harm, and the Upanishadic turn from outer ritual to inner reality. In Sufism, the polished heart as mirror fits the same intuition: what you see and do flows from what you are.
