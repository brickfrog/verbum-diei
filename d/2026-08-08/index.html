    IIILectioHabakkuk 1:12-2:4

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Habakkuk

    Habakkuk 1:12-2:4 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1:12Wast thou not from the beginning, O Lord my God, my holy one, and we shall not die? Lord, thou hast appointed him for judgment: and made him strong for correction.

    ☞R 1:12
    Invocation as argument: holiness leveraged against death.

    1:13Thy eyes are too pure to behold evil, and thou canst not look on iniquity. Why lookest thou upon them that do unjust things, and holdest thy peace when the wicked devoureth the man that is more just than himself?

    ☞R 1:13
    Purity posed as a problem: the scandal of divine sight.

    1:14And thou wilt make men as the fishes of the sea, and as the creeping things that have no ruler.

    ☞R 1:14
    Dehumanization: men reduced to prey because rule is absent.

    1:15He lifted up all them with his hook, he drew them in his drag, and gathered them into his net: for this he will be glad and rejoice.

    ☞R 1:15
    Empire as fisherman: violence rendered as technique.

    1:16Therefore will he offer victims to his drag, and he will sacrifice to his net: because through them his portion is made fat, and his meat dainty.

    ☞R 1:16
    Idolatry of means: the net becomes god because it works.

    1:17For this cause therefore he spreadeth his net, and will not spare continually to slay the nations.

    2:1I will stand upon my watch, and fix my foot upon the tower: and I will watch, to see what will be said to me, and what I may answer to him that reproveth me. Will stand, etc... Waiting to see what the Lord will answer to my complaint, viz., that the Chaldeans, who are worse than the Jews, and who attribute all their success to their own strength, or to their idols, should nevertheless prevail over the people of the Lord. The Lord's answer is, that the prophet must wait with patience and faith: that all should be set right in due time; and the enemies of God and his people punished according to their deserts.

    2:2And the Lord answered me, and said: Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables: that he that readeth it may run over it.

    2:3For as yet the vision is far off, and it shall appear at the end, and shall not lie: if it make any delay, wait for it: for it shall surely come, and it shall not be slack.

    ☞R 2:3
    Delay without slack: time as discipline, not defect.

    2:4Behold, he that is unbelieving, his soul shall not be right in himself: but the just shall live in his faith.

    ☞R 2:4
    Two interiors: crooked soul vs. faith that sustains life.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 17:14-20

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 17:14-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    14And when he was come to the multitude, there came to him a man falling down on his knees before him saying: Lord, have pity on my son, for he is a lunatic, and suffereth much: for he falleth often into the fire, and often into the water.

    ☞G 14
    Fire and water: the child oscillates between opposite deaths.

    15And I brought him to thy disciples, and they could not cure him.

    ☞G 15
    Apostolic impotence exposed in public.

    16Then Jesus answered and said: O unbelieving and perverse generation, how long shall I be with you? How long shall I suffer you? Bring him hither to me.

    ☞G 16
    Christ’s impatience: proximity does not equal receptivity.

    17And Jesus rebuked him, and the devil went out of him, and the child was cured from that hour.

    ☞G 17
    Rebuke as exorcistic speech-act; cure is immediate.

    18Then came the disciples to Jesus secretly, and said: Why could not we cast him out?

    19Jesus said to them: Because of your unbelief. For, amen I say to you, if you have faith as a grain of mustard seed, you shall say to this mountain: Remove from hence hither, and it shall remove: and nothing shall be impossible to you. As a grain of mustard seed... That is, a perfect faith; which in its properties, and its fruits, resembles the grain of mustard seed, in the parable, chap. 13. 31.

    ☞G 19
    Mustard seed: small measure, total quality; command over mass.

    20But this kind is not cast out but by prayer and fasting.

    ☞G 20
    Some evils yield only to embodied negation: hunger chosen.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s justice is real though delayed, and life is sustained only by faith. Christ’s authority exposes unbelief as impotence and makes deliverance contingent on faith expressed in disciplined dependence.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2:1,2:2The prophet shifts from litigation to procedure: he takes a station, then receives an order to inscribe. Revelation is made portable and public—meant to be read in motion—so the community can outlast the interval between promise and settlement.

    In Evangelium

    • G 18,19The disciples’ private question is answered with a principle about agency: power is not a technique they possess but a participation conditioned by trust. The “mountain” saying reframes impossibility as a diagnostic of the subject, not the object.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Habakkuk is not pious; he is nauseated. He watches a world where brutality is efficient and therefore holy-looking, where the conqueror doesn’t just kill but eroticizes his method, kissing the net that feeds him. The prophet’s scandal is not that God is absent, but that God seems to collaborate by delay, letting the worse devour the less worse, turning people into fish—pure prey, no ruler, no meaning but capture.

    The “vision” is a coping mechanism: write it down, make it legible, so the reader can run—run from panic, run from the fact that justice is always “not yet.” Faith here is not virtue; it’s the only way to keep a self from curdling into the crooked soul of the predator. The unbeliever is simply the person who can’t bear waiting and so becomes what he fears.

    In Matthew the same dread is domestic. A father kneels with a child who keeps throwing himself into fire and water, as if the body is trying to rehearse its own extinction. The disciples fail because they want power without cost; they want technique. Jesus snaps because he’s trapped in a generation that demands results while refusing the interior famine that makes results possible.

    Mustard-seed faith isn’t optimism; it’s a hard, concentrated desire that can’t be bribed by appearances. Prayer and fasting are not decorations; they are the stripping away of appetite until the only hunger left is for the child to live. The “demon” is whatever feeds on that hunger when it’s diluted—spectacle, impatience, the wish to be impressive. The miracle happens when desire becomes singular enough to move a mountain, or at least to stop a boy from burning himself to prove the world is real.

    Semina Verbi

    Habakkuk’s watchman is the inner sentinel: the part of the psyche that refuses both naïve optimism and corrosive cynicism, choosing disciplined waiting until reality ripens into intelligibility. That posture resembles the Stoic prohairesis holding the line under an unjust world-order, and the Bhagavad Gita’s steadiness in action without immediate consolation. Speculation: the “vision on tablets” reads like a technology of conscience, akin to Buddhist mindfulness notes or Sufi dhikr—external marks that keep the heart from being rewritten by panic.

    The net-idol is power worship as a closed feedback loop: success becomes proof, proof becomes permission, permission becomes appetite. That is the same trap the Dao De Jing warns against when force hardens into self-defeat, and what Nietzsche anatomizes when will-to-power forgets any measure beyond itself.

    The mustard seed names a paradox of efficacy: not bigness of belief but coherence of attention. Prayer and fasting function as cognitive and bodily retraining, like yogic tapas or Zen sesshin—an austerity that concentrates the self until it can confront what previously overwhelmed it. Speculation: the “demon” is the family’s chaotic pattern given a face, and the cure is authority plus practice, not mere intention.
