    IIILectio2 Kings 2:1,6-14

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Second Book of Kings

    2 Kings 2:1,6-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And it came to pass, when the Lord would take up Elias, into heaven, by a whirlwind, that Elias and Eliseus were going from Galgal. Heaven... By heaven here is meant the air, the lowest of the heavenly regions.

    6And Elias said to him: Stay here, because the Lord hath sent me as far as the Jordan. And he said: as the Lord liveth, and as thy soul liveth, I will not leave thee. And they two went on together.

    ☞R 6
    The oath binds disciple to master more tightly than geography; refusal to “stay” is refusal to be managed.

    7And fifty men, of the sons of the prophets, followed them, and stood in sight, at a distance: but they two stood by the Jordan.

    8And Elias took his mantle, and folded it together, and struck the waters, and they were divided hither and thither, and they both passed over on dry ground.

    ☞R 8
    Mantle becomes instrument: authority is portable, not intrinsic to the man.

    9And when they were gone over, Elias said to Eliseus: Ask what thou wilt have me to do for thee, before I be taken away from thee. And Eliseus said: I beseech thee, that in me may be thy double spirit. Double spirit... A double portion of thy spirit, as the eldest son and heir: or thy spirit which is double in comparison of that which God usually imparteth to his prophets.

    ☞R 9
    “Double spirit” is inheritance language smuggled into prophecy: charisma treated as estate.

    10And he answered: Thou hast asked a hard thing; nevertheless, if thou see me when I am taken from thee, thou shalt have what thou hast asked: but if thou see me not, thou shalt not have it.

    ☞R 10
    The condition is sight: transmission requires witness, not mere desire.

    11And as they went on, walking and talking together, behold, a fiery chariot and fiery horses parted them both asunder: and Elias went up by a whirlwind into heaven.

    ☞R 11
    Fire interrupts conversation; separation is violent, not sentimental.

    12And Eliseus saw him, and cried: My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the driver thereof. And he saw him no more: and he took hold of his own garments, and rent them in two pieces.

    ☞R 12
    He names the true military hardware of Israel, then tears his own clothing: succession costs identity.

    13And he took up the mantle of Elias, that fell from him: and going back, he stood on the bank of the Jordan;

    14And he struck the waters with the mantle of Elias, that had fallen from him, and they were not divided. And he said: Where is now the God of Elias? And he struck the waters, and they were divided hither and thither, and Eliseus passed over.

    ☞R 14
    First strike fails; the question “Where is…?” tests whether power is God’s or relic’s.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 6:1-6,16-18

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 6:1-6,16-18 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Take heed that you do not your justice before men, to be seen by them: otherwise you shall not have a reward of your Father who is in heaven. Your justice... that is, works of justice; viz., fasting, prayer, and almsdeeds; which ought to be performed not out of ostentation, or a view to please men, but solely to please God.

    ☞G 1
    “To be seen” is the rival liturgy; heaven’s reward system cancels public applause.

    2Therefore when thou dost an alms-deed, sound not a trumpet before thee, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may be honoured by men. Amen I say to you, they have received their reward.

    ☞G 2
    Trumpets: manufactured witness. Hypocrisy needs acoustics.

    3But when thou dost alms, let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doth.

    ☞G 3
    The self must be divided to keep secrecy: even the doer is not allowed full narration.

    4That thy alms may be in secret, and thy Father who seeth in secret will repay thee.

    5And when ye pray, you shall not be as the hypocrites, that love to stand and pray in the synagogues and corners of the streets, that they may be seen by men: Amen I say to you, they have received their reward.

    6But thou when thou shalt pray, enter into thy chamber, and having shut the door, pray to thy Father in secret, and thy father who seeth in secret will repay thee.

    ☞G 6
    Door shut: prayer as exclusion of audience; God is addressed as the only necessary observer.

    16And when you fast, be not as the hypocrites, sad. For they disfigure their faces, that they may appear unto men to fast. Amen I say to you, they have received their reward.

    ☞G 16
    Fasting becomes costume; the face is turned into a billboard.

    17But thou, when thou fastest anoint thy head, and wash thy face;

    ☞G 17
    Anointing reverses the sign: conceal deprivation under ordinary brightness.

    18That thou appear not to men to fast, but to thy Father who is in secret: and thy Father who seeth in secret, will repay thee.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s power and reward are not secured by spectacle but by hidden fidelity and divine witness. True succession and true righteousness are verified by God’s seeing, not by public acclaim.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,7,13The scene is staged with distances: air/heaven above, prophets watching from afar, then the lone return to the bank. The narrative narrows from public escort to solitary agency, so that succession is verified not by the crowd but by the repeated crossing.
    • R 9,10,13,14Elijah offers a final act of beneficence, but the gift is not a technique; it is a share in vocation under a divine condition. The mantle’s fall externalizes continuity, yet the decisive proof is the renewed passage through the boundary-water: the prophet is authenticated by re-entry into the world, not by exit from it.

    In Evangelium

    • G 2,4,5,18The passage builds a threefold pattern (alms, prayer, fasting) with the same logic: public recognition is a closed transaction, already paid. The repeated “Father” relocates moral action from civic theater to filial relation, making secrecy not a tactic but the proper venue of divine recompense.
    • G 1,4,6,18“In secret” is not anti-social; it is anti-competitive. The text attacks the economy of visibility that turns piety into status, insisting that God’s seeing is sufficient and that human seeing is a contaminant when sought as wage.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Elijah stages his own disappearance like a brutal love test. He keeps telling Elisha to stay behind, not out of kindness but to force a choice: abandon me and live, or follow me into the place where mentors become ghosts. The river opens, not as a pious sign, but as permission to cross into the zone where ordinary loyalty turns obsessive. Elisha’s “double spirit” is not humility; it is hunger. He wants the father’s power because he cannot bear the father’s absence.

    The chariot is less rescue than rupture. It cuts them apart mid-conversation, the way death interrupts intimacy without explanation. Elisha’s cry is raw dependence—my father—then immediate self-mutilation: he tears his clothes because identity cannot survive the severing. The mantle that falls is a leftover skin, a fetish of authority. When he strikes the water and nothing happens, the text admits the terror: inheritance is not automatic. “Where is the God of Elijah?” is really “Where is Elijah now that I need him?” Only on the second blow does the river yield, as if power must be coerced by grief.

    Jesus’ warning about secrecy is the same psychology turned inward. Public piety is a chariot you manufacture—fire and noise to keep people from seeing you’re afraid. The “reward” of being seen is a thin substitute for the one thing you actually want: a witness in the dark who won’t leave. So you close the door, wash your face, and fast without a costume. Not because God prefers manners, but because desire has to be stripped of its audience before it becomes real. The secret room is the Jordan bank after the whirlwind: no applause, just the question of whether anything will part when you strike.

    Semina Verbi

    Elijah’s leaving is the teacher’s vanishing act that forces the student to become a self, not a mirror. The “double spirit” feels like the psychological demand for legitimate inheritance: not imitation, but capacity. The mantle is a transitional object; authority becomes wearable, then testable, only real when it parts the river in the successor’s hands.

    That same river-test sits beside the Gospel’s refusal of an audience. The self that needs to be seen cannot carry the mantle; it spends its power on display. Secrecy is not piety-as-performance but the training of motivation: the deed must survive without witnesses, or it was never a deed, only a bid.

    Semina Verbi: the hidden alms and the closed-door prayer echo the Bhagavad Gita’s nishkama karma, action without hunger for fruit. The teacher’s departure and the transmitted cloak resemble Zen’s “mind-to-mind” succession and the shock of losing the master so the disciple can stand alone. Speculation: Elijah’s fiery chariot has kinship with shamanic ascent motifs in Siberian and Central Asian traditions, where the initiate’s passage legitimizes the next healer’s power without public theatrics.
