    IIILectioActs 22:30; 23:6-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 22:30; 23:6-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    22:30But on the next day, meaning to know more diligently for what cause he was accused by the Jews, he loosed him and commanded the priests to come together and all the council: and, bringing forth Paul, he set him before them.

    ☞R 22:30
    The state wants a clean charge; truth is treated as a procedural problem.

    23:6And Paul, knowing that the one part were Sadducees and the other Pharisees, cried out in the council: Men, brethren, I am a Pharisee, the son of Pharisees: concerning the hope and resurrection of the dead I am called in question.

    ☞R 23:6
    Paul weaponizes identity: not evasion, but a forced choice about resurrection.

    23:7And when he had so said, there arose a dissension between the Pharisees and the Sadducees. And the multitude was divided.

    23:8For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, neither angel, nor spirit: but the Pharisees confess both.

    ☞R 23:8
    Denial of angels and spirit: a flattened cosmos cannot host a risen man.

    23:9And there arose a great cry. And some of the Pharisees rising up, strove, saying: We find no evil in this man. What if a spirit hath spoken to him, or an angel?

    23:10And when there arose a great dissension, the tribune, fearing lest Paul should be pulled in pieces by them, commanded the soldiers to go down and to take him by force from among them and to bring him into the castle.

    ☞R 23:10
    “Pulled in pieces”: theology becomes mob physics; Rome rescues by violence.

    23:11And the night following, the Lord standing by him, said: Be constant: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome.

    ☞R 23:11
    The Lord “standing by”: presence replaces acquittal; Rome is named as necessity.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 17:20-26

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 17:20-26 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    20And not for them only do I pray, but for them also who through their word shall believe in me.

    21That they all may be one, as thou, Father, in me, and I in thee; that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent me.

    ☞G 21
    Unity is missional evidence: oneness as public argument, not private comfort.

    22And the glory which thou hast given me, I have given to them: that, they may be one, as we also are one.

    ☞G 22
    Glory is shared downward; the divine life is not hoarded.

    23I in them, and thou in me: that they may be made perfect in one: and the world may know that thou hast sent me and hast loved them, as thou hast also loved me.

    24Father, I will that where I am, they also whom thou hast given me may be with me: that they may see my glory which thou hast given me, because thou hast loved me before the creation of the world.

    ☞G 24
    “I will”: the Son’s desire is eschatological placement—where he is, they are.

    25Just Father, the world hath not known thee: but I have known thee. And these have known that thou hast sent me.

    ☞G 25
    “Just Father”: justice indicts the world’s ignorance; innocence is not neutrality.

    26And I have made known thy name to them and will make it known: that the love wherewith thou hast loved me may be in them, and I in them.

    ☞G 26
    Name disclosed, then re-disclosed: revelation as ongoing infusion—love inhabits, Christ indwells.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod advances witness by both exposing false unities and creating true unity through shared participation in the Son’s life. The resurrection and the indwelling love of Father and Son are the public criterion by which the world is judged and summoned.

    In Lectionem

    • R 23:6,23:7,23:9The hearing becomes a controlled detonation: Paul’s claim forces the council to reveal its internal metaphysics, and the verdict shifts from legal guilt to doctrinal alignment. The narrative shows providence working through factional fracture—truth advances by exposing what a community cannot hold together.

    In Evangelium

    • G 20,23,26The prayer extends beyond eyewitnesses to mediated believers, binding apostolic testimony to later faith. The logic runs from indwelling to perfected unity to knowledge: communion generates a visible form that functions as recognition of the Sender, and love becomes the medium by which Christ remains present in his own.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul survives by weaponizing doctrine as social engineering. He doesn’t “confess” resurrection so much as he throws a lit match into a room already soaked in rivalry, letting theology do what fists are about to do. The council isn’t a court; it’s a hungry machine, and Paul feeds it the one thing it can’t digest without tearing itself apart. His “hope” is also a tactic: if truth exists here, it exists as leverage.

    The dread is physical. He can be pulled in pieces. The state rescues him not out of justice but out of risk management, extracting the body before it becomes a spectacle. Then the night voice arrives, not as comfort but as conscription: you will go on. “Be constant” is not tenderness; it is a command to keep performing under pressure, to keep converting danger into narrative.

    John’s prayer for unity reads, in this light, like the most ambitious form of control: a desire to fuse many minds into one interior. “That they all may be one” is intimacy pushed to the edge of erasure, a spiritual politics where belonging requires absorption. The glory is shared, yes, but shared the way a brand is shared: by inhabiting you. Love becomes a presence that won’t leave, “I in them,” the sweetest line with the sharpest hook.

    Together the texts admit what they usually hide: communities are made by division and by fusion, by splitting opponents and by dissolving selves. Paul learns to live by fracture; Jesus dreams of an ending to fracture that feels like possession. The same desire drives both: to be held so tightly by meaning that the world can’t pry you loose.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s tactical cry—“resurrection”—is a psychological judo move: he refuses the court’s frame, names the hidden fault line, and makes the room reveal its own incoherence. It resembles the Zen use of a koan to short-circuit a brittle consensus, and Socrates’ elenchus that turns a tribunal into a mirror; both are less about winning than about forcing a community to meet the contradiction it lives by. The night-voice that steadies him has kinship, speculatively, with the Bhagavad Gita’s interior counsel to act without collapse when the world becomes a battlefield.

    John’s unity is not institutional glue but mutual indwelling, an identity formed by shared interior life rather than shared slogans. That is close to the Mahayana image of Indra’s Net, where each jewel contains the reflection of all the others, and to Sufi fana and baqa, where the self is not erased into nothingness but reconstituted as a vessel of love. The “glory given” reads like a transmission of participation, akin to the Buddhist bodhisattva vow’s paradox: liberation is not private escape but a relational state that only exists as it is shared. Speculatively, it also echoes Stoic oikeiosis, the widening of the self until the boundary between “me” and “us” becomes morally unreal.
