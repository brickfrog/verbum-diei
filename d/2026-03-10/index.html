IIILectioDaniel 3:25,34-43

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Daniel

Daniel 3:25,34-43 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

25Then Azarias standing up, prayed in this manner, and opening his mouth in the midst of the fire, he said:

☞R 25
Prayer is said aloud inside the furnace: speech as resistance, not escape.

34Deliver us not up for ever, we beseech thee, for thy name's sake, and abolish not thy covenant.

☞R 34
Appeal is tethered to the Name; covenant invoked as the only leverage.

35And take not away thy mercy from us, for the sake of Abraham, thy beloved, and Isaac, thy servant, and Israel, thy holy one:

36To whom thou hast spoken, promising that thou wouldst multiply their seed as the stars of heaven, and as the sand that is on the sea shore.

37For we, O Lord, are diminished more than any nation, and are brought low in all the earth this day for our sins.

38Neither is there at this time prince, or leader, or prophet, or holocaust, or sacrifice, or oblation, or incense, or place of first fruits before thee,

☞R 38
Total cultic absence listed like inventory: no mediating machinery left.

39That we may find thy mercy: nevertheless, in a contrite heart and humble spirit let us be accepted.

☞R 39
Contrition is offered as substitute-liturygy; interior becomes altar.

40As in holocausts of rams, and bullocks, and as in thousands of fat lambs: so let our sacrifice be made in thy sight this day, that it may please thee: for there is no confusion to them that trust in thee.

☞R 40
Imagined abundance (“thousands”) replaces what cannot be done; faith supplies the missing temple.

41And now we follow thee with all our heart, and we fear thee, and seek thy face.

42Put us not to confusion, but deal with us according to thy meekness, and according to the multitude of thy mercies.

☞R 42
They ask for “meekness” from the Judge: mercy framed as God’s own style.

43And deliver us, according to thy wonderful works, and give glory to thy name, O Lord:

IVEvangeliumMatthew 18:21-35

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Matthew

Matthew 18:21-35 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

21Then came Peter unto him and said: Lord, how often shall my brother offend against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?

22Jesus saith to him: I say not to thee, till seven times; but till seventy times seven times.

☞G 22
The number is detonated into impossibility: forgiveness becomes a posture, not a quota.

23Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened to a king, who would take an account of his servants.

24And when he had begun to take the account, one as brought to him, that owed him ten thousand talents. Talents... A talent was seven hundred and fifty ounces of silver, which at the rate of five shillings to the ounce is a hundred and eighty-seven pounds ten shillings sterling.

☞G 24
Debt is hyperbolic, unpayable by design: guilt quantified to show infinity.

25And as he had not wherewith to pay it, his lord commanded that he should be sold, and his wife and children, and all that he had, and payment to be made.

26But that servant falling down, besought him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.

27And the lord of that servant being moved with pity, let him go and forgave him the debt.

☞G 27
Release precedes restitution: pardon is not a payment plan.

28But when that servant was gone out, he found one of his fellow- servants that owed him an hundred pence: and laying hold of him, he throttled him, saying: Pay what thou owest. Pence... The Roman penny was the eighth part of an ounce, that is, about sevenpence half-penny English.

☞G 28
Having been absolved, he “throttled”: mercy not received becomes violence.

29And his fellow-servant falling down, besought him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.

30And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he paid the debt.

31Now his fellow servants seeing what was done, were very much grieved, and they came, and told their lord all that was done.

32Then his lord called him: and said to him: Thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all the debt, because thou besoughtest me:

33Shouldst not thou then have had compassion also on thy fellow servant, even as I had compassion on thee?

34And his lord being angry, delivered him to the torturers until he paid all the debt.

☞G 34
“Torturers” answer to refused mercy: punishment mirrors the man’s own hard terms.

35So also shall my heavenly Father do to you, if you forgive not every one his brother from your hearts.

☞G 35
The condition is internal (“from your hearts”): external correctness is irrelevant.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s mercy is real cancellation, and it binds the forgiven to enact the same mercy toward others. Refusal to forgive reactivates judgment under the measure one has chosen.

In Lectionem

  • R 35,36,37The prayer argues from ancestry and promise, then immediately concedes national diminishment and sin: election is not treated as immunity but as a claim that survives judgment.
  • R 41,43The movement is from seeking the face to demanding God’s glory; deliverance is requested as doxology, not as mere self-preservation.

In Evangelium

  • G 21,23,25,31,32,33Peter’s question is answered by shifting from casuistry to governance: the king’s audit establishes moral reality, witnesses report the refusal of mercy, and the verdict is rendered as public justice, not private sentiment.
  • G 26,29,30Both pleas are verbally identical, exposing the point: the difference is not in the debtor’s rhetoric but in the creditor’s memory; forgetting one’s forgivenness produces carceral ethics.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Azarias prays in the furnace like a man bargaining with the only power left, not out of serene faith but out of stripped-down need. The old apparatus is gone: no prince, no prophet, no altar, no incense. What remains is a body in heat and a voice trying to turn humiliation into currency. “Let us be accepted” is not piety; it’s the naked wish to still matter when every public sign of meaning has been burned away.

The gospel parable answers that furnace with a colder fire. The king’s mercy isn’t a soft blanket; it’s a terrifying cancellation that leaves the servant unmoored. A debt so vast it should define his life vanishes, and instead of gratitude he feels exposure: if nothing is owed, nothing protects him. So he recreates the lost structure by grabbing a smaller throat. He needs someone beneath him to make the world feel legible again.

“Seventy times seven” is not a sentimental command; it’s the demand to live without the narcotic of settling accounts. Forgiveness becomes the refusal to rebuild the prison you just escaped. And the ending admits the dread we avoid: if you insist on keeping score, you will get the universe you crave—an exacting universe with torturers, where the books never close and the fire never goes out.

Semina Verbi

Azarias prays inside the furnace as if the only altar left is the self: contrition becomes liturgy when institutions are ash. That move resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s inward sacrifice of ego, and Stoic piety that relocates worship into assent under pressure. Speculation: the fire reads like trauma—an environment that strips bargaining power and forces a language of dependence.

The unforgiving servant is a study in moral amnesia: mercy received but not metabolized. It parallels the Buddhist critique of grasping after insight without compassion, and the Qur’anic insistence that those forgiven must become forgiving. Speculation: the “ten thousand” is psychic magnitude—shame so large it can only be released, never repaid—while the small debt is the daily grievance the ego can still weaponize.

“From the heart” is not a feeling but a rewiring of reflex: the nervous system trained to reenact what it knows—domination—until it chooses imitation of pity instead. This is close to Hindu and Jain karma as patterning, and to Confucian ren as practiced reciprocity rather than sentiment.
