    IIILectioEzekiel 34:1-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

    Ezekiel 34:1-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And the word of the Lord came to me, it saying:

    2Son of man, prophesy concerning the shepherds of Israel: prophesy, and say to the shepherds: Thus saith the Lord God: Woe to the shepherds of Israel, that fed themselves: should not the flocks be fed by the shepherds? Shepherds... That is, princes, magistrates, chief priests, and scribes.

    ☞R 2
    Indictment begins at the top: “shepherds” named as rulers; office is the target, not temperament.

    3You ate the milk, and you clothed yourselves with the wool, and you killed that which was fat: but my flock you did not feed.

    ☞R 3
    Milk, wool, fat: the flock reduced to revenue streams.

    4The weak you have not strengthened, and that which was sick you have not healed, that which was broken you have not bound up, and that which was driven away you have not brought again, neither have you sought that which was lost: but you ruled over them with rigour, and with a high hand.

    ☞R 4
    A litany of neglected verbs—strengthen, heal, bind, bring back, seek—then the real method: “rigour… high hand.”

    5And my sheep were scattered, because there was no shepherd and they became the prey of all the beasts of the field, and were scattered.

    6My sheep have wandered in every mountain, and in every high hill: and my flocks were scattered upon the face of the earth, and there was none that sought them, there was none, I say, that sought them.

    7Therefore, ye shepherds, hear the word of the Lord:

    8As I live, saith the Lord God, forasmuch as my flocks have been made a spoil, and my sheep are become a prey to all the beasts of the field, because there was no shepherd: for my shepherds did not seek after my flock, but the shepherds fed themselves, and fed not my flocks:

    9Therefore, ye shepherds, hear the word of the Lord:

    10Thus saith the Lord God: Behold I myself come upon the shepherds, I will require my flock at their hand, and I will cause them to cease from feeding the flock any more, neither shall the shepherds feed themselves any more: and I will deliver my flock from their mouth, and it shall no more be meat for them.

    ☞R 10
    God turns predator on predators: the flock is taken “from their mouth.”

    11For thus saith the Lord God: Behold I myself will seek my sheep, and will visit them.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 20:1-16

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 20:1-16 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1The kingdom of heaven is like to an householder, who went out early in the morning to hire labourers into his vineyard.

    2And having agreed with the labourers for a penny a day, he sent them into his vineyard.

    ☞G 2
    The wage is fixed by covenant, not by comparison.

    3And going out about the third hour, he saw others standing in the marketplace idle.

    4And he said to them: Go you also into my vineyard, and I will give you what shall be just.

    5And they went their way. And again he went out about the sixth and the ninth hour, and did in like manner.

    6But about the eleventh hour he went out and found others standing, and he saith to them: Why stand you here all the day idle?

    7They say to him: Because no man hath hired us. He saith to them: Go ye also into my vineyard.

    8And when evening was come, the lord of the vineyard saith to his steward: Call the labourers and pay them their hire, beginning from the last even to the first.

    ☞G 8
    Payment order is a deliberate provocation: last-to-first manufactures the crisis.

    9When therefore they were come that came about the eleventh hour, they received every man a penny.

    10But when the first also came, they thought that they should receive more: And they also received every man a penny.

    11And receiving it they murmured against the master of the house,

    12Saying: These last have worked but one hour, and thou hast made them equal to us, that have borne the burden of the day and the heats.

    ☞G 12
    Complaint is framed as heat and burden; envy dresses as justice.

    13But he answering said to one of them: friend, I do thee no wrong: didst thou not agree with me for a penny?

    14Take what is thine, and go thy way: I will also give to this last even as to thee.

    15Or, is it not lawful for me to do what I will? Is thy eye evil, because I am good? What I will... Viz., with my own, and in matters that depend on my own bounty.

    ☞G 15
    Two axioms collide: divine freedom (“lawful”) and divine goodness (“I am good”); the “evil eye” is the interpreter.

    16So shall the last be first and the first last. For many are called but few chosen.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod judges shepherds who consume the flock and reserves to himself the right to restore and reward by sheer goodness. In the kingdom, covenant is kept and envy is condemned because grace is not a wage scale.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,7,9The oracle is juridical: repeated summons (“hear the word”) brackets the case, making leadership accountable to a word that precedes and judges it.
    • R 5,6The consequence of failed governance is cosmological dispersion—mountains, hills, face of the earth—showing that neglect is not private sin but public unraveling.
    • R 11The pivot is the divine assumption of the role that was delegated: God’s visitation is not consolation but intervention that reconstitutes authority.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,3,5,6,7The householder’s repeated going-out makes the parable about initiative: the kingdom advances by summons, not by the labor market’s merit calculus.
    • G 11,13,14The dispute exposes a rival standard of righteousness: the first workers appeal to proportionality; the master appeals to fidelity to agreement and the right to gratuitous excess.
    • G 16The closing maxim is not a moral about effort but an eschatological reversal: rank is unstable when election, not seniority, is the final sorter.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ezekiel isn’t comforting the flock; he’s staging a coup. The “shepherds” are condemned less for cruelty than for appetite, for treating bodies as a pantry: milk, wool, fat. The horror is that this is what leadership secretly is when it stops pretending. God’s promise to “seek” the sheep is not tenderness but repossession, a jealous retrieval of property, a hand closing around what others have been chewing.

    Matthew sharpens the dread. The vineyard owner is not fair; he is sovereign, and he enjoys the sting his generosity causes. He hires late not because he needs help but because he wants to expose the early workers’ hunger to be preferred. The penny becomes a mirror: you didn’t want bread, you wanted rank. The kingdom here is a workplace where desire is disciplined by humiliation, and the only ones who can bear the wage are those with nothing left to defend.

    Put together, the texts say: you will be fed, but you will not be flattered. The predators are punished, yes, but the rescued are also stripped of their claims. Salvation arrives like an audit and a pay envelope—enough to live, not enough to feel superior.

    Semina Verbi

    Ezekiel’s attack on shepherds reads like a diagnosis of institutional narcissism: leaders convert care into consumption, then blame the scattered for being scattered. The divine “I myself will seek” is the psyche’s immune response when the superego is captured by status—an inner authority reasserting protection for the abandoned parts.

    The vineyard wage parable targets envy as a moral hallucination: the first workers can’t enjoy a fair contract because comparison has become their god. The “evil eye” is the corrosive gaze that turns another’s gift into one’s own injury; goodness offends because it refuses to be rationed by resentment.

    Speculation: the shepherd motif echoes the Hindu ideal of raja-dharma in the Mahabharata, where kings exist to protect the vulnerable and are judged by the condition of the least; it also resembles Confucian ren, where rule is legitimized by humane attention rather than extraction. The equal wage has kinship with the Buddhist critique of comparative craving in the Dhammapada and with Stoic indifference to externals: the wound is not the coin but the measuring. In Islamic Sufism, divine generosity that outstrips human accounting is a recurring scandal to the calculating self; grace breaks the ledger to heal the heart.
