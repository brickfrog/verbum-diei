    IIILectioEphesians 2:12-22

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 2:12-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12That you were at that time without Christ, being aliens from the conversation of Israel and strangers to the testament, having no hope of the promise and without God in this world.

    ☞R 12
    A fourfold privation: Christless, polity-less, covenantless, hope-less—then the final verdict: godless.

    13But now in Christ Jesus, you, who some time were afar off, are made nigh by the blood of Christ.

    ☞R 13
    Distance is not moral effort but blood-measure; nearness is purchased, not achieved.

    14For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and breaking down the middle wall of partition, the enmities in his flesh:

    ☞R 14
    Peace is a person; the wall falls by a body taking enmity into its own flesh.

    15Making void the law of commandments contained in decrees: that he might make the two in himself into one new man, making peace.

    ☞R 15
    Abolition is creative: the voiding of decrees births “one new man,” not a truce between two old ones.

    16And might reconcile both to God in one body by the cross, killing the enmities in himself.

    17And coming, he preached peace to you that were afar off: and peace to them that were nigh.

    18For by him we have access both in one Spirit to the Father.

    19Now therefore you are no more strangers and foreigners: but you are fellow citizens with the saints and the domestics of God,

    20Built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ himself being the chief corner stone:

    ☞R 20
    Cornerstone governs alignment; apostles/prophets are foundation only by reference to him.

    21In whom all the building, being framed together, groweth up into an holy temple in the Lord.

    22In whom you also are built together into an habitation of God in the Spirit.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 12:35-38

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 12:35-38 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    35Let your loins be girt and lamps burning in your hands.

    ☞G 35
    Readiness is embodied: tightened waist, lit flame—discipline made visible.

    36And you yourselves like to men who wait for their lord, when he shall return from the wedding; that when he cometh and knocketh, they may open to him immediately.

    ☞G 36
    The master knocks; the servants’ holiness is measured in immediacy.

    37Blessed are those servants whom the Lord, when he cometh, shall find watching. Amen I say to you that he will gird himself and make them sit down to meat and passing will minister unto them.

    ☞G 37
    Reversal of rank: the lord girds himself; vigilance is answered by condescension.

    38And if he shall come in the second watch or come in the third watch and find them so, blessed are those servants.

    ☞G 38
    Time is stretched into watches; blessedness is endurance under delay.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChrist’s cross creates a single new humanity with direct access to the Father, and this reconciled people must live in vigilant readiness for the Lord’s return.

    In Lectionem

    • R 17,18,19,21,22The passage moves from proclamation to architecture. Peace is announced to both horizons (6), then translated into liturgical access (7): one Spirit, one Father, one route. The social result is a new civic status (8), and the metaphysical result is a new site: a temple that grows (10) into a habitation (11). Reconciliation is not merely the end of hostility; it is the construction of a dwelling where God resides.

    In Evangelium

    • G 36,37,38The scene is eschatological but domestic: a household at night, a door, a returning lord. The uncertainty of arrival (2,4) is the test; the promise is not wages but communion—table fellowship—and a scandalous inversion of service (3). The command therefore forms a community whose time is structured by expectation rather than schedule.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ephesians sells belonging as a rescue from the humiliation of being outside: aliens, strangers, without hope, without God. But the price of entry is blood, and blood is never just comfort; it’s a binding agent. The “middle wall” doesn’t merely fall, it’s broken down in a body, and the law is “made void” like a contract burned so a new household can be formed. Peace here is not serenity; it’s the quiet after a violent merger, the kind that leaves you wondering what parts of you had to die to become “one new man.”

    The temple image sharpens the dread. You are not welcomed as a guest; you are built in. Citizenship becomes architecture. The desire is to be held, to be necessary, to be part of something that cannot be easily expelled. The fear is that the same Spirit that inhabits you also inhabits the structure that owns you.

    Luke turns that ownership into insomnia. The servants are praised for readiness, but readiness is a life spent tensed, dressed, lit, waiting for a knock that could come in the second watch or the third. The twist is seductive and unsettling: the master will serve. It’s intimacy as reward, but also as surveillance’s climax, the moment the watcher is finally watched back and the boundary between lord and servant dissolves. The text makes desire and dread share a lamp: you keep it burning because you want to be chosen, and because you’re terrified of what happens if you’re found asleep.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “middle wall” reads like the psyche’s favorite architecture: the partition that keeps identity coherent by keeping the Other out. The new “one man” is an integration fantasy with teeth, not a bland tolerance—enmity is not negotiated but metabolized, turned into structure. That resembles Jung’s individuation in its demand that what was split-off be taken into the self, though here the agent is not insight but a violent tenderness that makes a home where there was only border.

    The “corner stone” is a stabilizing object: a single load-bearing reference that lets disparate lives stop improvising and start belonging. In Buddhist terms (speculation), it echoes refuge—taking one dependable orientation so the mind can be “framed together” rather than scattered. In Confucian language (speculation), it feels like li made interior: ritual not as external policing but as a shared grammar that turns strangers into kin.

    Luke’s watching servants are not anxious sentries but people trained for arrival, the way Zen speaks of mindfulness as readiness without grasping. The reversal—master serving—has the flavor of the Bhagavad Gita’s paradox of the Lord who bears the devotee, or the Sufi image of the Beloved who becomes the servant of love; speculation, but the psychological point holds: the awaited authority is not merely judge but nourishment, and vigilance becomes hospitality rather than fear.
