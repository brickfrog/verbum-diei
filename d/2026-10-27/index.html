    IIILectioEphesians 5:21-33

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 5:21-33 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    21Being subject one to another, in the fear of Christ.

    ☞R 21
    Mutual subjection: the command precedes the hierarchy.

    22Let women be subject to their husbands, as to the Lord:

    23Because the husband is the head of the wife, as Christ is the head of the church. He is the saviour of his body.

    ☞R 23
    “Head” is not “owner”; the model is cruciform, not managerial.

    24Therefore as the church is subject to Christ: so also let the wives be to their husbands in all things.

    25Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the church and delivered himself up for it:

    ☞R 25
    Authority is measured by self-delivery; love bleeds.

    26That he might sanctify it, cleansing it by the laver of water in the word of life:

    ☞R 26
    Water and word: cleansing is not sentiment but rite and speech.

    27That he might present it to himself, a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish.

    28So also ought men to love their wives as their own bodies. He that loveth his wife loveth himself.

    29For no man ever hated his own flesh, but nourisheth and cherisheth it, as also Christ doth the church:

    30Because we are members of him, body, of his flesh and of his bones.

    ☞R 30
    Bone-talk: marriage language is grafted onto Christology.

    31For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother: and shall cleave to his wife. And they shall be two in one flesh.

    32This is a great sacrament: but I speak in Christ and in the church.

    ☞R 32
    “Great sacrament”: the couple is a sign that exceeds them.

    33Nevertheless, let every one of you in particular love for his wife as himself: And let the wife fear her husband.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 13:18-21

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 13:18-21 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    18He said therefore: To what is the kingdom of God like, and whereunto shall I resemble it?

    19It is like to a grain of mustard seed, which a man took and cast into his garden: and it grew and became a great tree, and the birds of the air lodged in the branches thereof.

    ☞G 19
    Mustard: the kingdom begins as nuisance, ends as shelter.

    20And again he said: Whereunto shall I esteem the kingdom of God to be like?

    21It is like to leaven, which a woman took and hid in three measures of meal, till the whole was leavened.

    ☞G 21
    Leaven works by concealment; transformation is interior and total.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChrist’s headship is enacted as self-giving that sanctifies, and marriage is a sacramental sign of that cruciform union. The kingdom advances by hidden, organic transformation that makes the small decisive and the ordinary charged with divine agency.

    In Lectionem

    • R 22,24,33Paul frames household order inside liturgical fear (“of Christ”) and ends with paired imperatives: love/fear. The passage is not a sociology of power but a discipline of desire: eros is conscripted into sanctification, and the household becomes a site where Christ’s relation to the Church is rehearsed under ordinary pressures.
    • R 27,28,29,31The argument moves from telos (a Church without blemish) to ethics (love as self-care) to anthropology (one flesh). The logic is: union generates obligation; obligation is enacted as nourishment, not domination. The ‘one flesh’ citation functions as a hinge—creation language is recruited to explain redemption’s pattern.

    In Evangelium

    • G 18,20Jesus answers his own question twice, refusing a single image. The kingdom is not captured by one metaphor because it is not an object among objects; it is a regime that reorders scale and process.
    • G 19,21The paired agents (man sowing, woman hiding) and paired media (garden, meal) place the kingdom in common labor. Growth and fermentation are noncoercive powers: they do not argue; they work, and their success is visible only after time.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul stages marriage as a controlled experiment in domination and self-erasure. The language of “head” and “fear” isn’t a quaint hierarchy; it’s an erotic theology of asymmetry, where desire is made safe by turning it into obedience. The wife is asked to fear, the husband to love like a man walking toward execution. Each role is a different kind of dread: hers, the dread of being absorbed; his, the dread of having to deserve the power he’s given by dying to it.

    The trick is that “love” here is not tenderness but sacrifice with an edge. Christ “delivers himself up” to cleanse, to remove spot and wrinkle, to present the other back to himself perfected. That’s not romance; it’s possession by purification. The beloved becomes a project, a body washed into compliance. “One flesh” sounds like unity, but it’s also the threat of losing borders. No wonder the text keeps returning to body talk: nourishment, cherishing, flesh and bones. It’s intimacy described as management.

    Then Luke slips in a quieter terror. The kingdom doesn’t arrive as law; it infiltrates. A seed becomes a tree big enough for birds to lodge—parasites, guests, whatever you call what takes shelter in your growth. Leaven is “hid” in the meal until everything is altered. That’s the same marriage logic in miniature: something small, consented to, then irreversible transformation. The reign of God is not a fortress; it’s fermentation.

    Read together, they confess the same desire: to be taken over and to call it holiness. They also confess the fear underneath: that love, if it’s real, will remake you without asking again.

    Semina Verbi

    The marriage image as a single body being taught to love itself has clear kinship with Plato’s Symposium, where eros is a hunger for lost wholeness, and with the Upanishadic sense that union gestures toward a deeper identity than the separate self; speculation: both can be read as psychologies of integration rather than metaphysics. The mustard seed and hidden leaven echo Daoist smallness that overcomes hardness, and Zen’s insistence that the decisive change is ordinary and often unnoticed; speculation: the point is less conquest than permeation. The birds nesting in the branches recalls the Bodhi tree as a sheltering axis and the world-tree motifs in Norse and Vedic imaginations, where growth creates a habitat for many lives. The leaven working through the whole parallels Stoic pneuma and the Hermetic idea of a subtle fire diffusing through matter; speculation: it names transformation as an interior contagion rather than external force.
