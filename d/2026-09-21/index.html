    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 4:1-7,11-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1I therefore, a prisoner in the Lord, beseech you that you walk worthy of the vocation in which you are called:

    2With all humility and mildness, with patience, supporting one another in charity.

    3Careful to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.

    4One body and one Spirit: as you are called in one hope of your calling.

    ☞R 4
    The anaphora of “one” hammers plurality into confession; metaphysics becomes ethics.

    5One Lord, one faith, one baptism.

    ☞R 5
    The anaphora of “one” hammers plurality into confession; metaphysics becomes ethics.

    6One God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in us all.

    ☞R 6
    The anaphora of “one” hammers plurality into confession; metaphysics becomes ethics.

    7But to every one of us is given grace, according to the measure of the giving of Christ.

    ☞R 7
    Grace is apportioned—gifted inequality inside a demanded unity.

    11And he gave some apostles, and some prophets, and other some evangelists, and other some pastors and doctors:

    ☞R 11
    Offices are distributed; the body is organized, not improvised.

    12For the perfecting of the saints, for the word of the ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ:

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 9:9-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    9And when Jesus passed on from thence, he saw a man sitting in the custom house, named Matthew; and he saith to him: Follow me. And he arose up and followed him.

    ☞G 9
    A tax desk becomes a threshold; vocation interrupts revenue.

    10And it came to pass as he was sitting at meat in the house, behold many publicans and sinners came, and sat down with Jesus and his disciples.

    ☞G 10
    Table fellowship redraws the boundary; impurity is seated, not shunned.

    11And the Pharisees seeing it, said to his disciples: Why doth your master eat with publicans and sinners?

    12But Jesus hearing it, said: They that are in health need not a physician, but they that are ill.

    ☞G 12
    He names himself physician: diagnosis precedes moralism.

    13Go then and learn what this meaneth, I will have mercy and not sacrifice. For I am not come to call the just, but sinners.

    ☞G 13
    Mercy is set against sacrifice; the cult is judged by its object.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChrist forms one body by distributing unequal graces and offices ordered to a single maturity. His mercy authorizes communion with sinners as the proper exercise of holiness, not its betrayal.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2,12The passage moves from virtues to institutional means: the demanded manner of life (humility, patience) is not self-generated but trained by a ministry ordered toward construction. The church is treated as an artifact under assembly—saints are “perfected,” the body “edified”—so unity is a project with instruments, not a spontaneous consensus.
    • R 4,7,11,13A deliberate tension is installed: the confession of oneness does not erase differentiated graces and roles; it makes them intelligible. Diversity is not a threat to unity but the mechanism by which the one body grows toward a single standard—“the fulness of Christ”—which relativizes every partial gift.

    In Evangelium

    • G 11,13The dispute is not about whether sinners exist but about where holiness locates itself. Jesus refuses the Pharisaic premise that separation preserves righteousness; instead he asserts a mission that moves toward the compromised, and he authorizes that movement by appealing to Scripture’s hierarchy of values.
    • G 9,10,12The narrative pairs command and communion: “Follow” is immediately expressed as shared eating. Calling is not merely a change of occupation but a change of company, and the physician image frames the meal as therapeutic contact rather than contamination.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul writes like a captive selling captivity as freedom. “Unity” is not comfort; it is a demand that the self be thinned down until it fits the single body. Humility, mildness, patience: these are not virtues so much as the emotional anesthesia required to survive proximity. The Spirit’s “bond of peace” sounds like a strap. The oneness he repeats—one body, one Lord, one faith—has the rhythm of an incantation against panic, as if multiplicity is the real enemy and difference the crack where terror enters.

    Then comes the twist: grace is “measured,” parceled out, and the hierarchy arrives—apostles, prophets, pastors, doctors. Unity needs managers. The body is “edified” the way a building is: by scaffolding, rules, and sanctioned voices. The promised “perfect man” is less a person than a finished product, a standardized maturity. Desire is redirected: not toward God as mystery, but toward completion, toward being finally adequate, finally seamless.

    Matthew’s scene exposes what Paul’s program tries to contain. Jesus doesn’t recruit the pure to maintain the system; he plucks a compromised man from his booth, mid-transaction, and makes him a hinge. The meal is contamination on purpose. Mercy here is not softness; it is a refusal to let sacrifice—performance, boundary-keeping, respectable pain—be the currency of belonging. He wants the sick because sickness breaks the fantasy of the “just.” The Pharisees fear disorder; Jesus courts it, because only in that shame-stained room can a person stop pretending to be whole.

    Read together, the dread is that unity becomes a polished lie, and the desire is that mercy reintroduces the messy truth. The church wants one body; Jesus keeps choosing bodies that don’t fit, and calls that the beginning of health.

    Semina Verbi

    Ephesians’ “one body” with many offices resembles Plato’s Republic where the city’s health depends on differentiated functions ordered to a single good; the psychological parallel is the integrated self, where drives and talents stop competing for status and start serving coherence. Speculation: it also echoes the Stoic cosmopolis, a shared rational life that makes patience and mildness not niceness but disciplined citizenship.

    Matthew’s table scene matches the rabbinic instinct that Torah is for the yetzer-ha’ra as much as for the righteous, but it tilts further: belonging precedes reform. The “physician” image aligns with Buddhist medicine metaphors—diagnosis before moralism—where compassion is the method, not the reward. “Mercy not sacrifice” has a strong Sufi flavor too: the heart’s tenderness outranks outward performance, and the scandal is precisely that grace chooses contaminated company as its workshop.
