    Seven stations · the sun above traces the day · click a station to read its prayer.
    MatinsAd Matutinum00:00
    Look with favor, Lord, upon the desires and prayers of your people; grant us light to know your will and the strength needed to carry it out. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    LaudsAd Laudes06:00
    O God, who have illumined the darkness of our ignorance with the light of your Word: increase in us the faith that you yourself have given; may no temptation ever be able to destroy the fervor of faith and charity that your grace has kindled in our spirit. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit and is God for ever and ever.
    TerceAd Tertiam09:00
    Lord Jesus Christ, who at the hour of Terce were led to the torment of the cross for the salvation of the world, help us to weep for the sins of our past life and to avoid faults in the time to come. You who live and reign for ever and ever.
    SextAd Sextam12:00
    Lord Jesus Christ, who at the hour of Sext went up to the cross for our salvation while darkness covered the world, grant that your light may always shine upon us, so that, guided by it, we may attain eternal life. You who live and reign for ever and ever.
    NonesAd Nonam15:00
    Lord Jesus Christ, who, hanging upon the cross, granted the repentant thief the eternal kingdom, look upon us who, like him, confess our sins, and grant that we too, like him, may enter after death into paradise. You who live and reign for ever and ever.
    VespersAd Vesperas18:00
    O God, who granted to your bishop Saint Anthony Mary Claret an admirable charity and courage to proclaim the Gospel to the peoples, grant us, through his intercession, that, always seeking your will in all things, we may work generously to win new brothers and sisters for Christ. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever.
    ComplineAd Completorium21:00
    Let us pray: Lord God almighty, since by our rest we are going to imitate your Son who rested in the tomb, we ask you that, when we rise tomorrow, we may also imitate him by rising to newness of life. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    IIILectioRevelation 10:8-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Revelation

    Revelation 10:8-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    8And I heard a voice from heaven, again speaking to me and saying: Go and take the book that is open, from the hand of the angel who standeth upon the sea and upon the earth.

    ☞R 8
    Heaven’s voice issues a command, not an idea; the angel straddles sea and land—jurisdiction without border.

    9And I went to the angel, saying unto him that he should give me the book. And he said to me: Take the book and eat it up. And it shall make thy belly bitter: but in thy mouth it shall be sweet as honey.

    ☞R 9
    The “open” book is not for viewing but ingestion; sweetness at the lips, bitterness in the gut—revelation as delayed cost.

    10And I took the book from the hand of the angel and ate it up: and it was in my mouth, sweet as honey. And when I had eaten it, my belly was bitter.

    ☞R 10
    John verifies the paradox by repetition: experience, not metaphor; the body becomes the scroll’s proving ground.

    11And he said to me: Thou must prophesy again to many nations and peoples and tongues and kings.

    ☞R 11
    “Again” implies prior fatigue; the list (nations…kings) is totalizing—speech aimed at power and plurality alike.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 19:45-48

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 19:45-48 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    45And entering into the temple, he began to cast out them that sold therein and them that bought.

    ☞G 45
    Temple entry becomes judgment; commerce is expelled, not negotiated—purification by forceful removal.

    46Saying to them: It is written: My house is the house of prayer. But you have made it a den of thieves.

    ☞G 46
    Scripture is wielded as indictment; prayer-house inverted into “den”—a sanctuary redefined by predation.

    47And he was teaching daily in the temple. And the chief priests and the scribes and the rulers of the people sought to destroy him.

    ☞G 47
    Teaching and assassination plots run in parallel; the temple hosts both instruction and conspiracy.

    48And they found not what to do to him: for all the people were very attentive to hear him.

    ☞G 48
    The rulers’ impotence is procedural and popular; attention becomes a shield—listening as political fact.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s word must be taken into the self and then spoken against the world’s corrupt worship. True sanctity provokes resistance because it displaces entrenched profit and power.

    In Lectionem

    • R 8,11The scene binds revelation to mission: the message is not merely received but internalized, then redirected outward across every human boundary. Prophecy here is compelled labor under a universal mandate, not a private ecstasy.
    • R 9,10The text stages a logic of mediation: what comes from heaven must pass through the prophet’s body before it can pass to the world. Authority is authenticated by incorporation, not by citation.

    In Evangelium

    • G 46,47,48Jesus’ action reorders the temple’s telos: prayer and teaching are asserted as the institution’s proper end, and that reordering immediately generates lethal opposition. The narrative frames conflict as the predictable consequence of restoring true use to a captured sacred space.
    • G 45,48Public space is contested by competing claims of ownership—market, rulers, and Messiah. The crowd’s sustained attention functions as a temporary transfer of legitimacy, delaying the rulers’ violence.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Revelation’s “little book” is not a message delivered; it is a message ingested. The voice doesn’t ask for belief, it demands appetite. The scroll tastes like honey because language flatters the one who speaks it: to name the world feels like mastery. Then it turns in the gut, because words don’t stay words. Once swallowed, they become tissue, compulsion, a second stomach that digests you back. Prophecy is not a gift but an addiction: sweetness at the tongue, bitterness in the body, and the command to do it again.

    Luke’s temple scene is the same interior drama made public. Jesus isn’t cleansing a building; he’s attacking a system that makes desire manageable. Buying and selling in the holy place is not mere corruption, it’s a technology for turning longing into transaction. He drives it out because he wants prayer to be raw need, not a controlled exchange. That’s why they want him dead: he threatens the economy that keeps dread at bay.

    The people are “attentive” because he speaks like the scroll tastes: honey for those starved of unbought meaning. But attention is a dangerous intimacy. To listen is already to eat. And once the word is inside, it will sour, because it demands a life rearranged. The den of thieves is not just them; it’s the part of us that prefers a price tag to a wound, and would rather kill the one who names it than let the bitterness do its work.

    Semina Verbi

    Eating the open book reads like a psyche taking in a truth it cannot keep at arm’s length: first it flatters the tongue with coherence, then it turns in the gut where consequences live. That sweetness is the seduction of meaning; the bitterness is the cost of becoming its carrier, the way insight becomes obligation and isolates the one who bears it.

    The temple scene is the same inner drama in public. Cleansing is not etiquette; it is boundary-making. Commerce in the holy place is the mind’s habit of turning attention into transaction, prayer into leverage, presence into profit. He drives it out, then teaches in the cleared space, and that clarity makes him dangerous: systems can tolerate devotion, but not undivided attention.

    Semina Verbi: Ezekiel’s scroll is the obvious kin, but the deeper parallel is Vedic and Buddhist: in the Upanishads, knowledge that is “tasted” remakes the knower; in Zen, the sweetness of satori is followed by the bitter discipline of carrying it into the marketplace. Speculation: the “den of thieves” resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s warning about desire turning sacrifice into bargaining, and Sufi adab’s insistence that the heart’s sanctuary be kept free of trade, so listening can become a kind of collective liberation rather than a commodity.
