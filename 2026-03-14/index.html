Reading

A reading from the Book of Hosea

Hosea 6:1-6
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

1In their affliction they will rise early to me: Come, and let us return to the Lord.

2For he hath taken us, and he will heal us: he will strike, and he will cure us.

3He will revive us after two days: on the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in his sight. We shall know, and we shall follow on, that we may know the Lord. His going forth is prepared as the morning light, and he will come to us as the early and the latter rain to the earth.

4What shall I do to thee, O Ephraim? what shall I do to thee, O Juda? your mercy is as a morning cloud, and as the dew that goeth away in the morning.

5For this reason have I hewed them by the prophets, I have slain them by the words of my mouth: and thy judgments shall go forth as the light.

6For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice: and the knowledge of God more than holocausts.

Gospel

From the Gospel according to Luke

Luke 18:9-14
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

9And to some who trusted in themselves as just and despised others, he spoke also this parable:

10Two men went up into the temple to pray: the one a Pharisee and the other a publican.

11The Pharisee standing, prayed thus with himself: O God, I give thee thanks that I am not as the rest of men, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, as also is this publican.

12I fast twice in a week: I give tithes of all that I possess.

13And the publican, standing afar off, would not so much as lift up his eyes towards heaven; but struck his breast, saying: O God, be merciful to me a sinner.

14I say to you, this man went down into his house justified rather than the other: because every one that exalteth himself shall be humbled: and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 1,6The summons to “return” presumes rupture, not mere neglect; the passage frames repentance as re-entry into a covenant God actively administers, not a self-improvement program.
  • R 3The sequence of reviving/raising locates Israel’s hope in divine initiative that outlasts affliction; knowledge of the Lord is presented as pursuit shaped by God’s reliable advent, not by Israel’s consistency.
  • R 4,5God’s question—“What shall I do?”—signals not indecision but a lawsuit: ephemeral loyalty necessitates prophetic intervention that exposes, sentences, and clarifies the divine standard.
On the Gospel
  • G 9,10The parable targets a social theology: self-trust generates contempt, and worship becomes a stage for ranking rather than communion.
  • G 10,14Both enter the same temple and perform “prayer,” but the narrative separates them by their relation to God’s judgment—one assuming acquittal, the other requesting mercy; the closing maxim universalizes the reversal as a rule of the kingdom.

Doctrinal God justifies by mercy received, not by cultic or moral self-certification. True knowledge of God is covenantal submission to his judgment and healing, not the maintenance of religious appearance.

Heterodox Reading
Hosea sketches a god who wounds in order to be needed. Affliction is the alarm clock that makes them “rise early” and call it return; the piety is reflex, not love. “He hath taken us” reads like abduction dressed up as medicine: strike, then cure, so the bruised body confuses relief with intimacy. The promised “third day” isn’t comfort so much as a timetable for dependence, morning light and rain arriving on schedule like a reliable captor. Then the divine voice turns impatient: your mercy is vapor, your devotion evaporates as soon as the pain stops. So the prophets become blades, words that “slay,” judgment presented as clarity. “I desired mercy, not sacrifice” lands less like tenderness than like disgust at transactional religion: stop paying me off; let me into the bloodstream. Luke’s temple scene is the same addiction to being right. The Pharisee’s prayer is self-administered anesthesia, gratitude used to numb dread and keep distance from the contaminating other. The publican’s shame, by contrast, is raw and erotic in its honesty: he won’t look up, he strikes his body, he asks for mercy like someone asking to be touched without being exposed. He goes home “justified” not because humility is a virtue, but because he stops bargaining; he offers need, not résumé. The scandal is that the text rewards the one who admits he can’t control the relationship. The desire underneath both readings is to be held after harm, and the dread is that without the wound we’d never return.
Semina Verbi
Speculation: Hosea’s hunger for mercy over sacrifice rhymes with Isaiah’s inner critique, but also with the Upanishadic turn from external rite to direct knowing, and with Zen’s suspicion of merit-making when it hardens into a self. The Pharisee’s inventory of virtue resembles the Stoic temptation to convert discipline into superiority; the publican’s naked plea is closer to the Buddhist confession of dukkha and the Sufi collapse of the ego before the Real. The “third day” revival echoes the archetype of descent-and-return found in Inanna and Osiris: psyche broken, then reassembled, not by performance but by surrender to a truth that cuts and heals.
