Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

5The Lord God hath opened my ear, and I do not resist: I have not gone back.

He does not say, This is the figure of my body--but This is my body. (2 Council of Nice, Act. 6.) Neither does he say in this, or with this is my body; but absolutely, This is my body: which plainly implies transubstantiation.

26:27And taking the chalice, he gave thanks and gave to them, saying: Drink ye all of this. Drink ye all of this... This was spoken to the twelve apostles; who were the all then present; and they all drank of it, says St. Mark 14. 23. But it no ways follows from these words spoken to the apostles, that all the faithful are here commanded to drink of the chalice; any more than that all the faithful are commanded to consecrate, offer and administer this sacrament; because Christ upon this same occasion, and at the same time, bid the apostles do so; in these words, St. Luke 22. 19, Do this for a commemoration of me.

As the old testament was dedicated with the blood of victims, by Moses, in these words: This is the blood of the testament, etc., Heb. 9. 20; so here is the dedication and institution of the new testament, in the blood of Christ, here mystically shed by these words: This is the blood of the new testament, etc.

These words, by the account of St. Luke 26:22. 18, were not spoken of the sacramental cup, but of the wine that was drunk with the paschal lamb. Though the sacramental cup might also be called the fruit of the vine, because it was consecrated from wine, and retains the likeness, and all the accidents or qualities of wine.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 4,7Isaiah’s speaker is formed by a regimen: instruction precedes mission, and endurance is framed not as stoicism but as forensic vindication—“not confounded” is the courtroom term that drives the passage.
  • R 5,6The movement is from receptive discipline to public humiliation: the servant’s credibility is not established by dominance but by submitted suffering that paradoxically authorizes his word to the weary.
On the Gospel
  • G 26:17,26:18,26:29,26:30The narrative turns on timing and sovereignty: “my time is near,” the meal, the hymn, the exit. Passion is not an accident in motion; it is scheduled liturgy leading into arrest.
  • G 26:31,26:32,26:34,26:56Scripture functions as the spine of events: scattering, denial, flight are not merely failures but part of a pattern Christ names in advance, thereby stripping them of surprise while leaving their guilt intact.
  • G 27:11,27:14,27:15,27:21,27:26Matthew stages competing jurisdictions—council, governor, crowd—each seeking a clean mechanism to eliminate Jesus. The outcome reveals how authority disintegrates into managed violence when it is untethered from truth.
  • G 27:45,27:50,27:54Creation and Gentile witness answer what Israel’s leaders refuse: darkness and earthquake are not decoration but cosmic testimony, culminating in a confession from the watching execution detail.

Doctrinal The Servant and the Son submit to suffering by obedience, not impotence, and God publicly vindicates that obedience. In the Passion, human authority condemns the Innocent, and God answers by opening access and eliciting confession.

Heterodox Reading
Isaiah’s “learned tongue” is not consolation; it is training in how to make pain speak. Morning after morning the ear is opened like a wound, and the servant offers his body as curriculum. The hard face is not courage but dissociation, a chosen numbness that lets humiliation become vocation. Matthew stages the same psychology at full length: a community that can only cohere by selecting a scapegoat. Judas is not an alien villain but the one honest enough to price what everyone else wants without admitting it: a clean transfer of dread into a single body. The kiss is intimacy turned into procedure. At the table the “this is my body” is less miracle than consent: he converts impending violence into something ingestible, so they can keep loving him while they abandon him. Gethsemane is the crack where the performance almost fails; desire for escape speaks once, then is swallowed into “thy will,” the language of obedience that doubles as self-erasure. Peter’s denial is the group’s secret repeated aloud: survival requires amnesia. Pilate’s handwashing is the fantasy that guilt can be rinsed off like dirt. Even the guards at the tomb are fear’s last erotic gesture, sealing the stone as if to keep the story from changing them again.
Semina Verbi
Isaiah’s “learned tongue” that steadies the weary and the ear trained at dawn echoes Buddhist right speech yoked to compassion and Zen’s morning discipline; speculation: the “opened ear” reads like śravaṇa, listening as the first gate of awakening. The face set like rock under spittle resembles Stoic apatheia and Epictetus’s insistence that insult lands only if the self consents. The kiss that betrays has a close twin in the Bhagavad Gita’s portrait of the divided agent, knowing the good yet serving craving; speculation: Judas is the psyche’s bargain with relief, purchasing a quick end to intolerable ambiguity. Peter’s triple denial and bitter weeping matches Confucian shame as moral reset and the Sufi cycle of lapse and tawba where the wound becomes the entrance. Gethsemane’s “not my will” belongs to the same interior terrain as Islamic rida (consent to the decree) and the Taoist yielding that doesn’t break; speculation: the repeated sleeping of friends is the universal failure of witness, the lonely core of suffering that no community quite reaches. The mocked king with crown of thorns rhymes with the Roman Saturnalia inversion and with Hindu lila where majesty wears disguise; speculation: the spectacle exposes how crowds manage anxiety by turning complexity into scapegoat. The temple veil tearing and the shaken earth recall apocalyptic motifs shared with Zoroastrian frashokereti and Norse Ragnarok: the old order cracking so a new moral imagination can breathe.
