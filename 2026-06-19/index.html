    IIILectio2 Kings 11:1-4,9-18,20

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Second Book of Kings

    2 Kings 11:1-4,9-18,20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Now Athalia, the mother of Ochozias, seeing that her son was dead, arose and slew all the royal seed.

    ☞R 1
    Dynasty secured by extermination; maternity turns predatory.

    2But Josaba the daughter of king Joram, sister of Ochozias, took Joas, the son of Ochozias, and stole him from among the king's sons that were slain, out of the bedchamber with his nurse: and hid him from the face of Athalia; so that he was not slain.

    ☞R 2
    Salvation by theft: the heir survives as contraband.

    3And he was with her six years, hid in the house of the Lord. And Athalia reigned over the land.

    ☞R 3
    Six years: providence in concealment; usurpation runs on borrowed time.

    4And in the seventh year Joiada sent, and taking the centurions and soldiers, brought them in to him into the temple of the Lord, and made a covenant with them: and taking an oath of them in the house of the Lord, shewed them the king's son:

    9And the centurions did according to all things that Joiada the priest, had commanded them: and taking every one their men, that went in on the sabbath, with them that went out in the sabbath, came to Joiada, the priest.

    10And he gave them the spears, and the arms of king David, which were in the house of the Lord.

    ☞R 10
    David’s weapons stored in sanctuary: kingship cached as liturgical property.

    11And they stood, having every one their weapons in their hands, from the right side of the temple, unto the left side of the altar, and of the temple, about the king.

    12And he brought forth the king's son, and put the diadem upon him, and the testimony: and they made him king, and anointed him: and clapping their hands, they said: God save the king. The testimony... The book of the law.

    ☞R 12
    Crown plus “testimony”: rule yoked to text; acclamation follows inscription.

    13And Athalia heard the noise of the people running: and going in to the people into the temple of the Lord,

    14She saw the king standing upon a tribunal, as the manner was, and the singers, and the trumpets near him, and all the people of the land rejoicing, and sounding the trumpets: and she rent her garments, and cried: A conspiracy, a conspiracy. A tribunal... A tribune, or a place elevated above the rest.

    15But Joiada commanded the centurions that were over the army, and said to them: Have her forth without the precinct of the temple, and whosoever shall follow her, let him be slain with the sword. For the priest had said: Let her not be slain in the temple of the Lord.

    ☞R 15
    Sacred space refuses blood even for justice; violence is exiled to remain pure.

    16And they laid hands on her: and thrust her out by the way by which the horses go in, by the palace, and she was slain there.

    17And Joiada made a covenant between the Lord, and the king, and the people, that they should be the people of the Lord; and between the king and the people.

    18And all the people of the land went into the temple of Baal, and broke down his altars, and his images they broke in pieces thoroughly: they slew also Mathan the priest of Baal before the altar. And the priest set guards in the house of the Lord.

    ☞R 18
    Iconoclasm as regime change; Baal’s priest dies at his own altar.

    20And all the people of the land rejoiced, and the city was quiet: but Athalia was slain with the sword in the king's house.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 6:19-23

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 6:19-23 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    19Lay not up to yourselves treasures on earth: where the rust, and moth consume, and where thieves break through, and steal.

    ☞G 19
    Earthly treasure is already decomposing; possession is a slow loss.

    20But lay up to yourselves treasures in heaven: where neither the rust nor moth doth consume, and where thieves do not break through, nor steal.

    21For where thy treasure is, there is thy heart also.

    ☞G 21
    Heart is a compass set by what you hoard.

    22The light of thy body is thy eye. If thy eye be single, thy whole body shall be lightsome.

    ☞G 22
    “Single” eye: undivided aim makes the whole person luminous.

    23But if thy eye be evil thy whole body shall be darksome. If then the light that is in thee, be darkness: the darkness itself how great shall it be!

    ☞G 23
    Inner light can be counterfeit; darkness can masquerade as illumination.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod authorizes rule and life by covenantal fidelity and undivided vision, not by seizure and accumulation. What you enthrone—law or loot—determines whether your light is true or a darkness that consumes you.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4,17The narrative reconstitutes political legitimacy through covenantal procedure: oath, public showing, and a second covenant that binds king and people under the Lord. Authority is not merely seized; it is juridically and cultically re-founded, with the temple functioning as the court where the polity is re-authored.
    • R 13,14,16,20Athalia’s charge of “conspiracy” is formally correct yet materially irrelevant: the text treats her reign as an interruption, so her perception cannot name reality. Her removal is staged as a relocation—from temple to palace-way—so that the city’s “quiet” is achieved by reassigning violence to its proper jurisdiction.

    In Evangelium

    • G 19,20Jesus relocates security from the domain of entropy and breach to a domain where loss is structurally impossible. The contrast is not moral sentiment but an ontology of storage: one treasury is perishable by nature, the other is not.
    • G 22,23The eye-saying functions as an epistemic test: the body’s condition follows the organ of valuation and attention. Mis-seeing is not a minor error but a totalizing corruption—darkness that claims to be light becomes the worst darkness.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Athalia is not a cartoon tyrant; she is the only adult in a room of men who think bloodlines are weather. Her massacre is the blunt honesty of succession: if the throne is a god, it demands children. The text wants you to hate her so you won’t notice how closely the priest copies her logic, only with cleaner hands.

    Joash survives as contraband, hidden in the temple like a stolen jewel. For six years the house of the Lord is not sanctuary but vault, and the boy is a treasure laid up where thieves can’t reach because the thieves are the ones guarding the door. When the seventh year comes, Jehoiada doesn’t reveal a child; he unveils an investment. Spears of David come out like relics turned into leverage. The covenant is paperwork after the coup.

    Athalia’s cry—conspiracy—is the one moment of naked truth. The crowd’s rejoicing is desire for order, for noise that drowns dread. They refuse to kill her in the temple, not from mercy, but to keep the building bright while the violence is merely relocated to the horse gate.

    Matthew’s warning about treasure lands like an accusation on this whole scene. They store up a king in a holy place, then call it heaven. The “single eye” is not piety; it is fixation. Everyone here has an eye trained on one glittering object—power—and the darkness is how easily they name that fixation light.

    Semina Verbi

    The hidden child preserved in a sanctuary while a usurper purges the “royal seed” echoes the Hindu Krishna sheltered from Kamsa, and the Greek Zeus concealed from Cronus: a culture’s future smuggled past a devouring regime. The temple as vault for legitimacy, with ancestral weapons brought out like relics, resembles the Japanese regalia logic—authority as a kept object that must be revealed, not invented. Athalia’s cry of “conspiracy” reads like the psychology of the threatened narcissist: any restoration of reality feels like a plot.

    The refusal to spill blood inside the holy precinct parallels Greek suppliants and the Roman pomerium instinct: violence is exiled to the margins so the center can remain thinkable. The smashing of Baal’s images has a faint kinship with Buddhist iconoclastic moments in Japan and with Islamic aniconic suspicion of idols; speculation: the deeper target is not art but the mind’s addiction to manageable gods.

    “Treasure” and the “single eye” meet Stoic prosoche and Buddhist right view: attention as the real currency, desire as the thief that breaks in. The evil eye is not superstition here but a divided gaze—wanting light while hoarding rot—like the Taoist warning that chasing glitter blinds the spirit. Where you store value, you store self; the heart follows the account.
