IIILectioActs 4:13-21

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

Acts 4:13-21 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

13Now seeing the constancy of Peter and of John, understanding that they were illiterate and ignorant men, they wondered: and they knew them that they had been with Jesus.

☞R 13
“Illiterate” becomes credential: proximity to Jesus outranks schooling.

14Seeing the man also who had been healed, standing with them, they could say nothing against it.

☞R 14
The healed body is an argument that stands; rhetoric collapses before a man on his feet.

15But they commanded them to go aside out of the council: and they conferred among themselves,

16Saying: What shall we do to these men? For indeed a miracle hath been done by them, known to all the inhabitants of Jerusalem. It is manifest: and we cannot deny it.

17But that it may be no farther spread among the people, let us threaten them that they speak no more in this name to any man.

☞R 17
They concede the fact and police the name; power manages language when it cannot touch reality.

18And calling them, they charged them not to speak at all, nor teach in the name of Jesus.

19But Peter and John answering, said to them: If it be just, in the sight of God, to hear you rather than God, judge ye.

☞R 19
A jurisdictional clash: tribunal versus God; obedience is reframed as a legal question.

20For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard.

☞R 20
Witness compels speech; testimony is necessity, not strategy.

21But they, threatening, sent them away, not finding how they might punish them, because of the people: for all men glorified what had been done, in that which had come to pass.

IVEvangeliumMark 16:9-15

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Mark

Mark 16:9-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

9But he rising early the first day of the week, appeared first to Mary Magdalen; out of whom he had cast seven devils.

☞G 9
First herald is the ex-possessed; seven devils as a pedigree of deliverance.

10She went and told them that had been with him, who were mourning and weeping.

11And they hearing that he was alive and had been seen by her, did not believe.

☞G 11
A living Christ reported is treated as rumor; the ear refuses what the eye of another saw.

12And after that he appeared in another shape to two of them walking, as they were going into the country.

☞G 12
“Another shape”: recognition is not automatic; resurrection unsettles perception.

13And they going told it to the rest: neither did they believe them.

14At length he appeared to the eleven as they were at table: and he upbraided them with their incredulity and hardness of heart, because they did not believe them who had seen him after he was risen again.

☞G 14
The risen one prosecutes unbelief; rebuke precedes commission.

15And he said to them: Go ye into the whole world and preach the gospel to every creature.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod authorizes witness by acts that cannot be denied and by a risen Christ who commands speech despite human incredulity. The Church’s preaching rests on divine jurisdiction over against every attempt to regulate the name of Jesus.

In Lectionem

  • R 15,16,18,21The council’s deliberation exposes a statecraft of containment: they admit public undeniability, then move to procedural control—separation, internal conference, formal charge—because coercion lacks a punishable pretext. The crowd’s glorifying functions as an external court that limits the council’s options; fear of opinion substitutes for fear of God.

In Evangelium

  • G 10,13,15The narrative moves from private apparition to public mandate: grief-filled community, fractured channels of report, then a universal charge that ignores their psychological readiness. Mission is not granted because the eleven are reliable; it is imposed to make them so—speech is the instrument by which disbelief is broken open into proclamation.

Lectio Heterodoxa

These scenes aren’t triumph; they’re the ugly birth of a voice that won’t stop. In Acts the council isn’t shocked by holiness but by an uncontrollable fact: the healed man standing there like a bruise that won’t fade. The authorities can’t argue with the body, so they try to manage the story. Threats are what you use when reality has slipped your grip.

Peter and John look “illiterate and ignorant,” which is precisely the problem. If charisma can erupt from nobodies, the whole social order is porous. Their line—“we cannot but speak”—isn’t piety; it’s compulsion. Testimony as obsession. They’re not choosing courage; they’re trapped by what they’ve seen.

Mark makes it worse. The first witnesses are not the reliable ones, but Mary with “seven devils,” then two walkers with a story nobody wants. The disciples are mourning because grief is stable, familiar, almost pleasurable in its certainty. Resurrection is an intrusion. When Jesus appears, he doesn’t comfort; he upbraids. He turns their reluctance into a mandate.

The desire in both texts is for containment: keep the name quiet, keep the dead dead, keep sorrow predictable. The dread is that the world has changed and you have to speak it. The gospel arrives like a symptom—public, contagious, and impossible to punish without admitting it’s real.

Semina Verbi

What breaks the stalemate here is the public fact of a changed body and the private insistence of witness: the rulers can’t refute what stands in front of them, and the friends can’t yet trust what they’re told. That tension has close cousins. In Zen, an “unlettered” monk’s authority can rest on direct seeing rather than scholastic pedigree, and the proof is a life that looks different under pressure. In the Bhagavad Gita, there’s a hard line between obedience to worldly order and fidelity to dharma; “judge for yourselves” becomes the crisis-point where conscience refuses a command. In the Islamic idea of shahada and shahid, testimony is not mere report but a life put at risk for what one has seen; the credibility comes from willingness to suffer, not from rhetorical victory. Speculation: Mary Magdalene as first witness echoes traditions where those socially discounted become the initial carriers of insight, as with the Greek Sibyls or certain bhakti saints, and the community’s resistance reads like the psyche defending itself against a reality too disruptive to integrate quickly.
