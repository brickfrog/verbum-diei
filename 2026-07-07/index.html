    IIILectioHosea 8:4-7,11-13

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Hosea

    Hosea 8:4-7,11-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    4They have reigned, but not by me: they have been princes, and I knew not: of their silver and their gold they have made idols to themselves, that they might perish.

    ☞R 4
    Illicit sovereignty: rule without warrant; wealth alchemized into self-destruction.

    5Thy calf, O Samaria, is cast off, my wrath is kindled against them. How long will they be incapable of being cleansed?

    6For itself also is the invention of Israel: a workman made it, and it is no god: for the calf of Samaria shall be turned to spiders' webs.

    ☞R 6
    Handmade divinity collapses into vermin architecture; the artisan unmakes the sacred.

    7For they shall sow wind, and reap a whirlwind, there is no standing stalk in it, the bud shall yield no meal; and if it should yield, strangers shall eat it.

    ☞R 7
    Agrarian causality turned punitive: wind as seed, chaos as crop; yield confiscated by strangers.

    11Because Ephraim hath made many altars to sin: altars are become to him unto sin.

    12I shall write to him my manifold laws, which have been accounted as foreign.

    ☞R 12
    Law proliferates, reception shrinks; revelation treated as contraband.

    13They shall offer victims, they shall sacrifice flesh, and shall eat it, and the Lord will not receive them: now will he remember their iniquity, and will visit their sins: they shall return to Egypt.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 9:32-38

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 9:32-38 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    32And when they were gone out, behold they brought him a dumb man, possessed with a devil.

    ☞G 32
    Mute under occupation: speech held hostage by an alien will.

    33And after the devil was cast out, the dumb man spoke, and the multitudes wondered, saying, Never was the like seen in Israel.

    34But the Pharisees said, By the prince of devils he casteth out devils.

    ☞G 34
    Slander as counter-miracle: deliverance rebranded as collusion.

    35And Jesus went about all the cities and towns, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease, and every infirmity.

    36And seeing the multitudes, he had compassion on them: because they were distressed, and lying like sheep that have no shepherd.

    ☞G 36
    Sheep without shepherd: a political-theological vacuum, not mere sentiment.

    37Then he saith to his disciples, The harvest indeed is great, but the labourers are few.

    ☞G 37
    Abundance with scarcity of agents: the bottleneck is personnel, not need.

    38Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he send forth labourers into his harvest.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod rejects authority and worship that arise from human manufacture and self-interest, and he answers the resulting desolation by sending authorized laborers under the kingdom to liberate and gather.

    In Lectionem

    • R 5,11,13The passage prosecutes cultic substitution as statecraft: wrath ignites at the public symbol, altars multiply into an infrastructure of guilt, and the end is reversal into bondage—"Egypt" as the logic of chosen dependence. The indictment is not ignorance but a deliberate economy of worship that manufactures its own penalties.

    In Evangelium

    • G 33,35,38The exorcism functions as a sign that the kingdom is operative, but it immediately triggers an interpretive war: wonder and accusation compete to name the same act. Jesus answers not by debating but by expanding the campaign—teaching, proclaiming, healing—then institutionalizes the response through petition for workers, making mission the remedy for leaderless crowds.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Hosea isn’t railing against “false religion” so much as diagnosing a craving to be ruled by something we can manufacture. Kings “not by me” are the same as calves made of silver: authority you can touch, buy, and blame. The idol is a coping mechanism for dread, and it backfires. What you build to steady yourself turns to spiders’ webs; what you sow to feel control becomes a whirlwind. Even the laws, once intimate, feel “foreign” because the self has already defected.

    Matthew shows the same pathology in social form. A man can’t speak because something in him has seized the mouth; when the seizure is broken, the crowd’s wonder is immediately poisoned by the need to explain it away. The accusation—he casts out devils by devils—isn’t theology, it’s envy and panic: if healing is real, then their whole economy of control collapses. Jesus’ compassion is not sentimental; it’s the ache of watching people prefer their familiar torment to the risk of being led. The harvest is great because need is endless, and laborers are few because most of us would rather build altars than do the intimate work of shepherding—because shepherding means proximity to distress, and altars let you keep your hands clean while you feed on sacrifice.

    Semina Verbi

    Hosea’s “calf” that decays into webs has the same psychological contour as the Buddhist critique of clinging: what you fabricate to stabilize the self becomes insubstantial under pressure, a hungry structure that cannot feed you. “Sow wind, reap whirlwind” rhymes with karma as moral momentum, not as bookkeeping but as the way a pattern amplifies when it’s rehearsed; that parallel is speculative, but the felt logic is close.

    The Pharisees’ attribution—healing labeled demonic—resembles the Taoist suspicion of “cleverness” that calls genuine virtue a trick, and the Zen warning that the judging mind will reinterpret liberation as threat. The speech restored after expulsion reads like a Vedic or yogic image of unblocked voice: when the inner knot loosens, language returns as contact with reality rather than performance.

    “Sheep without a shepherd” meets the Confucian idea that people fray when rites and just governance fail, and the Stoic sense that a city needs exemplars to train perception. “Harvest” and “laborers” echo bodhisattva imagery: compassion as work, not mood, with too few willing to enter the field; that link is speculative but humane.
