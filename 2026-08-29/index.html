    IIILectio1 Corinthians 1:26-31

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 1:26-31 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    26For see your vocation, brethren, that there are not many wise according to the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble.

    ☞R 26
    Vocation read sociologically: God’s call exposes the thinness of “wise…mighty…noble.”

    27But the foolish things of the world hath God chosen, that he may confound the wise: and the weak things of the world hath God chosen, that he may confound the strong.

    ☞R 27
    Election as reversal: God chooses what the world uses as an insult.

    28And the base things of the world and the things that are contemptible, hath God chosen: and things that are not, that he might bring to nought things that are:

    ☞R 28
    “Things that are not”: ontological sabotage—nonbeing drafted to unmake being.

    29That no flesh should glory in his sight.

    ☞R 29
    The target is boasting, not merely pride: flesh has no standing in court.

    30But of him are you in Christ Jesus, who of God is made unto us wisdom and justice and sanctification and redemption:

    ☞R 30
    Christ is not a teacher of virtues but their substance: wisdom/justice/sanctification/redemption as one gift.

    31That, as it is written: He that glorieth may glory in the Lord.

    IVEvangeliumMark 6:17-29

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Mark

    Mark 6:17-29 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    17For Herod himself had sent and apprehended John, and bound him prison for the sake of Herodias the wife of Philip his brother, because he had married her.

    18For John said to Herod: It is not lawful for thee to have thy brother's wife.

    ☞G 18
    Prophetic speech is juridical: “not lawful” names a boundary, not an opinion.

    19Now Herodias laid snares for him: and was desirous to put him to death and could not.

    20For Herod feared John, knowing him to be a just and holy man: and kept him, and when he heard him, did many things: and he heard him willingly.

    ☞G 20
    Herod’s split mind: fear and fascination; conscience entertained as a hobby.

    21And when a convenient day was come, Herod made a supper for his birthday, for the princes, and tribunes, and chief men of Galilee.

    22And when the daughter of the same Herodias had come in, and had danced, and pleased Herod, and them that were at table with him, the king said to the damsel: Ask of me what thou wilt, and I will give it thee.

    ☞G 22
    Power intoxicated by performance: a dance purchases a vow.

    23And he swore to her: Whatsoever thou shalt ask I will give thee, though it be the half of my kingdom.

    ☞G 23
    The oath is theater—“half my kingdom” from a petty king.

    24Who when she was gone out, said to her mother, What shall I ask? But her mother said: The head of John the Baptist.

    25And when she was come in immediately with haste to the king, she asked, saying: I will that forthwith thou give me in a dish, the head of John the Baptist.

    26And the king was struck sad. Yet because of his oath, and because of them that were with him at table, he would not displease her:

    ☞G 26
    Honor culture as executioner: guests become the real tribunal.

    27But sending an executioner, he commanded that his head should be brought in a dish.

    28And he beheaded him in the prison, and brought his head in a dish: and gave to the damsel, and the damsel gave it her mother.

    ☞G 28
    The dish turns justice into cuisine; the head becomes a party favor.

    29Which his disciples hearing came, and took his body, and laid it in a tomb.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod nullifies fleshly status by locating all righteousness in Christ. When power seeks legitimacy through spectacle and honor, it inevitably turns to blood to silence law.

    In Lectionem

    • R 26,31Paul frames the community’s identity as a citation of Scripture: the only legitimate “glory” is doxological, because the community’s existence is derivative—“of him.” The argument is not anti-intellectual; it is anti-autonomous: any account of status that begins in “according to the flesh” is already a false genealogy.

    In Evangelium

    • G 17,19,21,24,29Mark stages a chain of mediations: unlawful marriage produces imprisonment; resentment waits for occasion; a banquet supplies the mechanism; a mother scripts the request; disciples perform the only clean act—burial. The narrative is about how private sin recruits public institutions (prison, oath, execution) to silence a boundary-setting voice, and how the court’s pageantry cannot prevent its own moral collapse.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “choice of the foolish” isn’t comfort; it’s a threat. It means the usual currencies—brains, pedigree, force—are being actively devalued, not politely ignored. The weak are not elevated because weakness is noble, but because weakness is usable. It slips past the guards of pride. It can be handled, directed, made into an instrument that humiliates the competent. “No flesh should glory” reads like a spiritual gag order: the self must be stripped of its right to narrate its own worth.

    Herod’s banquet shows what happens when glory is the only religion. Power needs an audience, desire needs a stage, and an oath becomes a trap you set for yourself to look grand. Herod likes John because John is the one voice that doesn’t flatter him; the king listens “willingly” the way an addict listens to the dealer—fascinated by the very thing that condemns him. Herodias understands the psychology: you don’t defeat a prophet with arguments; you turn him into a platter.

    The girl’s dance is not seduction as romance but seduction as mechanism. A body moves, a room agrees, and a man’s vanity signs a death warrant to avoid embarrassment. The head arrives like the real centerpiece of the feast: not food but proof that the king can still make reality obey him. Paul says the things that are “not” will bring to nothing the things that “are”; Mark shows the inverse dread, the ruler trying to erase the word that names his theft. He can kill the mouth, but the silence is louder, and the kingdom he offered “half” of is revealed as already hollow.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s inversion of status is the psyche’s revolt against the false self: the persona built from pedigree, competence, and applause. What is “wise” and “mighty” is often just well-defended adaptation; what is “weak” is the unarmored core that can’t win by performance, so it must live by truth. The text presses a humiliating therapy: let the ego lose its favorite proofs, so life can be received rather than manufactured.

    Mark stages the same dynamic as a court nightmare. Herod is split: he “hears willingly,” yet he cannot bear the social mirror of his guests. The banquet is a machine for turning private desire into public fate; the oath is not integrity but panic, a vow made to avoid looking small. Herodias is resentment with strategy; the girl is instrumentality dressed as charm; the platter is what happens when conscience is treated as an embarrassment to be removed.

    Speculation: this resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s warning about attachment to honor and outcome, where action becomes bondage when it serves reputation. It echoes the Daoist preference for the low and uncarved, where power is exposed as brittle theater. It also touches Buddhist critiques of “conceit” (mana): the need to be someone at the table becomes the cause of harm. In Greek tragedy, especially Euripides’ Bacchae, the ruler who half-reveres the holy yet fears ridicule is dragged by spectacle into sacrilege. In Sufi adab, the real disgrace is not losing face but refusing the truth that would break the idol of the self.
