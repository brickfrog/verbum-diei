    IIILectioIsaiah 55:6-9

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Isaiah

    Isaiah 55:6-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    6Seek ye the Lord, while he may be found: call upon him, while he is near.

    ☞R 6
    “While” and “near”: grace has a clock and a distance.

    7Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unjust man his thoughts, and let him return to the Lord, and he will have mercy on him, and to our God: for he is bountiful to forgive.

    ☞R 7
    Repentance is double: abandon routes and abandon interior scripts.

    8For my thoughts are not your thoughts: nor your ways my ways, saith the Lord.

    ☞R 8
    Not a tweak but a mismatch: divine cognition refuses human measure.

    9For as the heavens are exalted above the earth, so are my ways exalted above your ways, and my thoughts above your thoughts.

    ☞R 9
    Vertical metaphor as verdict: transcendence is not negotiable.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 20:1-16

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 20:1-16 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1The kingdom of heaven is like to an householder, who went out early in the morning to hire labourers into his vineyard.

    2And having agreed with the labourers for a penny a day, he sent them into his vineyard.

    ☞G 2
    Contract sets the trap: consent precedes complaint.

    3And going out about the third hour, he saw others standing in the marketplace idle.

    4And he said to them: Go you also into my vineyard, and I will give you what shall be just.

    5And they went their way. And again he went out about the sixth and the ninth hour, and did in like manner.

    6But about the eleventh hour he went out and found others standing, and he saith to them: Why stand you here all the day idle?

    ☞G 6
    Eleventh hour exposes the scandal: time is not the master’s metric.

    7They say to him: Because no man hath hired us. He saith to them: Go ye also into my vineyard.

    8And when evening was come, the lord of the vineyard saith to his steward: Call the labourers and pay them their hire, beginning from the last even to the first.

    ☞G 8
    Payment order is pedagogy: the last are made a mirror.

    9When therefore they were come that came about the eleventh hour, they received every man a penny.

    10But when the first also came, they thought that they should receive more: And they also received every man a penny.

    11And receiving it they murmured against the master of the house,

    ☞G 11
    Murmuring is the liturgy of entitlement.

    12Saying: These last have worked but one hour, and thou hast made them equal to us, that have borne the burden of the day and the heats.

    13But he answering said to one of them: friend, I do thee no wrong: didst thou not agree with me for a penny?

    14Take what is thine, and go thy way: I will also give to this last even as to thee.

    15Or, is it not lawful for me to do what I will? Is thy eye evil, because I am good?

    ☞G 15
    “Eye evil” names envy as a moral organ; goodness offends it.

    16So shall the last be first and the first last. For many are called but few chosen.

    ☞G 16
    Reversal is not romance but judgment; “called/chosen” tightens the gate.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s mercy is sovereign and non-proportional: it is given by divine freedom, not earned by human duration. The kingdom overturns merit-claims and exposes envy as rebellion against God’s goodness.

    In Lectionem

    • R 6,7The imperative sequence moves from access (“seek/call”) to conversion (“forsake/return”), then grounds both in divine initiative: mercy and forgiveness are not extracted but granted.
    • R 8,9The passage does not explain God; it limits the reader. The point is epistemic humility enforced by an asymmetry of agency: God’s “ways” govern the terms of approach and pardon.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,3,4,5,7The householder repeatedly initiates, crossing spaces and hours to recruit; the vineyard is not a meritocracy but a domain gathered by summons. “Just” is left undefined until the master defines it by action.
    • G 9,10,12,13,14The parable stages a collision between proportionality and promise. The early workers appeal to comparative justice; the master appeals to fidelity to agreement and to his freedom to be generous without becoming debtor to labor.
    • G 15,16The closing aphorism reframes the story as eschatological sorting: the reversal is a sign of the kingdom’s order, and the final clause warns that proximity to the call does not guarantee participation in the outcome.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    “Seek while he may be found” is not tenderness; it’s a closing door. Isaiah’s God is not offering intimacy but announcing scarcity: there is a window, and then there is absence. Mercy is real, but it is not predictable, not contractible. “My thoughts are not your thoughts” lands like a refusal to be understood, the kind that makes obedience feel less like love and more like survival.

    Matthew’s vineyard makes that refusal concrete. The householder doesn’t reward effort; he disrupts the moral economy that keeps the early workers sane. The latecomers aren’t romanticized; they are the raw fact that some lives begin too late, or are left unused, and the master’s sudden hiring is a jolt of desire: to be wanted at all, even at the eleventh hour.

    The first workers’ anger is the honest cry of the bruised self: if fairness doesn’t distinguish me, what was my suffering for. The master calls him “friend” the way power does when it wants to end a conversation. He hides behind the contract, then breaks the spirit of the contract with generosity that feels like contempt. His goodness is unilateral; it doesn’t console, it humiliates. “Is thy eye evil” names envy, but it also names the terror of watching meaning slip out of merit’s hands.

    This is not a parable about justice. It’s about a God who won’t be managed by our calculus, who saves as he pleases, and who forces the dutiful to face the possibility that their virtue was a bid for control. The last are lifted not because they deserve it, but because the master desires to give; the first are displaced because they cannot bear a gift that doesn’t need them.

    Semina Verbi

    The wage that ignores hours worked resembles the Zen refusal of “merit” as a spiritual currency: awakening is not proportional to effort, and resentment is the ego trying to invoice reality. It also brushes the Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga, where the demand for “more” reveals attachment to fruits rather than fidelity to the work. Speculation: the “evil eye” reads like the Greek phthonos that tragedy treats as a civic toxin; the master cures it not by argument but by an unanswerable act of generosity. The height-gap between divine and human thoughts echoes apophatic strains in Sufism and in the Upanishadic neti neti, where the real is approached by surrendering the mind’s bargaining logic.
