IIILectioEzekiel 34:11-12,15-17

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

Ezekiel 34:11-12,15-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

11For thus saith the Lord God: Behold I myself will seek my sheep, and will visit them.

☞R 11
God refuses delegation: “I myself.”

12As the shepherd visiteth his flock in the day when he shall be in the midst of his sheep that were scattered, so will I visit my sheep, and will deliver them out of all the places where they have been scattered in the cloudy and dark day.

☞R 12
The rescue is meteorological: “cloudy and dark day” names history as weather.

15I will feed my sheep: and I will cause them to lie down, saith the Lord God.

☞R 15
Feeding ends in enforced rest; pasture becomes command.

16I will seek that which was lost: and that which was driven away, I will bring again: and I will bind up that which was broken, and I will strengthen that which was weak, and that which was fat and strong I will preserve, and I will feed them in judgment.

☞R 16
Mercy is selective: the weak are repaired; the “fat and strong” are handled by “judgment.”

17And as for you, O my flocks, thus saith the Lord God: Behold I judge between cattle and cattle, of rams and of he-goats.

☞R 17
The flock is internally litigated; predators are not only outside.

IVEvangeliumMatthew 25:31-46

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Matthew

Matthew 25:31-46 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

31And when the Son of man shall come in his majesty, and all the angels with him, then shall he sit upon the seat of his majesty.

☞G 31
Majesty is a seat: sovereignty as furniture and posture.

32And all nations shall be gathered together before him: and he shall separate them one from another, as the shepherd separateth the sheep from the goats:

☞G 32
The shepherd’s work becomes geopolitical: “all nations” reduced to livestock logic.

33And he shall set the sheep on his right hand, but the goats on his left.

34Then shall the king say to them that shall be on his right hand: Come, ye blessed of my Father, possess you the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.

☞G 34
Inheritance precedes merit: “prepared… from the foundation.”

35For I was hungry, and you gave me to eat: I was thirsty, and you gave me to drink: I was a stranger, and you took me in:

36Naked, and you covered me: sick, and you visited me: I was in prison, and you came to me.

37Then shall the just answer him, saying: Lord, when did we see thee hungry and fed thee: thirsty and gave thee drink?

38Or when did we see thee a stranger and took thee in? Or naked and covered thee?

39Or when did we see thee sick or in prison and came to thee?

40And the king answering shall say to them: Amen I say to you, as long as you did it to one of these my least brethren, you did it to me.

☞G 40
The least are a sacrament of the King; contact transfers.

41Then he shall say to them also that shall be on his left hand: Depart from me, you cursed, into everlasting fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels.

☞G 41
Fire is not improvised; it has an address and prior occupants.

42For I was hungry and you gave me not to eat: I was thirsty and you gave me not to drink.

43I was a stranger and you took me not in: naked and you covered me not: sick and in prison and you did not visit me.

44Then they also shall answer him, saying: Lord, when did we see thee hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison and did not minister to thee?

45Then he shall answer them, saying: Amen: I say to you, as long as you did it not to one of these least, neither did you do it to me.

46And these shall go into everlasting punishment: but the just, into life everlasting.

☞G 46
Two eternities, no third category; symmetry without softness.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisChrist the Shepherd-King identifies himself with the least and judges the nations by their treatment of them. Salvation and condemnation are rendered by this identification, and the verdict is eternal.

In Lectionem

  • R 12,17Ezekiel’s oracle is a polemic against failed shepherds: God assumes direct governance, first by regathering, then by adjudicating within the community. Restoration is not mere comfort; it culminates in a tribunal that distinguishes exploiters from the exploited inside the same fold.

In Evangelium

  • G 35,36,42,43,44,45Matthew frames final judgment as the public revelation of what persons have been worshiping in practice: the King is encountered under the guise of need, and omission counts as a positive act of refusal. The surprise of both groups exposes that the criterion is not self-conscious piety but enacted allegiance, measured in concrete works toward the vulnerable.

Lectio Heterodoxa

The shepherd here is not gentle. He is the one who scattered them in the first place, or at least allowed the scattering, and now returns like a storm-front with a promise that feels like a threat: I will visit. In Ezekiel the comfort is inseparable from surveillance. He will make them lie down, not because they trust him, but because there will be nowhere else to stand. Even the care is coercive: binding, strengthening, preserving, feeding in judgment. The dark day doesn’t end; it becomes the lighting for an inspection.

Matthew intensifies the dread by turning mercy into a test you never knew you were taking. The “just” are not saints with clear intentions; they are sleepwalkers who stumbled into decency and are startled to learn it counted. The condemned are not cartoon villains; they are the people who missed the signal because the signal was disguised as the ordinary. The king’s demand is erotic in its intimacy and humiliating in its method: he hides in need, in hunger, in the unwashed body, and waits to see whether you can bear contact without reward.

The division between sheep and goats is less about morality than about tolerance for proximity. Sheep submit to being gathered, sorted, handled. Goats resist, keep their distance, preserve their clean autonomy—and that very self-protection becomes the crime. “Least brethren” is the trapdoor: you don’t get to love God directly; you have to love what you would rather step over. The afterlife verdict is the psychological truth made cosmic: you become what you practiced. If you trained your attention to pass by, you inherit the world of passing by forever.

Semina Verbi

The shepherd who hunts for the scattered and the king who hides inside the least both press the same psychological claim: the self is measured by what it does when no applause is possible. Care becomes identity. Neglect becomes a chosen blindness.

A close parallel sits in Mahayana Buddhism: the bodhisattva who refuses private liberation until all beings are gathered back from suffering; compassion is not sentiment but vow. In Islam, zakat and the prophetic insistence on the orphan, the traveler, and the prisoner make the poor a moral touchstone rather than a social problem. In Hindu bhakti, seva offered to the lowly treats service as encounter with the divine presence; speculation: the “least” functions like a living icon that dissolves the ego’s fantasy of purity. Stoicism’s oikeiosis extends the circle of concern outward until the stranger is kin; speculation: the sheep-and-goats divide resembles the Stoic split between a trained disposition and a life left to drift. Confucian ren makes the vulnerable the exam of character, not the object of pity, and turns judgment into the plain exposure of who one has become.
