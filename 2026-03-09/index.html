Reading

A reading from the Second Book of Kings

2 Kings 5:1-15b
Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition - Public Domain

1Naaman, general of the army, of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master, and honourable: for by him the Lord gave deliverance to Syria: and he was a valiant man, and rich, but a leper.

2Now there had gone out robbers from Syria, and had led away captive out of the land of Israel, a little maid, and she waited upon Naaman's wife.

3And she said to her mistress: I wish my master had been with the prophet that is in Samaria: he would certainly have healed him of the leprosy which he hath.

4Then Naaman went in to his lord, and told him, saying: Thus and thus said the girl from the land of Israel.

5And the king of Syria said to him: Go; and I will send a letter to the king of Israel. And he departed, and took with him ten talents of silver, and six thousand pieces of gold, and ten changes of raiment;

6And brought the letter to the king of Israel, in these words: When thou shalt receive this letter, know that I have sent to thee Naaman, my servant, that thou mayst heal him of his leprosy.

7And when the king of Israel had read the letter, he rent his garments, and said: Am I God, to be able to kill and give life, that this man hath sent to me to heal a man of his leprosy? mark, and see how he seeketh occasions against me.

8And when Eliseus, the man of God, had heard this, to wit, that the king of Israel had rent his garments, he sent to him, saying: Why hast thou rent thy garments? let him come to me, and let him know that there is a prophet in Israel.

9So Naaman came with his horses and chariots, and stood at the door of the house of Eliseus:

10And Eliseus sent a messenger to him, saying: Go, and wash seven times in the Jordan, and thy flesh shall recover health, and thou shalt be clean.

11Naaman was angry, and went away, saying: I thought he would have come out to me, and standing, would have invoked the name of the Lord his God, and touched with his hand the place of the leprosy, and healed me.

12Are not the Abana, and the Pharphar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel, that I may wash in them, and be made clean? So as he turned, and was going away with indignation,

13His servants came to him, and said to him: Father, if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, surely thou shouldst have done it: how much rather what he now hath said to thee: Wash, and thou shalt be clean?

14Then he went down, and washed in the Jordan seven times, according to the word of the man of God; and his flesh was restored, like the flesh of a little child: and he was made clean.

15And returning to the man of God, with all his train, he came, and stood before him, and said: In truth, I know there is no other God, in all the earth, but only in Israel: I beseech thee, therefore, take a blessing of thy servant. A blessing... a present.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 7,8The king’s panic names the real issue: only God “kill and give life.” Elisha’s summons is a jurisdictional transfer—Israel is not a healing-state; it is a site where the living God speaks and acts, independently of throne and treaty.
  • R 10,14,15The narrative turns on obedience to a word rather than access to a person. The cleansing is staged as re-creation (“little child”) and then immediately tested by economics: can the healed man learn worship without purchase, or will he reinstall patronage under pious vocabulary?
On the Gospel
  • G 25,28Jesus reads the tradition as a judgment on entitlement: famine and leprosy become proof that covenant privilege does not guarantee prophetic attention. The synagogue’s rage is not mere insult; it is a revolt against a God who reserves freedom to give outside their boundary.
  • G 24,30The saying about the prophet’s hometown frames the attempted lynching: rejection is the native response to unmanageable grace. His unimpeded departure enacts the theme—prophetic mission is not hostage to local approval.

Doctrinal God’s saving action is not bound to ethnic or political privilege but is granted by sovereign mercy to those who submit to his word. Faith is measured by obedient reception, not by status, payment, or proximity.

Heterodox Reading
Naaman is not healed because he is worthy but because he is useful: the story needs an enemy to be rewritten. The captive girl is the real prophet here, a stolen body forced into domestic silence who drops one sentence like a blade. Her knowledge moves upward through the house, through the king, through money and letters and panic. The whole machine of power starts turning because a nobody speaks. Eliseus refuses the theater Naaman craves. No handshake, no incantation, no respectful awe before rank. Just an order delivered by proxy: go wash in our muddy border river. Naaman’s rage is wounded desire—he wants to be cured without being reduced, cleansed without being humbled, touched as a hero not handled like a patient. The Jordan is not medicine; it is a demotion. Seven times is not ritual piety; it is enforced repetition until the ego breaks. “Flesh like a little child” is not innocence; it is dependency. He comes back clean and immediately tries to pay, to buy the meaning of what happened, to turn grace into transaction, to keep mastery by calling it a gift. The dread is that he cannot. Something in him has been taken without price. In Luke, Jesus weaponizes this story against hometown hunger. He says God’s attention has always been promiscuous, sliding past the insiders to the foreign widow, the Syrian leper, the wrong people. The synagogue’s anger is not theology; it is sexual jealousy of the chosen. They try to throw him off a cliff because he exposes the secret: belonging is not protection, and God’s desire does not stay at home. He walks through them, not triumphant, just unreachable, leaving them with the shame of wanting a god who will love only us.
Semina Verbi
Naaman’s cure turns on a psychological humiliation: the strong man is saved by submitting to a small, repeatable act prescribed by someone who refuses to stage-manage his dignity. That arc rhymes with Zen’s distrust of spectacle and its insistence on ordinary, bodily practice over “great things.” It also echoes Taoist reversals where softness defeats hardness and the lowly river outdoes the proud canals. The captive girl functions like the “holy fool” or marginal truth-teller: powerless, uninvested, and therefore precise. That has kinship with the Upanishadic child-sage trope and with Sufi stories where the slave or beggar becomes the corridor of grace. Speculation: the story’s moral energy depends on the insult of receiving life from beneath you. The refusal of payment reads as an attack on transactional religion, close to the Bhagavad Gita’s critique of bargaining piety and to Buddhist dana as non-purchase. The demand to wash seven times has the feel of ritual repetition as re-patterning of the self, akin to mantra japa or misogi in Shinto: not magic, but consent drilled into the body. In the synagogue scene, the rage is tribal narcissism pierced: salvation crossing borders feels like theft to those who think it is property. That resembles Jonah’s resentment at Nineveh, but also the Greek tragic motif where the polis cannot bear the stranger’s truth, and the Buddhist warning that attachment to identity turns teaching into offense.
