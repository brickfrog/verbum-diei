    IIILectio1 Peter 2:2-5,9-12

    Lectio Prima

    A Reading from the First Letter of St. Peter

    1 Peter 2:2-5,9-12 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    2As newborn babes, desire the rational milk without guile, that thereby you may grow unto salvation:

    ☞R 2
    Infancy is not innocence but appetite; growth is commanded, not assumed.

    3If so be you have tasted that the Lord is sweet.

    ☞R 3
    Taste precedes doctrine; sweetness is an argument the body understands.

    4Unto whom coming, as to a living stone, rejected indeed by men but chosen and made honourable by God:

    ☞R 4
    The “living stone” is a contradiction aimed at dead religion; rejection becomes the proof of election.

    5Be you also as living stones built up, a spiritual house, a holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God by Jesus Christ.

    ☞R 5
    Stones become house only by placement; priesthood is architecture before it is office.

    9But you are a chosen generation, a kingly priesthood, a holy nation, a purchased people: that you may declare his virtues, who hath called you out of darkness into his marvelous light:

    ☞R 9
    Purchased people: ownership language; light is not discovered but assigned.

    10Who in times past were not a people: but are now the people of God. Who had not obtained mercy: but now have obtained mercy.

    11Dearly beloved, I beseech you, as strangers and pilgrims, to refrain yourselves from carnal desires which war against the soul,

    ☞R 11
    Desire is militarized—“war against the soul”; the enemy is internal, not Gentile.

    12Having your conversation good among the Gentiles: that whereas they speak against you as evildoers, they may, by the good works which they shall behold in you, glorify God in the day of visitation.

    IVEvangeliumMark 10:46-52

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Mark

    Mark 10:46-52 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    46And they came to Jericho. And as he went out of Jericho with his disciples and a very great multitude, Bartimeus the blind man, the son of Timeus, sat by the way side begging.

    47Who when he had heard that it was Jesus of Nazareth, began to cry out and to say: Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me.

    ☞G 47
    He names what he cannot see: “Son of David” is sight by confession.

    48And many rebuked him, that he might hold his peace; but he cried a great deal the more: Son of David, have mercy on me.

    ☞G 48
    The crowd polices silence; mercy is accessed by refusing decorum.

    49And Jesus, standing still, commanded him to be called. And they call the blind man, saying to him: Be of better comfort. Arise, he calleth thee.

    ☞G 49
    Jesus halts the procession; the kingdom’s pace is set by the needy.

    50Who casting off his garment leaped up and came to him.

    ☞G 50
    Garment thrown off: he abandons his old economy before the cure is granted.

    51And Jesus answering, said to him: What wilt thou that I should do to thee? And the blind man said to him: Rabboni. That I may see.

    ☞G 51
    The question exposes desire; healing is not automatic but petitioned.

    52And Jesus saith to him: Go thy way. Thy faith hath made thee whole. And immediately he saw and followed him in the way.

    ☞G 52
    Sight issues in discipleship—he follows “in the way,” not back to the roadside.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod forms a people by joining them to the rejected Christ and makes their life a public testimony. Faith that cries for mercy is answered with restoration ordered toward following Jesus.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4,5,12The passage moves from Christ as the rejected cornerstone to a corporate identity that is publicly legible. The community’s vocation is not private piety but a visible polity whose conduct forces even hostile observers to render a verdict at the “day of visitation.”
    • R 9,10Election is described in civic and cultic terms—generation, nation, priesthood—so the Church is framed as a new people with a new allegiance. The point is not privilege but proclamation: identity is given for declaration.

    In Evangelium

    • G 46,49,51,52Mark stages a reversal of agency: the beggar initiates, the crowd obstructs, Jesus reorders the social field by calling him forward. The healing is narrated as a commissioning—faith is validated by movement into Jesus’ path, turning a marginal figure into a follower.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Peter’s “milk” is not innocence; it’s addiction. The newborn is a figure for dependence, a sanctioned craving that replaces other cravings. “Taste that the Lord is sweet” is the language of appetite, and the bargain is stark: trade the body’s hungers for a holy hunger, let desire be captured and redirected. The community becomes masonry—living stones—useful precisely because they are no longer soft. Chosen, purchased, built in: a people made by acquisition, held together by pressure.

    So they are “strangers and pilgrims” not because the world is corrupt, but because they have been reorganized into an enclave that must justify itself under suspicion. Their good conduct is performance under surveillance, a way of surviving accusation until the “day of visitation,” which feels less like comfort than audit.

    Bartimaeus makes the subtext audible. The crowd polices silence; mercy is a disturbance. He refuses decorum, and Jesus’ first act is not healing but stopping everything: attention as rupture. “What wilt thou?” is dangerous because it forces desire into speech. Bartimaeus throws off his garment—the last prop of his old identity—and risks being seen. Sight arrives, and with it a new captivity: he follows “in the way,” drafted into the same public scrutiny Peter describes. The miracle is not escape; it’s conscription into visibility.

    Semina Verbi

    The “milk” that makes you grow reads like the yogic insistence on sāttvic nourishment and steady practice: not information but a clean diet of attention that re-patterns desire. The “living stones” becoming a house resembles Stoic cosmopolis and civic virtue, where the self is a block fitted into a larger rational order; speculation: Peter’s masonry is a psychological antidote to fragmentation, a way to feel load-bearing without becoming hard.

    “Strangers and pilgrims” has a Buddhist timbre: non-attachment as a social stance, living in a world without being owned by it. The war of “carnal desires” against the soul parallels Plato’s charioteer and the Gītā’s battle within; speculation: the text frames libido not as dirt but as a rival polity inside the person.

    Bartimaeus’ insistence against the crowd’s shaming echoes the Zen trope of the outsider who refuses polite silence to ask the real question, and the Sufi cry that keeps calling the Beloved through rebuke. Casting off the garment is like renunciation in Jain and monastic traditions: dropping the identity that earns alms to risk a new self. “What do you want?” sounds almost like a therapeutic pivot, the Lacanian demand clarified into desire; the healing is not only sight but permission to want plainly, then to walk a path instead of orbiting it.
