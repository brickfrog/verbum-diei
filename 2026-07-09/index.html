    IIILectioHosea 11:1-4,8-9

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Hosea

    Hosea 11:1-4,8-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1As the morning passeth, so hath the king of Israel passed away. Because Israel was a child, and I loved him: and I called my son out of Egypt. I called my son... Viz., Israel. But as the calling of Israel out of Egypt, was a figure of the calling of Christ from thence; therefore this text is also applicable to Christ, as we learn from Matthew 2.15.

    ☞R 1
    Election spoken in the past tense: love precedes obedience; “son” is a title before it is a virtue.

    2As they called them, they went away from before their face: they offered victims to Baalim, and sacrificed to idols. They called... Viz., Moses and Aaron called; but they went away after other gods and would not hear.

    ☞R 2
    The nearer the summons, the faster the flight; liturgy traded for livestock and smoke.

    3And I was like a foster father to Ephraim, I carried them in my arms: and they knew not that I healed them.

    ☞R 3
    Providence as nursing: carried, healed—yet unrecognized; grace is often misattributed to weather.

    4I will draw them with the cords of Adam, with the bands of love: and I will be to them as one that taketh off the yoke on their jaws: and I put his meat to him that he might eat.

    ☞R 4
    “Cords” and “bands”: captivity re-described as courtship; the yoke lifted only to make room for feeding.

    8How shall I deal with thee, O Ephraim, shall I protect thee, O Israel? how shall I make thee as Adama, shall I set thee as Seboim? my heart is turned within me, my repentance is stirred up. Adama, etc... Adama and Seboim were two cities in the neighborhood of Sodom: and underwent the like destruction.

    ☞R 8
    Judgment names cities like a memory of fire; the threat is real, the hesitation more real.

    9I will not execute the fierceness of my wrath: I will not return to destroy Ephraim: because I am God, and not man: the holy one in the midst of thee, and I will not enter into the city.

    ☞R 9
    The decisive difference: God’s holiness interrupts wrath; transcendence becomes restraint.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 10:7-15

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 10:7-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    7And going, preach, saying: The kingdom of heaven is at hand.

    ☞G 7
    Proclamation without preface: the reign arrives before credentials do.

    8Heal the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out devils: freely have you received, freely give.

    ☞G 8
    Miracles as alms: power is not property; the price is zero because the source is gift.

    9Do not possess gold, nor silver, nor money in your purses:

    ☞G 9
    Poverty is policy, not aesthetic; the messenger must not look like the message is for sale.

    10Nor scrip for your journey, nor two coats, nor shoes, nor a staff; for the workman is worthy of his meat.

    ☞G 10
    No redundancies: one coat theology; dependence is built into the itinerary.

    11And into whatsoever city or town you shall enter, inquire who in it is worthy, and there abide till you go thence.

    12And when you come into the house, salute it, saying: Peace be to this house.

    13And if that house be worthy, your peace shall come upon it; but if it be not worthy, your peace shall return to you.

    ☞G 13
    Peace is reversible; blessing is not wasted on refusal.

    14And whosoever shall not receive you, nor hear your words: going forth out of that house or city shake off the dust from your feet.

    ☞G 14
    Dust shaken off: a small gesture that becomes a verdict; separation enacted in the body.

    15Amen I say to you, it shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city.

    ☞G 15
    Sodom reappears as a measuring rod: rejection of light outweighs ancient vice.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s holiness does not dilute judgment; it governs it, converting deserved destruction into a mission of gratuitous mercy. To receive or reject that mercy in the apostolic word is to choose one’s standing at the final judgment.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,9The passage stages a conflict inside divine action: covenant love generates a claim (“son”), yet holiness refuses to let justice collapse into mere retaliation. The result is not denial of judgment but its suspension for the sake of God’s own name—mercy grounded in ontology, not in Israel’s performance.
    • R 4,8The rhetoric turns coercive images into salvific ones: what could be read as control (cords, yoke) is re-specified as ordered freedom aimed at life. The threatened Sodom-like outcome functions as the negative horizon that makes the restraint intelligible: God can destroy; God chooses not to.

    In Evangelium

    • G 7,11Mission is framed as an extension of the kingdom’s immediacy: the announcement and the signs belong together, and the emissary’s lack of provisions is part of the sign. Worthiness is not moral perfection but receptivity; the household becomes the unit where the kingdom is either hosted or barred.
    • G 12,15Peace operates as a juridical token: offered, accepted, or withdrawn, it marks a house’s stance toward the reign being announced. The Sodom comparison escalates the stakes: refusal of apostolic speech is treated as eschatological evidence, because it is refusal of the visitation itself.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Hosea is not a tender father; he is a lover with power, remembering a childhood he manufactured. “I called my son out of Egypt” is the origin-story he keeps repeating to justify possession, and Israel’s running after Baal reads less like stupidity than like a desperate search for a love that doesn’t smother. The “cords of Adam” are not gentle; they are the old human ropes of attachment, the bands that feel like care until they tighten. He lifts the yoke from their jaws and feeds them, and the intimacy is almost animal: the beloved reduced to a mouth he can manage.

    Then the crack: “How shall I make thee as Adama… Seboim?” Desire flashes into annihilation. The divine “repentance” is not moral improvement but a surge of conflicted appetite, the moment the abuser discovers he cannot bear to lose what he wants to punish. “Because I am God, and not man” is not comfort; it is dread. It means his restraint is arbitrary, not accountable, not constrained by the limits that keep human rage from becoming weather.

    Matthew’s mission discourse is that same psychology exported. The disciples are sent out stripped, dependent, radiant with borrowed power, and trained to sort the world into “worthy” and “unworthy” on arrival. Peace becomes a projectile that can be revoked; rejection is met with a ritual of contempt, dust shaken off like a curse. The threat of Sodom is not a warning about vice so much as a demand for reception: love arrives hungry, and if it isn’t hosted it imagines fire. The kingdom at hand is not only good news; it is the nearness of a desire that cannot tolerate being unwanted.

    Semina Verbi

    Hosea’s God is a parent who cannot finish the sentence of punishment. The drama is not law but attachment: the child bolts, the parent reaches anyway, and the reaching becomes the parent’s identity. That inner turn—wrath arrested by tenderness—reads like the psyche refusing to annihilate what it loves even when betrayed, a refusal that costs pride and preserves relation.

    Matthew’s mission ethic is the same tenderness externalized as practice: go light, give without pricing, offer peace that cannot be stolen, and when refused, leave without revenge. The dust-shaking is boundary, not curse: a way to keep the heart from hardening into retaliation.

    Semina Verbi: Hosea’s “cords of love” resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s vision of the divine drawing the wandering self back through devotion, not coercion (speculation). The parent who stays merciful over against deserved ruin echoes the bodhisattva vow in Mahayana Buddhism: compassion that interrupts the logic of deserts (speculation). Matthew’s radical non-accumulation and itinerant trust parallels Jain and Buddhist mendicancy, and the offered “peace” that returns if rejected has kinship with Stoic autonomy: what is given in goodwill remains yours when refused (speculation).
