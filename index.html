    IIILectioJeremiah 18:1-6

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

    Jeremiah 18:1-6 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1The word that came to Jeremias from the Lord, saying:

    2Arise, and go down into the potter's house, and there thou shalt hear my words.

    ☞R 2
    Command is spatial: revelation requires descent.

    3And I went down into the potter's house, and behold he was doing a work on the wheel.

    ☞R 3
    The prophet learns by watching labor, not by argument.

    4And the vessel was broken which he was making of clay with his hands: and turning he made another vessel, as it seemed good in his eyes to make it.

    ☞R 4
    Ruin is not the end; it is raw material for a second intention.

    5Then the word of the Lord came to me, saying:

    6Cannot I do with you, as this potter, O house of Israel, saith the Lord? behold as clay is in the hand of the potter, so are you in my hand, O house of Israel.

    ☞R 6
    The insult of the metaphor: Israel is handled, not consulted.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 13:47-53

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 13:47-53 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    47Again the kingdom of heaven is like to a net cast into the sea, and gathering together of all kinds of fishes.

    ☞G 47
    The net is indiscriminate; the kingdom’s reach precedes its sorting.

    48Which, when it was filled, they drew out, and sitting by the shore, they chose out the good into vessels, but the bad they cast forth.

    ☞G 48
    Selection happens on shore: judgment is after the haul, not during.

    49So shall it be at the end of the world. The angels shall go out, and shall separate the wicked from among the just.

    ☞G 49
    Separation is delegated; angels do what humans cannot do cleanly.

    50And shall cast them into the furnace of fire: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

    ☞G 50
    The image is not correction but disposal: furnace, not workshop.

    51Have ye understood all these things? They say to him: Yes.

    52He said unto them: Therefore every scribe instructed in the kingdom of heaven, is like to a man that is a householder, who bringeth forth out of his treasure new things and old.

    ☞G 52
    The trained scribe is a curator: continuity without antiquarianism.

    53And it came to pass: when Jesus had finished these parables, he passed from thence.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s reign gathers without discrimination and judges without appeal; the same sovereignty that forms a people also separates them at the end. Instruction in the kingdom makes one accountable to preserve the old while dispensing the new under that judgment.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,5The passage frames prophecy as mediated speech: the “word” comes, then comes again, bracketing the enacted sign. The sign is not illustrative decoration; it is the mode by which divine agency is made intelligible in history.
    • R 6The point is covenantal sovereignty: the same Lord who formed a people reserves the right to refashion them. The metaphor denies national self-determination as a theological category; identity is derivative of the maker’s ongoing act.

    In Evangelium

    • G 47,49The parable sequence moves from mixed collection to terminal division, locating moral clarity at “the end,” not in the present field. The kingdom is portrayed as a process that tolerates mixture until its appointed reckoning.
    • G 51,53Understanding is tested, then immediately commissioned: instruction produces stewardship. The final note—Jesus “finished” and “passed”—signals closure of a teaching cycle and the transfer of responsibility to those who heard.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Jeremiah walks into the potter’s shop expecting instruction and sees a small cruelty: the vessel fails, and the hands do not mourn it. They simply crush the evidence and start again. The lesson isn’t comfort. It is the cold intimacy of power that calls itself care. You are not consulted; you are handled. The phrase “as it seemed good in his eyes” is the giveaway: taste, not justice, decides your shape.

    Matthew’s net is the same workshop, just scaled up. Everything is gathered, indiscriminately desired, then sorted with a calm, clerical violence. “Good into vessels” echoes the potter: some lives get a container, a future, a place to be kept; the rest are waste heat. The horror is not only the furnace but the shore-side quiet where the choosing happens, the way judgment is framed as housekeeping.

    And then the final turn of the knife: “Have ye understood?” They say yes, as if comprehension were consent. The trained scribe becomes the householder, the one who learns to enjoy the power of selection, to curate “new things and old” and call it wisdom. The kingdom here is not escape from dread; it is dread made orderly, desire made administrative, the promise that someone’s hands will finally decide what you are.

    Semina Verbi

    Jeremiah’s wheel is close, speculatively, to the Daoist uncarved block: form is provisional, and what matters is the yielding substance that can be re-made without resentment. It also brushes the Zen sense of “beginner’s mind,” where failure is not moral collapse but the moment the hands return to the clay.

    The net’s sorting echoes the Egyptian weighing of the heart in the Hall of Ma’at: a final discrimination that is less about tribal belonging than about what one has become. In Plato’s myth of Er, too, souls are sifted by the shape of their lives, not by their self-description.

    The householder with new and old resembles rabbinic midrash at its best, and, speculatively, the Confucian junzi: a keeper of inheritance who does not fossilize it, but makes it usable now without severing its roots.
