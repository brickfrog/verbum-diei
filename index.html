    IIILectioEzekiel 37:1-14

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

    Ezekiel 37:1-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1The hand of the Lord was upon me, and brought me forth in the spirit of the Lord: and set me down in the midst of a plain that was full of bones.

    ☞R 1
    “Hand… upon me”: coercive grace; the prophet is carried, not consulted.

    2And he led me about through them on every side: now they were very many upon the face of the plain, and they were exceeding dry.

    ☞R 2
    A guided tour of ruin; God makes him look until denial is exhausted.

    3And he said to me: Son of man, dost thou think these bones shall live and I answered: O Lord God, thou knowest.

    ☞R 3
    The only safe answer: ignorance that yields to omniscience.

    4And he said to me: Prophesy concerning these bones; and say to them: Ye dry bones, hear the word of the Lord.

    ☞R 4
    Addressing bones is liturgy at the edge of absurdity.

    5Thus saith the Lord God to these bones: Behold, I will send spirit into you, and you shall live. Spirit... That is, soul, life, and breath.

    6And I will lay sinews upon you, and will cause flesh to grow over you, and will cover you with skin: and I will give you spirit and you shall live, and you shall know that I am the Lord.

    7And I prophesied as he had commanded me: and as I prophesied there was a noise, and behold a commotion: and the bones came together, each one, its joint.

    ☞R 7
    Resurrection begins as sound: noise before life, order before breath.

    8And I saw, and behold the sinews, and the flesh came up upon them: and the skin was stretched out over them, but there was no spirit in them.

    ☞R 8
    Anatomy without spirit: a corpse can be well-assembled.

    9And he said to me: Prophesy to the spirit, prophesy, O son of man, and say to the spirit: Thus saith the Lord God: Come, spirit, from the four winds, and blow upon these slain, and let them live again.

    ☞R 9
    “Four winds”: breath as cosmic summons, not private vitality.

    10And I prophesied as he had commanded me: and the spirit came into them, and they lived: and they stood up upon their feet, an exceeding great army.

    ☞R 10
    Not merely alive—militarized: life reorganized into an “army.”

    11And he said to me: Son of man: All these bones are the house of Israel: they say: Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost, and we are cut off.

    12Therefore prophesy, and say to them: Thus saith the Lord God: Behold I will open your graves, and will bring you out of your sepulchres, O my people: and will bring you into the land of Israel.

    13And you shall know that I am the Lord, when I shall have opened your sepulchres, and shall have brought you out of your graves, O my people:

    14And shall have put my spirit in you, and you shall live, and I shall make you rest upon your own land: and you shall know that I the Lord have spoken, and done it, saith the Lord God:

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 22:34-40

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 22:34-40 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    34But the Pharisees, hearing that he had silenced the Sadducees, came together.

    35And one of them, a doctor of the law, asked him, tempting him:

    ☞G 35
    A question as trap; theology used as leverage.

    36Master, which is the great commandment in the law?

    37Jesus said to him: Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart and with thy whole soul and with thy whole mind.

    ☞G 37
    Totalizing love: heart/soul/mind leaves no unclaimed interior.

    38This is the greatest and the first commandment.

    39And the second is like to this: Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.

    ☞G 39
    Neighbour-love measured by self-love: the standard is already in you.

    40On these two commandments dependeth the whole law and the prophets.

    ☞G 40
    “Dependeth”: law and prophets hang like a door on two hinges.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod restores the dead by his word and Spirit, and the restored life is ordered toward total love of God that necessarily issues in love of neighbour.

    In Lectionem

    • R 5,6The sequence is deliberate: command precedes capacity. God speaks life into what cannot respond, then builds form, then grants the animating principle, and only then yields recognition (“you shall know”). Knowledge of God is the product of enacted restoration, not its precondition.
    • R 11,12,14The vision is interpreted as national death and return: despair (“cut off”) is answered by exodus-from-graves and settlement. The end is covenantal stability—rest on the land—grounded in divine speech-act (“spoken, and done”).

    In Evangelium

    • G 34,36,38After a public defeat of one faction, another tests him with hierarchy: which command governs the rest. Jesus answers by naming a first principle rather than a casuistic rule, shifting the debate from legal ranking to the source of obedience.
    • G 37,39,40The two loves function as a single architecture: love of God defines the orientation of the whole person; love of neighbour is its necessary social extension. The claim that everything “depends” on them makes love not an add-on virtue but the load-bearing structure of revelation.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ezekiel is walked through a field of the already-finished. The bones aren’t tragic; they’re clean, bleached, beyond sentiment. God doesn’t comfort him with meaning. God asks for an opinion: do you think this can live. Ezekiel answers like a survivor: you know, not me. Then the real violence begins, not with death but with reconstruction. Sinew, flesh, skin: a forced intimacy with matter, bodies assembled by command, a miracle that sounds like machinery. The first resurrection is a horror—perfectly formed, perfectly empty. Life arrives only when the wind is ordered to invade them. They stand as an “army,” not a family. Hope returns as mobilization.

    Read beside that, “love” stops sounding like warmth and starts sounding like total claim. Whole heart, whole soul, whole mind: nothing left unoccupied. And “neighbor as yourself” is not tenderness; it’s the rule that makes you responsible for the other the way you can’t escape yourself. The law hangs on this because it’s a leash disguised as desire. The dread is that you will be made alive for a purpose you didn’t choose, and then told the name of that purpose is love.

    Semina Verbi

    Ezekiel’s valley reads like trauma work: the psyche is a field of disarticulated parts, “exceeding dry,” and speech is the first intervention that makes scattered fragments find their joints. The staged sequence matters: structure returns before breath, body before animating meaning. That is how recovery often feels—function without vitality, then, if it comes, a second arrival that turns mere reconstruction into agency. The “army” is not militarism so much as coherence: a self able to stand.

    The “four winds” echoes prana in Yoga and qi in Daoist practice: life as a circulating breath that is not owned but received, gathered, invited. Speculation: the prophet’s command to the breath resembles mantra and dharani—language used less to inform than to tune attention until aliveness re-enters.

    The double command to love reads like a psychological integration principle: devotion as the vertical axis that orders desire, and neighbor-love as the horizontal test that prevents spiritual narcissism. In Confucian ren and Buddhist metta, care is trained outward until it becomes a stable disposition; here it is framed as the load-bearing beam of the whole moral architecture. Speculation: “as thyself” assumes a self worth befriending, making self-contempt a moral problem, not a private mood.

    Together they suggest one movement: breath returns to the reassembled self, and the reanimated self is measured by its capacity to love without splitting—no holiness that bypasses the neighbor, no ethics that forgets the source of breath.
