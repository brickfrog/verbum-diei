    IIILectioWisdom 6:12-16

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Wisdom

    Wisdom 6:12-16 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12Covet ye, therefore, my words, and love them, and you shall have instruction.

    ☞R 12
    “Covet” is rehabilitated: desire redirected from possession to instruction.

    13Wisdom is glorious, and never fadeth away, and is easily seen by them that love her, and is found by them that seek her.

    ☞R 13
    Wisdom is not scarce; the scarcity is love and seeking.

    14She preventeth them that covet her, so that she first sheweth herself unto them.

    ☞R 14
    Priority is inverted: the seeker is preceded; grace outruns appetite.

    15He that awaketh early to seek her, shall not labour: for he shall find her sitting at his door.

    ☞R 15
    She is found “sitting”: Wisdom waits; the door becomes a threshold of judgment.

    16To think, therefore, upon her, is perfect understanding: and he that watcheth for her, shall quickly be secure.

    ☞R 16
    Thinking becomes vigilance; contemplation is treated as security, not ornament.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 25:1-13

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 25:1-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Then shall the kingdom of heaven be like to ten virgins, who taking their lamps went out to meet the bridegroom and the bride.

    ☞G 1
    Lamps without oil: liturgy without interior reserve; procession without provision.

    2And five of them were foolish and five wise.

    3But the five foolish, having taken their lamps, did not take oil with them.

    4But the wise took oil in their vessels with the lamps.

    5And the bridegroom tarrying, they all slumbered and slept.

    ☞G 5
    All sleep: virtue is not insomnia; the test is what remains when consciousness drops.

    6And at midnight there was a cry made: Behold the bridegroom cometh. Go ye forth to meet him.

    ☞G 6
    Midnight: the summons arrives at the least convenient hour; readiness is preloaded.

    7Then all those virgins arose and trimmed their lamps.

    8And the foolish said to the wise: Give us of your oil, for our lamps are gone out.

    9The wise answered, saying: Lest perhaps there be not enough for us and for you, go ye rather to them that sell and buy for yourselves.

    ☞G 9
    Refusal is not cruelty but non-transferability: preparedness cannot be donated at the last minute.

    10Now whilst they went to buy the bridegroom came: and they that were ready went in with him to the marriage. And the door was shut.

    ☞G 10
    The shut door is the parable’s hard edge: time congeals into verdict.

    11But at last came also the other virgins, saying: Lord, Lord, open to us.

    12But he answering said: Amen I say to you, I know you not.

    ☞G 12
    “I know you not”: exclusion framed as failed recognition, not failed effort.

    13Watch ye therefore, because you know not the day nor the hour.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGrace offers Wisdom ahead of our search, but entry into the kingdom requires a non-transferable readiness formed before the summons. Judgment is the moment when delayed desire meets a shut door.

    In Lectionem

    • R 13,15The passage argues that Wisdom is publicly available yet personally appropriated: she is “easily seen” and also encountered at one’s own threshold. The rhetoric collapses distance—seeking is met by presence—so the moral problem shifts from access to attention.
    • R 12,14,16Desire, initiative, and cognition are braided: the text treats rightly ordered longing as the engine of formation, while insisting that Wisdom’s initiative precedes human motion. The result is a doctrine of receptivity: instruction is not seized but received by those already turned toward it.

    In Evangelium

    • G 2,3,4The narrative divides humanity not by invitation but by preparation: all are in the wedding party, yet only some carry surplus. The “vessels” signal an interior capacity that must be filled before the crisis, not during it.
    • G 7,8,11,13The sequence—trim, request, plead—shows escalating urgency that cannot reverse prior neglect. The final imperative to watch is not about predicting the hour but about living in a state where recognition and entry are already secured.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Wisdom here is not a gentle teacher but a seducer with a schedule. “Covet” is the tell: desire is the engine, not virtue. She “prevents” you, gets there first, sits at your door like an ambush. The promise of “security” is the narcotic—keep thinking of her and you won’t feel the panic of contingency.

    Matthew turns that narcotic into dread. Everyone sleeps; the division isn’t moral alertness but private reserves. Oil is interior life hoarded in a flask, the part of you that can’t be borrowed at the last minute. The wise refuse to share not because they’re cold, but because the self can’t be portioned out without going dark. The market is a cruel joke: go buy what can’t be bought.

    The bridegroom’s “I know you not” lands like a diagnosis. You can perform readiness, carry a lamp, join the procession, even call him “Lord,” and still be unrecognizable when the door closes. The story doesn’t reward goodness; it rewards having enough hidden fuel to burn through delay. Desire either becomes a storehouse or it becomes a costume, and midnight exposes which one you are.

    Semina Verbi

    This desire for Wisdom that arrives before you do feels like the Dao that “comes” when you stop forcing it, and like Zen’s insistence that the sought is already at the gate when attention is clean. The lamp-and-oil divide reads psychologically as the difference between borrowed persona and metabolized practice; you can’t outsource readiness at the moment of demand.

    The midnight cry has the snap of Sufi wakefulness, the sudden summons that finds the heart either seasoned or merely excited; speculation, but the “oil” resembles baraka as an accumulated interior quality, not a transferable commodity. The shut door echoes the Bhagavad Gita’s emphasis on steady discipline over last-minute fervor, and the Stoic claim that character is forged in advance, not improvised under crisis.

    Wisdom “sitting at the door” also rhymes with the Upanishadic idea that the Self is nearer than near, and with Plato’s anamnesis, learning as remembering what was always available. Speculation: the harsh “I know you not” lands like the Buddhist mirror—reality reflects what is there, not what is pleaded for.
