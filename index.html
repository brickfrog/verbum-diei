    IIILectio1 Corinthians 15:35-37,42-49

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 15:35-37,42-49 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    35But some man will say: How do the dead rise again? Or with what manner of body shall they come?

    36Senseless man, that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die first.

    ☞R 36
    Life is gated by death; no bypass.

    37And that which thou sowest, thou sowest not the body that shall be: but bare grain, as of wheat, or of some of the rest.

    ☞R 37
    Continuity without identity: seed is not the body.

    42So also is the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption: it shall rise in incorruption.

    43It is sown in dishonour: it shall rise in glory. It is sown in weakness: it shall rise in power.

    44It is sown a natural body: it shall rise a spiritual body. If there be a natural body, there is also a spiritual body, as it is written:

    ☞R 44
    Two modes of embodiment; “spiritual” is not disembodied.

    45The first man Adam was made into a living soul; the last Adam into a quickening spirit.

    ☞R 45
    Adam ends as pattern; Christ begins as engine.

    46Yet that was not first which is spiritual, but that which is natural: afterwards that which is spiritual.

    47The first man was of the earth, earthly: the second man, from heaven, heavenly.

    ☞R 47
    Origin determines gravity: earth pulls down, heaven pulls up.

    48Such as is the earthly, such also are the earthly: and such as is the heavenly, such also are they that are heavenly.

    49Therefore, as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of the heavenly.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 8:4-15

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 8:4-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    4And when a very great multitude was gathered together and hastened out of the cities, unto him, he spoke by a similitude.

    5The sower went out to sow his seed. And as he sowed, some fell by the way side. And it was trodden down: and the fowls of the air devoured it.

    ☞G 5
    The path is public and compacted; exposure invites theft.

    6And other some fell upon a rock. And as soon as it was sprung up, it withered away, because it had no moisture.

    ☞G 6
    Speedy sprouting is a symptom; moisture is time.

    7And other some fell among thorns. And the thorns growing up with it, choked it.

    ☞G 7
    Thorns do not kill the seed; they outcompete it.

    8And other some fell upon good ground and, being sprung up, yielded fruit a hundredfold. Saying these things, he cried out: He that hath ears to hear, let him hear.

    9And his disciples asked him what this parable might be.

    10To whom he said: To you it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God; but to the rest in parables, that seeing they may not see and hearing may not understand.

    ☞G 10
    Parables are judgment as much as pedagogy.

    11Now the parable is this: The seed is the word of God.

    12And they by the way side are they that hear: then the devil cometh and taketh the word out of their heart, lest believing they should be saved.

    13Now they upon the rock are they who when they hear receive the word with joy: and these have no roots: for they believe for a while and in time of temptation they fall away.

    14And that which fell among thorns are they who have heard and, going their way, are choked with the cares and riches and pleasures of this life and yield no fruit.

    15But that on the good ground are they who in a good and perfect heart, hearing the word, keep it and bring forth fruit in patience.

    ☞G 15
    Fruit is delayed; patience is the proof of reception.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisResurrection is the divine reconstitution of embodied life under the heavenly Adam, not the revival of the old mode. The word of God saves only where it is kept through time, resisting theft, shallowness, and rival loves.

    In Lectionem

    • R 35,42,43,46,48,49Paul answers a curiosity about mechanics by shifting to ontology: resurrection is not repair but transposition. The sequence natural→spiritual establishes an ordered economy rather than a dualism, and the Adam/Christ contrast frames humanity as participation in a head: what you are “in” determines what you become. The imperatives culminate in imaging—ethics as eschatological conformity, not self-improvement.

    In Evangelium

    • G 4,8,9,11,12,13Luke stages the word as an event that divides a crowd. The explanation turns the parable into a diagnostic of hearing: the same word encounters different interior regimes, and the adversary is named as an active counter-agent. The disciples’ access is not merit but grant; interpretation itself is part of the kingdom’s administration.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul and Luke share a cruel image: nothing grows without consent to loss. The seed must be buried, the body must be “sown,” and what rises is not a repaired version of what you loved but something alien that uses death as its technique. Paul calls the question “senseless” because it clings to continuity, to the fantasy that the self can keep its familiar skin.

    The parable sharpens the dread: most seed is wasted. Trampled, scorched, strangled. The sower is extravagant to the point of indifference, scattering into failure as if failure is the normal cost of speech. “To the rest in parables” reads like a deliberate withholding: language as a filter that lets desire approach and then frustrates it, so that only the ones who can endure not-understanding stay near.

    “Good ground” is not moral superiority; it is a temperament that can be invaded and reorganized. The word does not console; it takes root, displaces, and demands patience—the slow violence of becoming someone else. Resurrection, then, is not reward after death but the psyche’s terrifying promise: if you let yourself be planted, you will live, but you will not be the person who wanted to live.

    Semina Verbi

    Speculation: Paul’s seed that “dies” into a different body brushes the Upanishadic image of the self shedding forms as it passes through states, not as a crude reassembly but as a transfiguration of mode. The parable’s soils resemble Buddhist accounts of karmic “seeds” (bīja) that ripen only when conditions are hospitable; distraction and craving are thorns, shallow enthusiasm is rock, steady attention is good earth. Stoic moral psychology also fits: impressions arrive like seed, but only the trained hegemonikon can hold, assent, and let virtue fruit; otherwise passions or social noise snatch it away. In Sufism, the heart as tilth and remembrance as irrigation echoes the moisture and patience required for ripening, with heedlessness as the bird that steals what never sank in.
