    IIILectioDaniel 7:9-10,13-14

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Daniel

    Daniel 7:9-10,13-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    9I beheld till thrones were placed, and the ancient of days sat: his garment was white as snow, and the hair of his head like clean wool: his throne like flames of fire: the wheels of it like a burning fire.

    ☞R 9
    White garment; fire-throne: purity seated on combustion.

    10A swift stream of fire issued forth from before him: thousands of thousands ministered to him, and ten thousand times a hundred thousand stood before him: the judgment sat, and the books were opened.

    ☞R 10
    Administration without intimacy: numbers, court, books—impersonal verdict.

    13I beheld, therefore, in the vision of the night, and lo, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and he came even to the ancient of days: and they presented him before him.

    ☞R 13
    “Like” the Son of man: resemblance that still exceeds taxonomy; arrival on clouds, not roads.

    14And he gave him power, and glory, and a kingdom: and all peoples, tribes, and tongues shall serve him: his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken away: and his kingdom that shall not be destroyed.

    ☞R 14
    Kingdom defined by negations: not taken, not destroyed—duration as dominion.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 17:1-9

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 17:1-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And after six days Jesus taketh unto him Peter and James, and John his brother, and bringeth them up into a high mountain apart:

    2And he was transfigured before them. And his face did shine as the sun: and his garments became white as snow.

    ☞G 2
    Sun-face, snow-garments: Daniel’s court imagery migrates onto a body.

    3And behold there appeared to them Moses and Elias talking with him.

    4And Peter answering, said to Jesus: Lord, it is good for us to be here: if thou wilt, let us make here three tabernacles, one for thee, and one for Moses, and one for Elias.

    ☞G 4
    Three tents: piety’s reflex to domesticate glory into equal stalls.

    5And as he was yet speaking, behold a bright cloud overshadowed them. And lo a voice out of the cloud, saying: This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased: hear ye him.

    ☞G 5
    Cloud interrupts speech; the command is auditory: “hear ye him,” not “look.”

    6And the disciples hearing fell upon their face, and were very much afraid.

    7And Jesus came and touched them: and said to them: Arise, and fear not.

    8And they lifting up their eyes, saw no one, but only Jesus.

    ☞G 8
    After the spectacle, the remainder is singular: only Jesus.

    9And as they came down from the mountain, Jesus charged them, saying: Tell the vision to no man, till the Son of man be risen from the dead.

    ☞G 9
    Secrecy tethered to resurrection: vision without cross becomes propaganda.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe Father publicly installs the Son as the sole hearable authority, and that authority is intelligible only through the resurrection that follows the cross.

    In Lectionem

    • R 9,13Daniel’s scene is not a private mystical ascent but a public transfer of rule: the court is convened, then the humanlike figure is formally presented and invested. The vision’s logic is juridical—authority is conferred, not seized—so the “Son of man” functions as the legitimate bearer of divine governance within history.
    • R 10,14The opened books and the universal service frame the kingdom as moral and cosmic order, not mere expansion. Everlastingness here is not timeless abstraction; it is the stability of a verdict that outlasts every rival regime.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,3Matthew stages a controlled disclosure to a restricted witness set, then immediately binds it with a prohibition. Moses and Elijah appear as the Law and the Prophets rendered secondary by proximity: they converse, but they do not authorize; the voice does.
    • G 6,7The narrative turns from radiance to fear to touch: revelation produces collapse, and Christ’s first act is not explanation but restoration to standing. The mountain is not an escape from the world; it is a calibration of perception for what will follow below.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Daniel’s courtroom is not comfort; it’s a machine. Fire is the furniture, wheels under the throne like a mobile furnace, a bureaucracy of terror. The books open the way a body opens under scrutiny: everything written down, nothing forgotten. “Ancient of days” reads less like tenderness than like the oldest appetite for order, the cold ecstasy of judging.

    Then the “Son of man” arrives on clouds like a figure promoted by the system, not its opponent. He is presented, authorized, handed the kingdom. Desire here is for a face that can survive the blaze, a human shape that can wear the verdict and make it look like glory.

    On the mountain, the transfiguration repeats the same aesthetic: white as snow, bright cloud, the sun-face. It’s not revelation as intimacy; it’s revelation as exposure. Moses and Elijah appear like witnesses summoned to authenticate the transfer of power. Peter’s instinct is to build structures, to domesticate the shock into shelters, to turn dread into real estate.

    The voice interrupts him. “Hear ye him” is not soothing; it is command, the same courtroom logic now lodged in a single person. The disciples fall because the body knows what the mind denies: this beauty is dangerous. Jesus’ touch doesn’t cancel fear; it manages it, like a hand on a trembling animal.

    And the secrecy on the way down is the final tell. This vision is not meant to console the crowd; it is meant to bind the witnesses. They’ve seen how authority is made: through spectacle, through terror, through a human figure set inside the fire and called beloved. They must carry that desire and that dread until it becomes death, and only then speak.

    Semina Verbi

    Daniel’s court of fire and opened books has the same psychic architecture as the Egyptian weighing of the heart and the Tibetan Bardo’s scrutiny: the self exposed to an impersonal clarity that cannot be bargained with. Speculation: the “books” function like a mythic memory palace, a way cultures externalize conscience and the terror of being finally readable.

    The “Ancient of Days” beside “one like a son of man” echoes the Hindu pairing of the timeless ground (Brahman) with its manifest face (Ishvara), and the Daoist sense of the nameless Dao giving rise to named forms; not equivalence, but a shared intuition that ultimacy can be both beyond image and yet encountered. Speculation: the doubled figure is a narrative solution to the mind’s need for both awe and address.

    The mountain transfiguration parallels the Upanishadic “light of lights,” the Sufi tajalli (divine self-disclosure), and Zen kenshō: a sudden intensification where the ordinary person is seen as more real than the usual self. The whiteness and radiance read like a cross-cultural symbol for unshadowed perception—purity not as moral tidiness but as undivided attention.

    Moses and Elijah appearing as living interlocutors resembles ancestor-presence in Confucian and Yoruba worlds: tradition not as dead rule but as a speaking continuity. Peter’s urge to build shelters matches the universal reflex to institutionalize peak experience; Buddhism’s warning about attachment to jhāna and the desert fathers’ suspicion of consolations both recognize the same trap.

    The cloud and the voice align with the “cloud of unknowing” in apophatic mysticism and with the Sinai motif of hiddenness: revelation arrives as concealment. “Hear him” sounds like the Sikh emphasis on śabad (the heard Word) and the Qur’anic primacy of recitation—truth as something received by listening rather than seized by sight.

    “Tell no one” resonates with esoteric restraint in the Eleusinian mysteries and in Vajrayana’s guarded transmission: some knowledge deforms when turned into display. Speculation: secrecy here protects the psyche from converting terror and wonder into social capital.
