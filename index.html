    IIILectioJeremiah 26:1-9

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

    Jeremiah 26:1-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1In the beginning of the reign of Joakim the son of Josias king of Juda, came this word from the Lord, saying:

    2Thus saith the Lord: stand in the court of the house of the Lord, and speak to all the cities of Juda, out of which they come, to adore in the house of the Lord, all the words which I have commanded thee to speak unto them: leave not out one word.

    ☞R 2
    Public court, not private vision: the word is staged.

    3If so be they will hearken and be converted every one from his evil way; that I may repent me of the evil that I think to do unto them for the wickedness of their doings.

    ☞R 3
    Divine “repent” is conditional judgment, not fickleness.

    4And thou shalt say to them: Thus saith the Lord: If you will not hearken to me to walk in my law, which I have given you:

    5To give ear to the words of my servants the prophets, whom I sent to you rising up early: and sending, and you have not hearkened:

    6I will make this house like Silo: and I will make this city a curse to all the nations of the earth.

    ☞R 6
    Temple threatened with Shiloh: sacred history turned into warning label.

    7And the priests, and the prophets, and all the people heard Jeremias speaking these words in the house of the Lord.

    8And when Jeremias had made an end of speaking all that the Lord had commanded him to speak to all the people, the priests, and the prophets, and all the people laid hold on him, saying: Let him be put to death.

    ☞R 8
    Completion triggers seizure: obedience finishes the trap.

    9Why hath he prophesied in the name of the Lord, saying: This house shall be like Silo; and this city shall be made desolate, without an inhabitant? And all the people were gathered together against Jeremias in the house of the Lord.

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 13:54-58 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    54And coming into his own country, he taught them in their synagogues, so that they wondered and said: How came this man by this wisdom and miracles?

    ☞G 54
    Amazement without submission: wonder as a cul-de-sac.

    55Is not this the carpenter's son? Is not his mother called Mary, and his brethren James, and Joseph, and Simon, and Jude: His brethren... These were the children of Mary the wife of Cleophas, sister to our Blessed Lady, (St. Matt. 27. 56; St. John 19. 25,) and therefore, according to the usual style of the Scripture, they were called brethren, that is, near relations to our Saviour.

    ☞G 55
    They inventory his kin to cancel his authority.

    56And his sisters, are they not all with us? Whence therefore hath he all these things?

    ☞G 56
    They inventory his kin to cancel his authority.

    57And they were scandalized in his regard. But Jesus said to them: A prophet is not without honour, save in his own country, and in his own house.

    ☞G 57
    Proverb names the pattern: proximity breeds contempt, not clarity.

    58And he wrought not many miracles there, because of their unbelief.

    ☞G 58
    Unbelief throttles signs: miracles are not forced on closed eyes.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s word judges the institutions that host it, and those most familiar with the holy are most capable of weaponizing that familiarity against the prophet. Unbelief does not merely miss miracles; it constrains the economy of signs and turns revelation into offense.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,4,5The scene is dated to a regime change, then framed as covenant litigation: law given, prophets sent, refusal recorded. The charge is not ignorance but sustained noncompliance under repeated summons.
    • R 7,9The hearing becomes a tribunal inside the sanctuary: worshippers convert the house of prayer into a court against the word. The crowd’s unity is achieved by targeting the messenger rather than answering the indictment.

    In Evangelium

    • G 54,57,58Jesus’ teaching in the synagogue exposes a local hermeneutic: they can register “wisdom and miracles” yet refuse the conclusion those signs demand. The saying about the prophet interprets the rejection as structural—honor is withheld where familiarity claims ownership.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Jeremiah is ordered to “leave not out one word,” and that insistence is the point: the message is not medicine but an instrument. It is meant to scrape the nerve. The threat—temple like Shiloh, city a curse—is less prediction than humiliation, an attack on the people’s erotic bond with their own permanence. They do not seize him because he is wrong; they seize him because he says aloud what they secretly fear, that the sacred building is a prop and can be taken away. Their piety is revealed as possession anxiety.

    Jesus returns home and meets the same psychology in a softer key. Familiarity is not comfort; it is control. “Carpenter’s son” is a leash: stay small, stay ours, don’t exceed the story we assigned you. So the town’s scandal is not moral outrage but wounded entitlement, the rage of people denied the right to define you. He does few miracles not because he lacks power but because wonder cannot be forced on those who prefer the safety of contempt. In both scenes the crowd chooses death over desire: kill the voice that destabilizes the shrine, or shrink the neighbor who threatens to become more than a neighbor. The real unbelief is not intellectual; it is the refusal to risk being changed.

    Semina Verbi

    Jeremiah’s mandate to “leave not out one word” has the hard edge of satya in Hindu thought: truth as a vow that costs you social safety; speculation: the temple court functions like a public dharma-sabha where speech is judged less by accuracy than by its threat to order. The crowd’s reflex to kill the speaker echoes the Athenian pattern around Socrates, and the Buddhist motif of the kalyāṇa-mitta who is rejected because he wounds collective self-deception.

    Nazareth’s “carpenter’s son” contempt matches Zen’s suspicion of the too-familiar: the ordinary face is dismissed precisely because it is ordinary; speculation: the scandal is a defense against intimacy with the uncanny. The line about miracles withheld by unbelief parallels the Taoist sense that efficacy (de) is not forced on a closed heart, and the Sufi idea that baraka is veiled by denial, not absent.
