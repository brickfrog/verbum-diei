    IIILectioSirach 48:1-14

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Sirach

    Sirach 48:1-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And Elias the prophet stood up, as a fire, and his word burnt like a torch.

    ☞R 1
    Prophet as combustion: person becomes element; speech becomes flame.

    2He brought a famine upon them, and they that provoked him in their envy, were reduced to a small number, for they could not endure the commandments of the Lord.

    3By the word of the Lord he shut up the heaven, and he brought down fire from heaven thrice.

    ☞R 3
    Heaven “shut up”: drought as liturgical censure; fire answers fire.

    4Thus was Elias magnified in his wondrous works. And who can glory like to thee?

    5Who raisedst up a dead man from below, from the lot of death, by the word of the Lord God.

    ☞R 5
    Resurrection is attributed to “word,” not technique; command outruns death’s allotment.

    6Who broughtest down kings to destruction, and brokest easily their power in pieces, and the glorious from their bed.

    7Who heardest judgment in Sina, and in Horeb the judgments of vengeance.

    8Who anointedst kings to penance, and madest prophets successors after thee.

    9Who wast taken up in a whirlwind of fire, in a chariot of fiery horses.

    ☞R 9
    Ascent is not escape but translation: fire carries fire.

    10Who art registered in the judgments of times to appease the wrath of the Lord, to reconcile the heart of the father to the son, and to restore the tribes of Jacob.

    11Blessed are they that saw thee, and were honoured with thy friendship.

    12For we live only in our life, but after death our name shall not be such.

    13Elias was indeed covered with the whirlwind, and his spirit was filled up in Eliseus: in his days he feared not the prince, and no man was more powerful than he.

    14No word could overcome him, and after death his body prophesied.

    ☞R 14
    Corpse still speaks: prophecy outlives breath; death fails to silence office.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 6:7-15

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 6:7-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    7And when you are praying, speak not much, as the heathens. For they think that in their much speaking they may be heard.

    ☞G 7
    Prayer is not verbosity; pagan logic treats God as a mechanism to be triggered.

    8Be not you therefore like to them for your Father knoweth what is needful for you, before you ask him.

    ☞G 8
    Foreknowledge of need strips prayer of informing; it becomes alignment, not briefing.

    9Thus therefore shall you pray: Our Father who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name.

    10Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven.

    11Give us this day our supersubstantial bread. Supersubstantial bread... In St. Luke the same word is rendered daily bread. It is understood of the bread of life, which we receive in the Blessed Sacrament.

    ☞G 11
    Bread named beyond substance: request is for a life that exceeds calories.

    12And forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors.

    ☞G 12
    Debt-language: sin is obligation incurred; forgiveness is release, not amnesia.

    13And lead us not into temptation. But deliver us from evil. Amen. Lead us not into temptation... That is, suffer us not to be overcome by temptation.

    14For if you will forgive men their offences, your heavenly Father will forgive you also your offences.

    15But if you will not forgive men, neither will your Father forgive you your offences.

    ☞G 15
    Refusal to forgive installs a closed circuit: you keep what you demand to be remitted.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s word governs history through appointed servants, and God’s prayer governs the petitioner by binding divine pardon to human pardon.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2,4The passage constructs Elijah as a public instrument of divine governance: scarcity, judgment, and reversal of royal security are presented as the Lord’s enforcement executed through a man. The repeated “who…” is a litany of delegated authority, culminating in a role that exceeds his lifetime.
    • R 10,13Elijah is framed as eschatological function: “registered” for appointed times, tasked with turning hearts and restoring tribes. Yet the same spirit is said to be “filled up” in Elisha, making continuity—not nostalgia—the point: the office persists by transmission.

    In Evangelium

    • G 9,10,13,14The prayer is structured as a reordering of loyalties: name, kingdom, will precede any petition, and the final plea concerns moral vulnerability rather than circumstance. The appended condition on forgiveness makes the prayer juridical: it binds the petitioner to the very mercy invoked.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Sirach makes Elijah a pure instrument of appetite: fire that speaks, speech that burns. He doesn’t persuade; he reduces. Famine, lightning, kings snapped in half, the bed invaded. Holiness here is not gentleness but a kind of sanctioned violence, the pleasure of seeing power humiliated and the world made small enough to obey. Even his “friendship” is a dangerous honor, proximity to a man who can call down weather.

    Then Matthew turns and forbids the very thing Elijah embodies: incantation, volume, the fantasy that enough words can bend heaven. The Lord’s Prayer is not a spell but a surrender, a tightening of desire into a few hard asks: bread, release, rescue. Yet the catch is psychological and brutal: forgiveness is not a virtue but a gate that only opens from inside. If you cling to your grievance, you lock yourself out and call it justice.

    Read together, Elijah is the dream of external control—make the sky comply, make enemies vanish—while the prayer drags the battlefield inward. The real famine is the one you feed with resentment. The real fire is the one you keep rehearsing in your mouth. God already knows; the prayer is for you, to stop enjoying your wrath long enough to be delivered from it.

    Semina Verbi

    Elijah as “fire” reads like the psyche’s purgative force: the part of a person that refuses compromise with self-deception and therefore scorches the social order built on it. That refusal can look like famine and vengeance because it starves the old appetites; the violence is the image of inner detox, not mere cruelty. The whirlwind ascent is the fantasy of a life so integrated with its own imperative that it no longer needs ordinary continuity; it becomes pure trajectory, leaving a successor as the residue of its intensity.

    The prayer’s insistence on few words is an attack on magical thinking: the anxious mind believes verbosity can purchase safety. “Father” names a trustful attachment stance; “hallowed” is the ego learning reverence for what it cannot control. “Forgive as you forgive” is psychological realism: resentment is a self-administered toxin, and the refusal to release another person keeps the self locked in the same punitive economy. “Lead us not into temptation” is the adult admission that willpower is finite and that environments, habits, and cues matter.

    Semina Verbi: Elijah’s fiery purification parallels Shiva as destroyer of illusion in Shaivism, and the Taoist image of true power acting like a consuming clarity rather than coercion; speculation: the whirlwind resembles the shamanic ascent motif where the self is “taken” into a higher register to return as medicine. The short prayer echoes Zen’s suspicion of discursive grasping and the Sufi preference for dhikr that simplifies the heart; “forgive to be forgiven” meets Buddhist metta and the Stoic practice of releasing injury as a way to free one’s own ruling faculty.
