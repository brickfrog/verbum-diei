    IIILectioIsaiah 5:1-7

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Isaiah

    Isaiah 5:1-7 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1I will sing to my beloved the canticle of my cousin concerning his vineyard. My beloved had a vineyard on a hill in a fruitful place.

    ☞R 1
    Love-song as lawsuit: lyric opens into indictment.

    2And he fenced it in, and picked the stones out of it, and planted it with the choicest vines, and built a tower in the midst thereof, and set up a winepress therein: and he looked that it should bring forth grapes, and it brought forth wild grapes.

    ☞R 2
    Total provision—hedge, tower, press—yet the yield is counterfeit.

    3And now, O ye inhabitants of Jerusalem, and ye men of Juda, judge between me and my vineyard.

    ☞R 3
    The accused are drafted as jurors; self-condemnation is the trap.

    4What is there that I ought to do more to my vineyard, that I have not done to it? was it that I looked that it should bring forth grapes, and it hath brought forth wild grapes?

    5And now I will shew you what I will do to my vineyard. I will take away the hedge thereof, and it shall be wasted: I will break down the wall thereof, and it shall be trodden down.

    6And I will make it desolate: it shall not be pruned, and it shall not be digged: but briers and thorns shall come up: and I will command the clouds to rain no rain upon it.

    ☞R 6
    Judgment is subtraction: no pruning, no digging, no rain—creation reversed.

    7For the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel: and the man of Juda, his pleasant plant: and I looked that he should do judgment, and behold iniquity: and do justice, and behold a cry.

    ☞R 7
    The punchline is ethical: expected justice; received a scream.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 21:33-43

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 21:33-43 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    33Hear ye another parable. There was a man, an householder, who planted a vineyard and made a hedge round about it and dug in it a press and built a tower and let it out to husbandmen and went into a strange country.

    ☞G 33
    Same vineyard, new scandal: stewardship without presence breeds theft.

    34And when the time of the fruits drew nigh, he sent his servants to the husbandmen that they might receive the fruits thereof.

    35And the husbandmen laying hands on his servants, beat one and killed another and stoned another.

    36Again he sent other servants, more than the former; and they did to them in like manner.

    37And last of all he sent to them his son, saying: They will reverence my son.

    ☞G 37
    “They will reverence my son”: a calculated exposure of contempt.

    38But the husbandmen seeing the son, said among themselves: This is the heir: come, let us kill him, and we shall have his inheritance.

    ☞G 38
    Inheritance logic: murder as a title-deed.

    39And taking him, they cast him forth out of the vineyard and killed him.

    ☞G 39
    Cast out, then killed: exclusion precedes execution.

    40When therefore the lord of the vineyard shall come, what will he do to those husbandmen?

    41They say to him: He will bring those evil men to an evil end and let out his vineyard to other husbandmen that shall render him the fruit in due season.

    42Jesus saith to them: Have you never read in the Scriptures: The stone which the builders rejected, the same is become the head of the corner? By the Lord this has been done; and it is wonderful in our eyes.

    ☞G 42
    Rejected stone becomes corner: the builders’ verdict is overruled.

    43Therefore I say to you that the kingdom of God shall be taken from you and shall be given to a nation yielding the fruits thereof.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s election is ordered to justice and fruit; when the stewards reject the Son and withhold the yield, the kingdom is removed and reassigned by divine right.

    In Lectionem

    • R 4,5The oracle turns on a measured question—what more could be done?—then answers with a measured dismantling. The punishment fits the form of the gift: protections and cultivation are withdrawn, not because the land failed, but because the people refused the purpose of the planting.

    In Evangelium

    • G 34,35,36,40,41,43The parable escalates from rent-collection to regime-change. The tenants’ violence against messengers establishes a pattern; the audience supplies the sentence, and Jesus converts their verdict into a transfer of the vineyard. The issue is not mere misconduct but a refusal to render fruit to the owner, so the kingdom passes to fruit-bearers.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    The vineyard is not a moral project that failed; it is a love affair that curdled into surveillance. The beloved plants, fences, towers, presses, then waits for sweetness with the anxious entitlement of someone who has invested too much. “Wild grapes” are not mere defects; they are the vineyard’s refusal to taste the way it was designed to taste. The song becomes a lawsuit because tenderness can’t bear being unanswered.

    The threat to remove the hedge is less punishment than abandonment. If you won’t be what I imagined, I will stop tending you and call it justice. The drought is the lover’s last weapon: to make the beloved feel the absence that the beloved has already inflicted.

    In the parable the tenants are not simply wicked; they are people who have lived under an owner’s distant claim until the claim feels unreal, then suddenly invasive. The servants arrive like bills, audits, reminders that the fruit was never theirs. Violence is the grotesque logic of possession: if we destroy the messenger, we can keep the harvest without the shame of owing it.

    The son is the most perverse move. He is sent not because it will work, but because the owner needs the drama of reverence, needs his desire to be recognized to risk blood. The tenants kill him to end the story, to sever the gaze that makes them tenants. The “stone rejected” is not a pious twist; it is the revelation that the whole economy runs on rejection and replacement. The kingdom is taken and given elsewhere not as clean judgment, but as the cold fact that love, once spurned, becomes a machine that finds new bodies to feed it fruit.

    Semina Verbi

    This vineyard is a psyche built with care, then betrayed by its own stewards. The fence, tower, and press are not decorations; they are structures of attention and discipline meant to turn raw life into wine. When the yield comes back “wild,” it is not ignorance but refusal: the inner managers choose appetite, status, and violence over the hard, ordinary work of justice. The punishment is not a tantrum; it is the withdrawal of sustaining conditions. Remove the hedge and you don’t need to “cause” ruin—neglect is enough. The text’s terror is that grace can be structurally offered and still be structurally squandered.

    The son’s murder is the climax of self-deception: the fantasy that killing the claim will erase the claimant. It is the mind’s attempt to own the vineyard by silencing the voice that names it. The “stone rejected” is the disowned part of the self—truth, conscience, the inconvenient heir—that returns as the cornerstone. What you exile becomes architecture anyway, either as integration or as collapse.

    Semina Verbi: In Greek tragedy, especially the Oresteia, violated order breeds a cycle where blood answers blood until a higher judgment breaks it; here too the vineyard’s crisis is juridical before it is agricultural. In Buddhism’s account of karma, (speculation) “no rain” resembles conditions withdrawing when wholesome causes are refused; the world stops cooperating with a mind that won’t learn. In the Bhagavad Gita, the demand for fruit without duty is condemned; the tenants want inheritance without stewardship. In Confucian thought, the “rectification of names” parallels the son’s claim: reality insists on being called what it is, and violence against the name does not change the thing. In Sufism, (speculation) the beloved’s garden evokes the heart as a cultivated place; when the nafs usurps it, the garden turns thorny until surrender restores fruit.
