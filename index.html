    IIILectio1 Corinthians 5:1-8

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 5:1-8 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1It is absolutely heard that there is fornication among you and such fornication as the like is not among the heathens: that one should have his father's wife.

    ☞R 1
    The scandal is not pagan excess but Christian tolerance; the sin is named without euphemism.

    2And you are puffed up and have not rather mourned: that he might be taken away from among you that hath done this thing.

    ☞R 2
    Pride where grief should be: a community anesthetized to its own rot.

    3I indeed, absent in body but present in spirit, have already judged, as though I were present, him that hath so done,

    4In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, you being gathered together and my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus:

    5To deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ.

    ☞R 5
    Excommunication is framed as medicine: Satan as instrument, flesh as the casualty, spirit as the target.

    6Your glorying is not good. Know you not that a little leaven corrupteth the whole lump?

    ☞R 6
    Leaven is small, totalizing, and indifferent to intentions; corruption spreads by contact.

    7Purge out the old leaven, that you may be a new paste, as you are unleavened. For Christ our pasch is sacrificed.

    ☞R 7
    Identity precedes command: “you are unleavened,” therefore act like it; Pasch turns ethics into liturgy.

    8Therefore, let us feast, not with the old leaven, nor with the leaven of malice and wickedness: but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 6:6-11

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 6:6-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    6And it came to pass also, on another sabbath, that he entered into the synagogue and taught. And there was a man whose right hand was withered.

    7And the scribes and Pharisees watched if he would heal on the sabbath: that they might find an accusation against him.

    ☞G 7
    They watch for healing as if it were contraband; piety becomes surveillance.

    8But he knew their thoughts and said to the man who had the withered hand: Arise and stand forth in the midst. And rising he stood forth.

    ☞G 8
    He drags the hidden test into the center; the man becomes a public sign.

    9Then Jesus said to them: I ask you, if it be lawful on the sabbath days to do good or to do evil? To save life or to destroy?

    ☞G 9
    Sabbath is reduced to a binary: good/evil, save/destroy; neutrality is excluded.

    10And looking round about on them all, he said to the man: Stretch forth thy hand. And he stretched it forth. And his hand was restored.

    11And they were filled with madness: and they talked one with another, what they might do to Jesus.

    ☞G 11
    A restored hand provokes “madness”: the miracle exposes not weakness but hostility.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe holiness of the community is guarded by judgment and enacted in worship; mercy and discipline are not rivals but the same divine claim against corruption and death.

    In Lectionem

    • R 3,4Paul asserts jurisdiction across distance: apostolic authority is not local sentiment but a trans-local court convened “in the name” and “with power.” The assembly is not a town meeting; it is an organ of Christ’s rule.
    • R 8The feast-image relocates moral discipline into covenant time: the community’s table must match its sacrifice. The issue is not private vice but sacramental contradiction—celebration with ingredients that deny what is being celebrated.

    In Evangelium

    • G 6,10The healing is staged as a legal demonstration inside the synagogue’s teaching space: restoration functions as interpretation. Jesus does not merely break a rule; he redefines what the day signifies by making wholeness the sabbath’s proper work.
    • G 7,11The opponents’ logic is forensic: they seek an “accusation,” then plot action. The narrative shows how law, when detached from mercy, becomes a machine that manufactures guilt and then justifies violence.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul is terrified of pleasure that refuses to stay private. The scandal isn’t sex; it’s that the community can look at it without grief. Their “glorying” is the real infection: a smug tolerance that makes desire sovereign and turns the group into an audience. So he stages a counter-ritual, a violent exorcism of ambiguity. “Deliver to Satan” is not theology but psychology: exile as shock therapy, the body punished so the group can feel clean again. The leaven image gives it away; he wants a bread without fermentation, without the wildness that makes dough rise. He wants sincerity and truth, meaning a self that doesn’t swell.

    Jesus does the opposite. He heals in public on purpose, not despite the trap but because of it. He makes the withered hand stand in the middle like an exposed wound, then forces everyone to watch the law fail to protect life. The Pharisees’ “madness” is the rage of people whose control depends on delay. Jesus’ question is a knife: the sabbath can be used to destroy by doing nothing. The restored hand is not just mercy; it’s an accusation made flesh, desire for wholeness breaking the schedule.

    Both texts circle the same dread: what happens when the body won’t obey the community’s timing. Paul wants to purge desire to preserve the feast. Jesus ignites desire to reveal the community’s rot. The tension is that holiness can look like sanitation or like sabotage, and both can claim “truth” while someone’s body is made the battleground.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “leaven” reads like the Stoic diagnosis of moral contagion: one unexamined vice becomes a habit, a habit becomes a climate, and soon the group’s pride is just collective self-deception. The surgical removal of the offender resembles the Greek pharmakos ritual, a scapegoat expelled to cleanse the city; speculation: Paul spiritualizes that civic purge into an inner therapy aimed at breaking the body’s momentum so the person can return.

    “Deliver to Satan” has an uncanny parallel in Tibetan Buddhism’s wrathful-compassion imagery, where fierce forms appear to destroy what binds, not the person; speculation: the violence is symbolic of interrupting craving’s circuitry. The “unleavened” ideal of sincerity and truth echoes the Upanishadic and yogic pursuit of satya and tapas: truthfulness and heat that burns residue, not as moralism but as a technology of clarity.

    In the synagogue scene, Jesus’ question cuts like a Zen koan: law without life is a dead answer, and the trap is exposed by a simple act. The withered hand is the psyche’s learned helplessness made visible, and the command “stretch” functions like a therapeutic paradox, akin to Sufi tales where the impossible instruction unlocks a frozen will. The observers’ rage matches Nietzsche’s ressentiment: when someone heals without asking permission, the guardians of status feel their authority wither, and they call that injury “justice.”
