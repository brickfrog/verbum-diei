    IIILectioEphesians 6:10-20

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 6:10-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    10Finally, brethren, be strengthened in the Lord and in the might of his power.

    ☞R 10
    Strength is not sourced in the self; it is borrowed power.

    11Put you on the armour of God, that you may be able to stand against the deceits of the devil.

    ☞R 11
    The command is to stand, not to roam; the battlefield is fixed.

    12For our wrestling is not against flesh and blood; but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of the world of this darkness, against the spirits of wickedness in the high places.

    ☞R 12
    The enemy is administrative: ranks, jurisdictions, a dark bureaucracy.

    13Therefore, take unto you the armour of God, that you may be able to resist in the evil day and to stand in all things perfect.

    14Stand therefore, having your loins girt about with truth and having on the breastplate of justice:

    ☞R 14
    Truth is a belt: it holds everything else together or nothing fits.

    15And your feet shod with the preparation of the gospel of peace.

    ☞R 15
    Peace is footwear: not sentiment, but traction for advance.

    16In all things taking the shield of faith, wherewith you may be able to extinguish all the fiery darts of the most wicked one.

    ☞R 16
    Faith is a shield because accusation arrives as projectiles.

    17And take unto you the helmet of salvation and the sword of the Spirit (which is the word of God).

    ☞R 17
    Only one weapon is issued; it is not steel but speech.

    18By all prayer and supplication praying at all times in the spirit: and in the same watching with all instance and supplication for all the saints:

    19And for me, that speech may be given me, that I may open my mouth with confidence, to make known the mystery of the gospel,

    20For which I am an ambassador in a chain: so that therein I may be bold to speak according as I ought.

    ☞R 20
    Chains do not cancel office; they certify the embassy.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 13:31-35

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 13:31-35 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    31The same day, there came some of the Pharisees, saying to him: Depart, and get thee hence, for Herod hath a mind to kill thee.

    32And he said to them: Go and tell that fox: Behold, I cast out devils and do cures, to-day and to-morrow, and the third day I am consummated.

    ☞G 32
    Herod is named by species: predator reduced to a small animal.

    33Nevertheless, I must walk to-day and to-morrow and the day following, because it cannot be that a prophet perish, out of Jerusalem.

    ☞G 33
    Necessity governs the route; danger does not.

    34Jerusalem, Jerusalem, that killest the prophets; and stonest them that are sent to thee, how often would I have gathered thy children as the bird doth her brood under her wings, and thou wouldest not?

    ☞G 34
    Wings offered; refusal is the city’s defining act.

    35Behold your house shall be left to you desolate. And I say to you that you shall not see me till the time come when you shall say: Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord.

    ☞G 35
    Desolation is not mere punishment; it is abandonment to one’s choice.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s kingdom advances by steadfast witness under hostile powers, not by evasion or force. Judgment falls as the withdrawal of shelter from those who refuse the visitation of Christ.

    In Lectionem

    • R 13,18,19The passage frames Christian action as endurance under pressure: the goal is to remain upright through an appointed “evil day,” sustained by a regimen of vigilance and intercession. Paul’s request for speech locates the decisive contest in disclosure of the “mystery”: the church’s warfare is fought by public articulation, not by seizure of control.

    In Evangelium

    • G 31,33,35Luke sets political threat beside prophetic necessity: the warning about Herod functions as background noise against a trajectory that runs through Jerusalem’s history of rejecting messengers. The lament and the final conditional acclamation place judgment and future recognition on the same axis: the city’s fate turns on whether it will receive the one it expels.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “armor” is not heroism; it’s a panic protocol. He strips the enemy of a human face because the real threat is the invisible machinery that runs through everyone: moods, contagions, loyalties, the sweet logic that makes cruelty feel necessary. The gear he prescribes is a way to survive yourself. Truth cinches the body like a belt because without it you spill everywhere. Justice is a breastplate because guilt is the blade that always finds the ribs. Faith is a shield because the darts are thoughts, sudden and plausible, that set you on fire from the inside. And the “word of God” as sword is not piety but speech that cuts, the one thing that can interrupt the spell.

    Then Luke shows what that war looks like in daylight. Herod is a “fox,” not a dragon: cunning, petty, lethal in a small way. Jesus refuses the bait of fear and keeps his rhythm—today, tomorrow, the third day—like someone walking into an ending he has already accepted. The exorcisms and cures are not side-miracles; they are raids against the same darkness Paul names, but carried out in bodies.

    Jerusalem is the real battlefield, because it is the place that loves holiness and kills prophets. Jesus’ desire is almost embarrassing in its tenderness: a mother bird gathering children who won’t be gathered. The dread is that refusal is stronger than love. “Your house left desolate” is not punishment from above; it’s the natural consequence of choosing autonomy over shelter. The last line is the cruel hope: you will bless him, but only after absence teaches you what you refused to want.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s armor is a psychology of attention: the self is porous, and the real fight is for the inner theater where suggestion becomes consent. “Principalities” are not comic-book monsters but organized pressures—habits, crowds, appetites, ideologies—that impersonate necessity. Truth at the waist is the refusal to live split; justice at the chest is the trained heart that can bear impact without becoming what hits it. Peace under the feet is mobility without panic. Faith is not optimism but a practiced trust that interrupts intrusive images before they ignite. The “word” as sword is speech that cuts through self-deception, including one’s own. Prayer is the vigilance that keeps the whole system awake.

    Jesus answers threat with tempo, not flinch. He will not let Herod set the calendar. Calling him “fox” is moral diagnosis: cunning without courage. The lament over Jerusalem is the ache of love meeting refusal; the tragedy is not punishment but the vacuum left when protection is declined. “Under the wings” is a picture of shelter that does not coerce; desolation is what autonomy feels like when it has driven out tenderness. The end is not revenge but a delayed recognition: the city will speak blessing only after it has exhausted its defenses.

    Semina Verbi: the Stoics’ prosoche, attention as the battleground, resembles the armor’s disciplined interiority. In Buddhism, Mara’s “arrows” of craving and aversion parallel the fiery darts; speculation: the shield functions like mindfulness interrupting escalation. In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna’s counsel to stand firm amid unseen forces echoes wrestling beyond “flesh and blood.” In Sufism, the greater jihad against the nafs rhymes with resisting the inner tyrant; speculation: “fox” names the ego’s clever evasions. In Daoism, wu-wei’s unforced persistence resembles Jesus’ steady walking that refuses the enemy’s timetable.
