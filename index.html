    IIILectioActs 8:5-8,14-17

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 8:5-8,14-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    5And Philip, going down to the city of Samaria, preached Christ unto them.

    ☞R 5
    Samaria: the wrong place becomes the first field.

    6And the people with one accord were attentive to those things which were said by Philip, hearing, and seeing the miracles which he did.

    ☞R 6
    Attention is welded from word and spectacle; hearing needs seeing.

    7For many of them who had unclean spirits, crying with a loud voice, went out.

    ☞R 7
    Unclean spirits exit noisily; liberation is not polite.

    8And many, taken with the palsy, and that were lame, were healed.

    14Now, when the apostles, who were in Jerusalem, had heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent unto them Peter and John.

    15Who, when they were come, prayed for them that they might receive the Holy Ghost.

    16For he was not as yet come upon any of them: but they were only baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus.

    ☞R 16
    Baptized, yet not arrived: a deliberate gap in the economy.

    17Then they laid their hands upon them: and they received the Holy Ghost. They laid their hands upon them, etc... The apostles administered the sacrament of confirmation, by imposition of hands, and prayer; and the faithful thereby received the Holy Ghost. Not but they had received the grace of the Holy Ghost at their baptism: yet not that plenitude of grace and those spiritual gifts which they afterwards received from bishops in the sacrament of confirmation, which strengthened them to profess their faith publicly.

    ☞R 17
    Hands: the Church’s body becomes conduit; gift is mediated.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 14:15-21

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 14:15-21 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    15If you love me, keep my commandments.

    ☞G 15
    Love is measured by obedience, not heat.

    16And I will ask the Father: and he shall give you another Paraclete, that he may abide with you for ever: Paraclete... That is, a comforter: or also an advocate; inasmuch as by inspiring prayer, he prays, as it were, in us, and pleads for us. For ever... Hence it is evident that this Spirit of Truth was not only promised to the persons of the apostles, but also to their successors through all generations.

    ☞G 16
    “Another” Paraclete: presence by replacement, not return.

    17The spirit of truth, whom the world cannot receive, because it seeth him not, nor knoweth him. But you shall know him; because he shall abide with you and shall be in you.

    ☞G 17
    World’s incapacity is epistemic: no sight, no reception.

    18I will not leave you orphans: I will come to you.

    ☞G 18
    Orphans: absence is redefined as a mode of coming.

    19Yet a little while and the world seeth me no more. But you see me: because I live, and you shall live.

    20In that day you shall know that I am in my Father: and you in me, and I in you.

    ☞G 20
    Indwelling stacks: Father–Son–you; intimacy as structure.

    21He that hath my commandments and keepeth them; he it is that loveth me. And he that loveth me shall be loved of my Father: and I will love him and will manifest myself to him.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe Holy Ghost is given as Christ’s abiding presence, but he is received through ordered mediation and verified by obedient love.

    In Lectionem

    • R 14,15Jerusalem hears and sends: the mission is real, but it is also audited. The Spirit’s public bestowal is tied to apostolic visitation, binding new territory to the originating witnesses and preventing a free-floating Samarian church.

    In Evangelium

    • G 19,21The “world” loses sight while disciples gain a different visibility: life shared with the living Christ. The promised manifestation is conditional—not on mystical aptitude but on covenantal fidelity—so revelation is moral before it is experiential.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Samaria believes too easily. Philip’s miracles are loud, theatrical expulsions: spirits screaming as they’re driven out, bodies snapping back into usefulness. The crowd’s “one accord” feels less like conviction than relief, the hunger of a despised place for any power that will finally notice it. They accept the name, they take the washing, and still something doesn’t happen. The text admits a gap: baptized, yet untouched. Desire has been awakened, but not satisfied.

    Jerusalem hears and dispatches inspectors. Peter and John arrive like the center reclaiming the periphery, and only then does the Spirit come—by hands, by permission, by a sanctioned touch. It reads like an anxiety about uncontrolled ecstasy: the divine must be administered, delayed, made dependent on apostolic bodies. Grace is not just gift; it is governance.

    In John, love is not romance but compliance: keep my commandments. The “Paraclete” is promised as comfort, yes, but also as an internal advocate, a voice installed inside you that speaks for you and against you. “I will not leave you orphans” lands with dread: you won’t be abandoned because you will be inhabited. The world can’t receive this because it can’t see it; that’s the point—an invisible presence that bypasses public proof and settles into the private. The desire here is for manifestation, but the cost is possession: you in me, and I in you.

    Semina Verbi

    Philip’s Samaria reads like a psyche learning to consent. Attention gathers “with one accord” not because an argument wins, but because the body stops being a battlefield: the loud, alien voices exit; the paralyzed parts move. Then the center arrives from Jerusalem, not to control but to complete the circuit—touch, breath, transmission. It’s the difference between relief and integration, between a first awakening and a durable capacity to stand in public without splitting.

    John’s Paraclete is the inner advocate who makes love executable. “Keep” is not moral bookkeeping; it is fidelity that turns desire into habit until presence becomes inhabitable. “Not orphans” names the primal fear of abandonment; the answer is not reassurance but indwelling—an internalized other who stays. The world can’t “see” this because it looks for objects; this is a mode of knowing that arrives when obedience and affection converge, and then the self discovers itself nested: in the Father, in the Son, and the Son in it.

    Semina Verbi: the laying on of hands echoes shaktipat in Kashmir Shaivism and some tantric lineages, where transmission is embodied and catalytic (speculation). The Paraclete as inner advocate parallels the Mahayana bodhisattva ideal of compassionate presence that abides for the sake of others, and the Daoist sense of de as an indwelling power that becomes visible only in lived alignment (speculation). The move from baptism to “plenitude” resembles Sufi fanā’ to baqā’: an initial turning, then a steadier abiding that can bear witness without collapse (speculation).
