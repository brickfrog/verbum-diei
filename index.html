    IIILectio1 Corinthians 7:25-31

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 7:25-31 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    25Now, concerning virgins, I have no commandment of the Lord: but I give counsel, as having obtained mercy of the Lord, to be faithful.

    ☞R 25
    Apostolic counsel, not dominical fiat; authority grounded in mercy, not mandate.

    26I think therefore that this is good for the present necessity: that it is good for a man so to be.

    ☞R 26
    “Present necessity” makes ethics situational under eschatological pressure.

    27Art thou bound to a wife? Seek not to be loosed. Art thou loosed from a wife? Seek not a wife.

    28But if thou take a wife, thou hast not sinned. And if a virgin marry, she hath not sinned: nevertheless, such shall have tribulation of the flesh. But I spare you.

    ☞R 28
    Marriage permitted, then immediately taxed: “tribulation of the flesh” as built-in cost.

    29This therefore I say, brethren: The time is short. It remaineth, that they also who have wives be as if they had none:

    ☞R 29
    Time compressed; ordinary states kept at arm’s length—“as if” becomes the Christian adverb.

    30And they that weep, as though they wept not: and they that rejoice, as if they rejoiced not: and they that buy as if they possessed not:

    31And they that use this world, as if they used it not. For the fashion of this world passeth away.

    ☞R 31
    Use without use: the world is handled like a tool already scheduled for disposal.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 6:20-26

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 6:20-26 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    20And he, lifting up his eyes on his disciples, said: Blessed are ye poor: for yours is the kingdom of God.

    ☞G 20
    Beatitude begins with lack; poverty is not romanticized but enthroned.

    21Blessed are ye that hunger now: for you shall be filled. Blessed are ye that weep now: for you shall laugh.

    22Blessed shall you be when men shall hate you, and when they shall separate you and shall reproach you and cast out your name as evil, for the Son of man's sake.

    ☞G 22
    Social expulsion becomes a credential when tied to the Son of Man.

    23Be glad in that day and rejoice: for behold, your reward is great in heaven, For according to these things did their fathers to the prophets.

    24But woe to you that are rich: for you have your consolation.

    ☞G 24
    Woe names the danger of satiation: consolation can be terminal.

    25Woe to you that are filled: for you shall hunger. Woe to you that now laugh: for you shall mourn and weep.

    26Woe to you when men shall bless you: for according to these things did their fathers to the false prophets.

    ☞G 26
    Universal praise is a diagnostic; applause aligns with false prophecy.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisBecause the kingdom is imminent and the world’s form is passing, Christian life treats every earthly condition as provisional and measures blessedness by alignment with the rejected Son of Man rather than by comfort or acclaim.

    In Lectionem

    • R 27Paul does not dissolve obligations; he freezes them. The counsel is conservative in form (do not seek change) but radical in orientation: every status is relativized by an approaching horizon, so the believer’s primary task is not self-reconstruction but undivided availability.
    • R 30The repeated “as though” disciplines affect and possession. Grief, joy, commerce are not denied; they are stripped of ultimacy so that interior freedom can survive the churn of circumstances.

    In Evangelium

    • G 21Luke’s blessings and woes are not generic moral advice but an apocalyptic reversal: present conditions are read as indicators of where one is storing life. Hunger and tears are temporary; fullness and laughter can be premature settlements.
    • G 23The reward is explicitly relocated to heaven, and the community is inserted into a prophetic lineage. The point is not stoic endurance but identification with the rejected messengers of God.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul isn’t preaching purity; he’s managing panic. “The time is short” turns marriage, grief, shopping, even joy into props you handle with gloves. His counsel is not moral elevation but emergency triage: attachment is dangerous because it makes you legible to the world, and the world is about to be rearranged. “Tribulation of the flesh” is not a warning about sex; it’s the cost of having something to lose.

    Luke’s blessings aren’t comfort; they’re a recruitment speech for the dispossessed. Poverty, hunger, tears, hatred—these are not virtues but conditions that strip you of alternatives until the kingdom becomes the only imaginable compensation. The “woes” land on the satisfied because satisfaction is anesthesia: it keeps you from noticing how temporary your life is, how purchased your laughter is, how dependent your praise is on staying harmless.

    Together they read like a manifesto of holy detachment driven by dread and desire. Dread of the world’s collapse, desire for a different order that can only be wanted fully when this one stops feeding you. The blessed are not the good; they’re the unbribable. The damned are not the evil; they’re the successfully seduced.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “as if not” has the feel of Stoic apatheia and Epictetus’s inner freedom: keep your roles, loosen your grip. Speculation: it also resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s karma-yoga, action without possessiveness, and the Daoist lightness that treats status and gain as passing weather.

    Luke’s blessings and woes echo the Buddhist diagnosis of craving: fullness, laughter, and social approval become narcotics that dull perception, while lack and grief can sharpen it. Speculation: the reversal motif tracks the Sufi praise of faqr (spiritual poverty) and the Zen preference for “beginner’s mind,” where deprivation breaks the spell of self-satisfaction.

    Both texts share a psychological wager: attachment to comfort and reputation makes you manipulable, while voluntary simplicity makes you harder to buy, flatter, or threaten. Speculation: that aligns with Cynic shamelessness and certain strands of Hindu sannyasa, not as contempt for life but as training in non-dependence.
