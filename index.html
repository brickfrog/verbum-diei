Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.

Who lives and reigns for ever and ever.
    SextAd Sextam12:00

Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    VespersAd Vesperas18:00

    IIILectioJeremiah 14:17-22

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

    Jeremiah 14:17-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    17And thou shalt speak this word to them: Let my eyes shed down tears night and day, and let them not cease, because the virgin daughter of my people is afflicted with a great affliction, with an exceeding grievous evil.

    ☞R 17
    Tears are commanded, not merely permitted; grief becomes liturgy.

    18If I go forth into the fields, behold the slain with the sword: and if I enter into the city, behold them that are consumed with famine. The prophet also and the priest are gone into a land which they knew not.

    ☞R 18
    Field and city both fail; there is no safe geography.

    19Hast thou utterly cast away Juda, or hath thy soul abhorred Sion? why then hast thou struck us, so that there is no healing for us? we have looked for peace, and there is no good: and for the time of healing, and behold trouble.

    ☞R 19
    The wound is named “no healing”; peace is sought as a commodity and not found.

    20We acknowledge, O Lord, our wickedness, the iniquities of our fathers, because we have sinned against thee.

    21Give us not to be a reproach, for thy name's sake, and do not disgrace in us the throne of thy glory: remember, break not thy covenant with us.

    ☞R 21
    They bargain with God’s reputation: “for thy name’s sake.”

    22Are there any among the graven things of the Gentiles that can send rain? or can the heavens give showers? art not thou the Lord our God, whom we have looked for? for thou hast made all these things.

    ☞R 22
    Rain becomes theology: idols cannot weather the world; the Maker can.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 13:36-43

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 13:36-43 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    36Then having sent away the multitudes, he came into the house, and his disciples came to him, saying: Expound to us the parable of the cockle of the field.

    37Who made answer and said to them: He that soweth the good seed is the Son of man.

    ☞G 37
    The sower is not anonymous providence but the Son of Man.

    38And the field is the world. And the good seed are the children of the kingdom. And the cockle are the children of the wicked one.

    ☞G 38
    Two genealogies in one soil: kingdom-children and wicked-one’s children.

    39And the enemy that sowed them, is the devil. But the harvest is the end of the world. And the reapers are the angels.

    ☞G 39
    Time is weaponized: harvest is delayed judgment; angels are the executors.

    40Even as cockle therefore is gathered up, and burnt with fire: so shall it be at the end of the world.

    41The Son of man shall send his angels, and they shall gather out of his kingdom all scandals, and them that work iniquity.

    ☞G 41
    Purging begins “out of his kingdom”; scandal is an internal parasite.

    42And shall cast them into the furnace of fire: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

    43Then shall the just shine as the sun, in the kingdom of their Father. He that hath ears to hear, let him hear.

    ☞G 43
    The final image is photic: justice is visibility, not mere acquittal.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod alone governs both providence and judgment: he gives rain and he sends reapers. Covenant mercy does not cancel final separation; it postpones it until the harvest.

    In Lectionem

    • R 20,22The prayer concedes moral causality (“we have sinned”) while refusing rival causality: no secondary gods, no autonomous heavens. The appeal to covenant assumes God has bound himself to a people even when the people have unbound themselves from him.
    • R 17,21Jeremiah’s speech fuses lament and intercession: the prophet’s tears stand in for the nation’s incapacity to mourn rightly, and the plea is framed as a dispute over what God will publicly be shown to be—faithful or not.

    In Evangelium

    • G 36,40,42The private explanation replaces parable with taxonomy: agents, objects, and terminus are identified so the disciples cannot sentimentalize history. The end is not reform but separation, and the furnace is presented as the coherent outcome of what was sown.
    • G 41,43The kingdom is depicted as presently mixed and finally clarified: removal of “scandals” is not an external conquest but an internal rectification. The promised radiance of the just is the public manifestation of a hidden allegiance.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Jeremiah is not confessing to be forgiven; he is bargaining to be seen. The tears are performance and compulsion at once, a way to keep the catastrophe from becoming meaningless. “Virgin daughter” is a violated body, and the speaker can’t stop looking: fields full of corpses, city full of hunger. The real horror is that the prophet and priest have vanished into an unknown land; authority has already fled, leaving the people to improvise a theology out of dread. “For thy name’s sake” is naked self-interest: if God lets them rot, God’s reputation rots too. Covenant becomes leverage.

    Matthew’s explanation turns that same panic into a fantasy of sorting. The world is a mixed field; the desire is for clean borders, for a final audit that will make the pain intelligible. “Children of the kingdom” and “children of the wicked one” are not descriptions but cravings: the wish that the enemy is external, that the rot is not in us. The furnace is the dream of purgation, a fire that will finally explain why the good suffered and why the bad prospered. The shining sun is the payoff for endurance, but it’s also the seduction that keeps the anxious mind from admitting the simpler truth: the weeds and wheat are tangled because they share the same soil, and the need to separate them is itself a symptom.

    Semina Verbi

    Jeremiah’s tears for a wounded people echo Avalokiteśvara’s compassion in Mahāyāna Buddhism: the bodhisattva hears the world’s crying and responds not with explanation but with presence; the text’s grief-as-vigil feels like that. The insistence that idols cannot send rain parallels the Vedic move from many names to one sustaining order, ṛta, later sharpened in Upaniṣadic non-idolatry; speculation: both are psychological refusals to outsource causality to convenient objects.

    The wheat and cockle reads like a moral ecology akin to Stoic and Platonic thought: the world is mixed, and the task is discernment without premature purging, because judgment belongs to a larger logos than private rage. It also resembles the Bhagavad Gītā’s division between daivī and āsurī dispositions, not as tribes but as inner lineages; speculation: “children” here can be read as habituated patterns sown into character. The final shining has a Taoist flavor of de, virtue becoming luminous when obstruction is cleared, less reward than revelation of what was already true.
