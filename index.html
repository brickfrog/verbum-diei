Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    LaudsAd Laudes06:00

Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    TerceAd Tertiam09:00

Who lives and reigns for ever and ever.
    SextAd Sextam12:00
    O God, who revealed to Peter your plan to save all nations, grant us your grace, that all our actions may be pleasing in your sight and useful to your design of love and universal salvation. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    O God, who sent an angel to the centurion Cornelius to reveal to him the way of salvation, help us to work each day with ever greater dedication for the salvation of all, so that, together with all our brothers and sisters, incorporated into your Church, we may come to you. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    VespersAd Vesperas18:00

    ComplineAd Completorium21:00

    IIILectioGalatians 1:13-24

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Galatians

    Galatians 1:13-24 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    13For you have heard of my conversation in time past in the Jews' religion: how that, beyond measure, I persecuted the church of God and wasted it.

    ☞R 13
    “Beyond measure”: zeal without limit becomes demolition.

    14And I made progress in the Jew's religion above many of my equals in my own nation, being more abundantly zealous for the traditions of my fathers.

    15But when it pleased him who separated me from my mother's womb and called me by his grace,

    ☞R 15
    Separated before consent; vocation precedes biography.

    16To reveal his Son in me, that I might preach him among the Gentiles: immediately I condescended not to flesh and blood.

    ☞R 16
    Revelation “in me,” not merely to me; the messenger is the medium.

    17Neither went I to Jerusalem, to the apostles who were before me: but I went into Arabia, and again I returned to Damascus.

    ☞R 17
    Refusal of “flesh and blood”: no human court of appeal for the call.

    18Then, after three years, I went to Jerusalem to see Peter: and I tarried with him fifteen days.

    19But other of the apostles I saw none, saving James the brother of the Lord.

    20Now the things which I write to you, behold, before God, I lie not.

    ☞R 20
    Oath inserted like a seal: credibility is part of the argument.

    21Afterwards, I came into the regions of Syria and Cilicia.

    22And I was unknown by face to the churches of Judea, which were in Christ:

    23But they had heard only: He, who persecuted us in times past doth now preach the faith which once he impugned.

    ☞R 23
    The same mouth that “impugned” now “preach[es]”: reversal as evidence.

    24And they glorified God in me.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 10:38-42

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 10:38-42 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    38Now it came to pass, as they went, that he entered into a certain town: and a certain woman named Martha received him into her house.

    39And she had a sister called Mary, who, sitting also at the Lord's feet, heard his word.

    ☞G 39
    At the feet: posture becomes theology—reception before production.

    40But Martha was busy about much serving. Who stood and said: Lord, hast thou no care that my sister hath left me alone to serve? Speak to her therefore, that she help me.

    ☞G 40
    Service turns accusatory; hospitality weaponized into complaint.

    41And the Lord answering, said to her: Martha, Martha, thou art careful and art troubled about many things:

    ☞G 41
    Name doubled: tenderness and indictment in one stroke.

    42But one thing is necessary. Mary hath chosen the best part, which shall not be taken away from her.

    ☞G 42
    “One thing”: necessity cuts through the tyranny of the many; the “best part” is non-confiscable.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s call establishes authority and priority apart from human validation. The word of Christ is the necessary good that orders all service and cannot be taken away.

    In Lectionem

    • R 14,18,19,21,22,24Paul constructs an apostolic pedigree by refusing the usual pedigree. He notes former eminence (2) yet narrates a delayed, minimal Jerusalem contact (6–7), then dispersal to the margins (9–10). The point is jurisdiction: his gospel is not a derivative franchise of Judea but an act of God that nonetheless yields ecclesial doxology (12).

    In Evangelium

    • G 38,40,42The scene is a household turned courtroom. Martha’s “received” (1) becomes a claim of leverage, and her petition (3) tries to conscript Jesus into reallocating labor. Jesus answers by redefining value: the decisive good is not the smooth running of the house but the unlosable attachment to his word (5), which relativizes every other task.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s conversion reads less like a moral turnaround than a controlled burn. He was already built for violence: disciplined, ambitious, intoxicated by purity. The “revelation” doesn’t dissolve that engine; it re-aims it. He insists he didn’t consult “flesh and blood,” didn’t submit to Jerusalem, didn’t learn the new story from the old witnesses. Arabia first: a desert where a mind like his can harden a private certainty into a mission. The oath “before God, I lie not” sounds like the tell of someone still fighting the same battle—now for his own narrative. The churches glorify God “in” him, but what they really receive is a frightening instrument: the persecutor returned as preacher, the same force now wearing different clothes.

    Martha and Mary stage the same terror in domestic miniature. Martha’s labor is love, but it’s also control: if the house is ordered, the guest is contained, the relationship is manageable. Mary refuses management. She sits at the feet, not helping, not performing, letting desire be naked and unproductive. Jesus’ rebuke isn’t a gentle spiritual tip; it’s a threat to the economy of usefulness. “One thing is necessary” means everything else can be stripped away, including the identity built on service. Mary chooses the part that cannot be taken because it isn’t earned; it’s seized by attention. Martha’s dread is that attention makes her obsolete.

    Put together, the texts whisper a bleak comfort: the self doesn’t get healed so much as reconfigured. Paul keeps his zeal; Mary keeps her hunger. The holy arrives not as balance but as fixation, and the people around it must decide whether to call it grace or to admit how much it resembles obsession.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s rupture reads like a psyche released from compulsive identity-maintenance: the persecutor’s zeal was a disciplined addiction to belonging, and the “reveal in me” is an interior event that reorders desire without asking permission from the old peer-court. Speculatively, it resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s turning from inherited duty to a direct apprehension that reassigns vocation, and also the Sufi account of sudden unveiling that makes former certainties feel like sleep.

    Martha and Mary stage attention as destiny. Martha’s service is not condemned; her agitation is. Mary’s stillness is not passivity; it is consent to be addressed, the ego’s relinquishment of control. Speculatively, this parallels Zen’s insistence that frantic merit-making is a subtler form of grasping, and the Taoist preference for wu-wei over anxious management: the “one thing” is the unfragmented mind that lets reality arrive.
