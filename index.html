4Then Samuel did as the Lord had said to him. And he came to Bethlehem, and the ancients of the city wondered, and meeting him, they said: Is thy coming hither peaceable?

5And he said: It is peaceable: I am come to offer sacrifice to the Lord, be ye sanctified, and come with me to the sacrifice. And he sanctified Isai and his sons, and called them to the sacrifice.

6And when they were come in, he saw Eliab, and said: Is the Lord's anointed before him?

7And the Lord said to Samuel: Look not on his countenance, nor on the height of his stature: because I have rejected him, nor do I judge according to the look of man: for man seeth those things that appear, but the Lord beholdeth the heart.

8And Isai called Abinadab, and brought him before Samuel. And he said: Neither hath the Lord chosen this,

9And Isai brought Samma, and he said of him: Neither hath the Lord chosen this.

10Isai therefore brought his seven sons before Samuel: and Samuel said to Isai: The Lord hath not chosen any one of these.

11And Samuel said to Isai: Are here all thy sons? He answered: There remaineth yet a young one, who keepeth the sheep. And Samuel said to Isai: Send, and fetch him: for we will not sit down till he come hither.

12He sent therefore and brought him. Now he was ruddy and beautiful to behold, and of a comely face. And the Lord said: Arise, and anoint him, for this is he.

13Then Samuel took the horn of oil, and anointed him in the midst of his brethren: and the Spirit of the Lord came upon David from that day forward: and Samuel rose up, and went to Ramatha.

Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 4,5Bethlehem’s elders interrogate the prophet as a public risk; holiness arrives as a potential disturbance. Samuel’s sanctification rite converts a political operation into a cultic event, showing how Israel’s governance is mediated through worship rather than separated from it.
  • R 6,8,9,10,12The sequence of rejected candidates creates an anti-criterion: selection is not a reward for primogeniture or obvious fitness. David’s later description (ruddy, comely) is noted only after the divine designation, to prevent the reader from backfilling God’s choice with aesthetic inevitability.
On the Gospel
  • G 24,27,28The Pharisaic accusation assumes law is primarily a boundary-marker; Jesus answers by relocating sabbath within creation-purpose and then asserting authority to interpret that purpose. The dispute is not about permission to snack but about who has standing to define fidelity.
  • G 25,26The appeal to David is a messianic insinuation: the contested act is justified by a king-in-waiting acting under necessity. Mark uses the precedent to collapse the gap between Davidic irregularity and Jesus’ present authority, tightening the identification.

DoctrinalGod’s election and God’s law are governed by divine purpose, not by human optics or boundary-keeping. The Son of Man possesses authority over sabbath as the Davidic Lord who orders holiness toward life.

Heterodox Reading
This is a story about holy theft. God doesn’t replace Saul with a better man; he replaces him with a more useful appetite. Samuel is told to stop mourning and start plotting, and the divine instruction is essentially a cover story: bring a calf, say “sacrifice,” and hide the coup inside ritual. Bethlehem trembles because everyone knows how power changes hands: not with sermons, with knives. The prophet’s piety is camouflage. The famous line about “the heart” isn’t tenderness; it’s the ruthless interior criterion that makes bodies interchangeable. One son after another is rejected like livestock at market. The family is paraded, assessed, dismissed. And then the youngest is fetched only because the ceremony refuses to sit down without him. David is chosen while still smelling of sheep, not because he is pure, but because he is unformed enough to be seized. Even the text can’t resist his beauty; desire enters as proof of election. Mark picks up that thread and turns it into a principle: hunger outranks holiness. Jesus defends the disciples’ petty foraging by invoking David’s earlier sacrilege, as if anointing grants permission to break the rules that protect everyone else. The sabbath becomes a tool, and the “Lord of the sabbath” is a claim that need can legislate. Underneath the argument is dread: if the chosen can eat what is forbidden when they feel hungry, then law is merely the rationing system of the unchosen.
Semina Verbi
The anointing scene insists that the real criterion is interior, not legible on the body. That resembles the Upanishadic turn from form to the hidden self, and the Sufi suspicion of reputation in favor of the heart’s truth. It also touches a Stoic instinct: worth is not a spectacle but a disposition. The covert tactic—arriving under the cover of sacrifice—reads like the pragmatics of survival found in the Mahabharata’s hard counsel and in rabbinic pikuach nefesh reasoning: law exists within a world of predators. The gospel’s line that the sabbath is for man lands in the same neighborhood as pikuach nefesh, and, more broadly, as the Confucian idea that rite serves human flourishing rather than consuming it. David as hungry outlaw taking sacred bread anticipates the Zen willingness to break a rule to keep the vow’s living spirit. Speculation: it also parallels the Islamic concept of darura, necessity loosening the forbidden, not as indulgence but as fidelity to the law’s aim.
