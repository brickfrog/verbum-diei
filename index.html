    IIILectioGalatians 2:1-2,7-14

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Galatians

    Galatians 2:1-2,7-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Then, after fourteen years, I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus also with me.

    ☞R 1
    Fourteen years: patience measured, not felt.

    2And I went up according to revelation and communicated to them the gospel which I preach among the Gentiles: but apart to them who seemed to be some thing: lest perhaps I should run or had run in vain.

    ☞R 2
    “According to revelation”: private warrant brought into public scrutiny.

    7But contrariwise, when they had seen that to me was committed the gospel of the uncircumcision, as to Peter was that of the circumcision.

    8(For he who wrought in Peter to the apostleship of the circumcision wrought in me also among the Gentiles.)

    9And when they had known the grace that was given to me, James and Cephas and John, who seemed to be pillars, gave to me and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship: that we should go unto the Gentiles, and they unto the circumcision:

    ☞R 9
    “Pillars” that only “seem”: architecture with quotation marks.

    10Only that we should be mindful of the poor: which same thing also I was careful to do.

    ☞R 10
    The poor appear as the lone non-negotiable.

    11But when Cephas was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed.

    ☞R 11
    Face-to-face: authority corrected without ceremony.

    12For before that some came from James, he did eat with the Gentiles: but when they were come, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing them who were of the circumcision.

    ☞R 12
    Table-fellowship becomes the battlefield; fear redraws the map.

    13And to his dissimulation the rest of the Jews consented: so that Barnabas also was led by them into that dissimulation.

    ☞R 13
    Dissimulation is contagious; even Barnabas catches it.

    14But when I saw that they walked not uprightly unto the truth of the gospel, I said to Cephas before them all: If thou, being a Jew, livest after the manner of the Gentiles and not as the Jews do, how dost thou compel the Gentiles to live as do the Jews?

    ☞R 14
    The charge: you live free, then legislate bondage.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 11:1-4

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 11:1-4 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And it came to pass that as he was in a certain place praying, when he ceased, one of his disciples said to him: Lord, teach us to pray, as John also taught his disciples.

    ☞G 1
    Prayer is learned; even disciples need instruction.

    2And he said to them: When you pray, say: Father, hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come.

    ☞G 2
    “Father”: access granted before any request is made.

    3Give us this day our daily bread.

    ☞G 3
    Bread: the petition is small, daily, unromantic.

    4And forgive us our sins, for we also forgive every one that is indebted to us. And lead us not into temptation.

    ☞G 4
    Forgiveness tied to practice: mercy made measurable.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe gospel creates one truth that governs both doctrine and table-practice, and it teaches the Church to ask first for God’s reign, then for the mercies that keep sinners alive together.

    In Lectionem

    • R 7,8Paul frames mission as a single divine operation expressed in two fields: circumcision and uncircumcision. The point is not two gospels but one agency assigning different jurisdictions, so unity is theological before it is diplomatic.
    • R 12,14The Antioch incident exposes how social pressure can smuggle a rival soteriology into ordinary habits. The dispute is adjudicated by “truth of the gospel,” meaning conduct is treated as doctrinal evidence, not mere etiquette.

    In Evangelium

    • G 2,3The prayer orders desire: first God’s reign, then sustenance. It trains the petitioner to receive life as gift under a coming sovereignty rather than as a possession secured by control.
    • G 4The final petitions assume moral vulnerability and communal entanglement: sin and debt are parallel grammars. Prayer is thus not self-expression but submission to a regime of pardon and restraint.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “revelation” reads like a preemptive alibi: he goes up to Jerusalem not to learn, but to make sure his life’s work won’t be annulled by men “who seemed to be something.” The phrase keeps biting. He wants their handshake and despises needing it. The gospel here is not serene truth; it’s a contested territory, and Paul is terrified of running “in vain,” of being exposed as a brilliant mistake.

    Peter in Antioch is the portrait of desire under surveillance. He eats with Gentiles until the eyes arrive. Then the body remembers the old borders, not from conviction but from fear. Hypocrisy isn’t a moral lapse; it’s a survival reflex. Paul’s fury is also envy: Peter can slip between worlds and still be Peter, while Paul must harden into principle to keep from dissolving.

    The prayer in Luke is not piety but a stripped-down dependency. “Father” is intimate, almost reckless. “Daily bread” admits need without romance. “Forgive us… for we also forgive” makes mercy transactional, a bargain struck in dread of being refused. “Lead us not into temptation” is the clearest line: don’t test me, because I will fail. Set beside Antioch, it sounds like the honest version of Peter’s retreat and Paul’s aggression: both are prayers for a world where belonging doesn’t require dissimulation.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s public clash with Cephas reads like the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence on swadharma over social fear: act from the truth you’ve seen, not from the gaze of your in‑group. Speculation: it also resembles Confucius’ zhengming, the “rectification of names,” where conduct must match the role one claims, or the community corrodes into performative compliance.

    The prayer’s “Father” and “kingdom” echo the Stoic sense of a shared civic order under a higher reason, and the Jewish Kaddish’s sanctifying of the Name; both make speech an act that reorders desire. “Daily bread” parallels the Buddhist alms-bowl ethic and the Qur’anic trust in daily provision: enough for today as a discipline against grasping. “Forgive as we forgive” meets the Jain and Buddhist logic of karmic entanglement: release the debt to loosen the knot. “Lead us not into temptation” aligns with Islamic isti‘adha and yogic pratyahara: the practiced turning away from the hooks that hijack attention.
