Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God for ever and ever.
    LaudsAd Laudes06:00

    TerceAd Tertiam09:00

Who lives and reigns for ever and ever.
    SextAd Sextam12:00
    O God, who revealed to Peter your plan to save all nations, grant us your grace, that all our actions may be pleasing in your sight and useful to your design of love and universal salvation. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    NonesAd Nonam15:00
    O God, who sent an angel to the centurion Cornelius to reveal to him the way of salvation, help us to work each day with ever greater dedication for the salvation of all, so that, together with all our brothers and sisters, incorporated into your Church, we may come to you. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.
    VespersAd Vesperas18:00
    May our evening prayer rise up to you, Father of mercy, and may your blessing come down upon us; thus, with your help, we shall be saved now and forever. Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son, who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit and is God for ever and ever.
    ComplineAd Completorium21:00
    Let us pray: Light our night, Lord, and grant us peaceful rest; may we rise in your name tomorrow and, in health and joy, behold the dawning of the new day. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

    IIILectio1 Kings 21:17-29

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Book of Kings

    1 Kings 21:17-29 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    17And the word of the Lord came to Elias, the Thesbite, saying:

    18Arise, and go down to meet Achab, king of Israel, who is in Samaria: behold he is going down to the vineyard of Naboth, to take possession of it:

    ☞R 18
    The king descends to seize; the prophet is sent to intercept. Power moves downhill; judgment meets it there.

    19And thou shalt speak to him, saying: Thus saith the Lord: Thou hast slain: moreover also thou hast taken possession. And after these words thou shalt add: Thus saith the Lord: In this place, wherein the dogs have licked the blood of Naboth, they shall lick thy blood also.

    ☞R 19
    Two verbs: killed, possessed. Theft is sealed with blood; possession is treated as evidence, not excuse.

    20And Achab said to Elias: Hast thou found me thy enemy? He said: I have found thee because thou art sold, to do evil in the sight of the Lord. Sold, to do evil in the sight, etc... That is, so addicted to evil, as if thou hadst sold thyself to the devil, to be his slave to work all kinds of evil.

    21Behold I will bring evil upon thee, and I will cut down thy posterity, and I will kill of Achab him that pisseth against the wall, and him that is shut up, and the last in Israel.

    ☞R 21
    Posterity cut: sin is dynastic; the body politic is punished through the household.

    22And I will make thy house like the house of Jeroboam the son of Nabat, and like the house of Baasa the son of Ahias: for what thou hast done to provoke me to anger, and for making Israel to sin.

    23And of Jezabel also, the Lord spoke, saying: The dogs shall eat Jezabel in the field of Jezrahel.

    ☞R 23
    Jezabel’s end is unburial. The queen becomes carrion; sovereignty is reversed into meat.

    24If Achab die in the city, the dogs shall eat him: but if he die in the field, the birds of the air shall eat him.

    25Now, there was not such another as Achab, who was sold to do evil in the sight of the Lord: for his wife, Jezabel, set him on,

    26And he became abominable, insomuch that he followed the idols which the Amorrhites had made, whom the Lord destroyed before the face of the children of Israel.

    27And when Achab had heard these words, he rent his garments, and put haircloth upon his flesh, and fasted, and slept in sackcloth, and walked with his head cast down.

    ☞R 27
    Rending, haircloth, fasting: the king performs abasement with his whole body; repentance is staged in fabric and hunger.

    28And the word of the Lord came to Elias, the Thesbite, saying:

    29Hast thou not seen Achab humbled before me? therefore, because he hath humbled himself, for my sake, I will not bring the evil in his days, but in his son's days will I bring the evil upon his house.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 5:43-38

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 5:43-38 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    38You have heard that it hath been said: An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth.

    ☞G 38
    Measured retaliation: justice as arithmetic.

    39But I say to you not to resist evil: but if one strike thee on thy right cheek, turn to him also the other: Not to resist evil, etc... What is here commanded, is a Christian patience under injuries and affronts, and to be willing even to suffer still more, rather than to indulge the desire of revenge: but what is further added does not strictly oblige according to the letter, for neither did Christ nor St. Paul turn the other cheek. St. John 18., and Acts 23.

    ☞G 39
    The right cheek specifies insult, not assault; the command targets honor-culture reflex.

    40And if a man will contend with thee in judgment, and take away thy coat, let go thy cloak also unto him.

    ☞G 40
    Litigation strips; the disciple yields surplus, refusing to let courts define the self.

    41And whosoever will force thee one mile, go with him other two.

    ☞G 41
    Forced mile: empire conscripts the body; the second mile breaks the script of domination.

    42Give to him that asketh of thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not away.

    ☞G 42
    Open hand as discipline: generosity without vetting, a refusal to police need.

    43You have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thy enemy.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod judges blood-guilt as a public, dynastic reality, yet he recognizes self-abasement as a real moral event that can alter the timing of judgment. Christ abolishes revenge as a governing principle and establishes non-retaliation and open-handedness as the form of righteous agency.

    In Lectionem

    • R 17,28,29The narrative is framed by repeated divine speech: judgment is not Elijah’s temperament but a commissioned verdict. Yet the verdict is temporally re-ordered when humiliation appears; the sentence remains, but its execution is deferred, showing that history is governed, not merely predicted.
    • R 22,25,26Ahab is placed in a typology of failed kingship: his house is made an example by comparison, and his idolatry is described as assimilation to a displaced people. The text treats political apostasy as cultural surrender—Israel becomes what it was meant to replace.

    In Evangelium

    • G 39,43The speaker asserts authority over received formulae and redirects the axis from reciprocity to non-vengeance. The horizon is not passivity but a new kind of agency: the disciple refuses to let injury set the terms of action, and the coming command about enemies completes the logic by severing love from tribal accounting.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Achab isn’t punished for murder alone. He’s punished for wanting the vineyard to mean something it can’t: a clean, owned, silent piece of earth that will swallow the fact of Naboth. He buys innocence the way he buys land, and Elijah appears as the return of the repressed, naming the blood that still clings to the soil. The dogs are not moral symbols; they are appetite, the world’s mouth, insisting that what was taken will be taken back in the same register—flesh for flesh, possession answered by consumption.

    Then the twist: Achab’s humiliation works. Not because he’s converted, but because the system that threatens him is aroused by abasement. Sackcloth becomes a currency; dread becomes leverage. Mercy is delayed, not canceled, and the delay is the most chilling part: violence is not ended, only rescheduled onto the son. Repentance here is less a cleansing than a negotiation with inevitability.

    Against that, the Gospel’s “do not resist” reads like a rival technology of power. Turn the cheek, hand over the cloak, walk the second mile: not saintly passivity, but refusal to play the old arithmetic where injury demands payment. It’s an erotic kind of freedom—choosing excess where the world demands equivalence—yet it also risks becoming another way to let Achab keep the vineyard. The tension is brutal: Elijah insists the body remembers; Jesus suggests you can break the chain by refusing to remember in the usual way. Desire wants both: justice that bites, and a self so ungrabbable it can’t be owned or avenged.

    Semina Verbi

    Ahab is addiction with a crown: the self that has outsourced agency to appetite, then calls the messenger “enemy” because conscience feels like persecution. Elijah is the returning reality principle, naming the crime in public language so the psyche can’t launder it as policy. The dogs are the social body’s scavengers: what you spill into the world comes back as contamination, not as mystical curse but as the ecology of violence.

    The surprising hinge is Ahab’s abasement. It doesn’t erase the harm; it changes the timing. Psychologically, remorse can interrupt the immediate compulsion cycle without undoing the downstream consequences already set in motion. The text refuses cheap catharsis: contrition is real, and so is inheritance.

    “Do not resist” is not passivity; it is refusal to mirror. Turning the cheek blocks the contagion of retaliation, the way nonviolent discipline breaks the script that keeps injury reproducing itself. It’s a strategy for starving the ego’s craving to feel righteous through revenge.

    Speculation: this resembles karma in Buddhism and Jainism as moral causality without a vengeful accountant; it echoes the Bhagavad Gita’s warning about desire binding the will; it parallels Stoic prohairesis, where dignity is kept by not surrendering inner rule to insult; it rhymes with Daoist wu-wei, yielding to avoid feeding conflict; it touches Sufi nafs work, where the “enemy” is the self’s lower tyranny.
