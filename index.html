    IIILectio1 Corinthians 4:6-15

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 4:6-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    6But these things, brethren, I have in a figure transferred to myself and to Apollo, for your sakes: that in us you may learn that one be not puffed up against the other for another, above that which is written.

    ☞R 6
    He hides behind exempla to strike the real target: their factional vanity.

    7For who distinguisheth thee? Or what hast thou that thou hast not received, and if thou hast received, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?

    ☞R 7
    A single question dismantles merit: possession implies prior gift.

    8You are now full: you are now become rich: you reign without us; and I would to God you did reign, that we also might reign with you.

    ☞R 8
    Ironic coronation: they “reign” in imagination while the founders stand outside the palace.

    9For I think that God hath set forth us apostles, the last, as it were men appointed to death. We are made a spectacle to the world and to angels and to men.

    ☞R 9
    Apostolic office as public execution: theology staged as disgrace.

    10We are fools for Christs sake, but you are wise in Christ: we are weak, but you are strong: you are honourable, but we without honour.

    ☞R 10
    Antiphonal inversion—wise/weak/honourable—exposes their counterfeit “in Christ.”

    11Even unto this hour we both hunger and thirst and are naked and are buffeted and have no fixed abode.

    12And we labour, working with our own hands. We are reviled: and we bless. We are persecuted: and we suffer it.

    13We are blasphemed: and we entreat. We are made as the refuse of this world, the offscouring of all, even until now.

    ☞R 13
    Refuse and offscouring: the Church’s foundation laid in what society sweeps away.

    14I write not these things to confound you: but I admonish you as my dearest children.

    15For if you have ten thousand instructors in Christ, yet not many fathers. For in Christ Jesus, by the gospel, I have begotten you.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 6:1-5

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 6:1-5 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And it came to pass on the second first sabbath that, as he went through the corn fields, his disciples plucked the ears and did eat, rubbing them in their hands.

    ☞G 1
    Sabbath framed by hunger; law meets the body in the field, not the academy.

    2And some of the Pharisees said to them: Why do you that which is not lawful on the sabbath days?

    ☞G 2
    The accusation is procedural: legality replaces discernment.

    3And Jesus answering them, said: Have you not read so much as this, what David did, when himself was hungry and they that were with him:

    4How he went into the house of God and took and ate the bread of proposition and gave to them that were with him, which is not lawful to eat but only for the priests?

    ☞G 4
    Holy bread becomes precedent: necessity trespasses into sanctuary and is not condemned.

    5And he said to them: The Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath.

    ☞G 5
    Lordship over time itself: the Sabbath is not an equal partner but a possession.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisAll Christian boasting is theft, because every gift is received and every authority is cruciform. The Son of Man governs the Sabbath, so divine law is ordered by Christ’s lordship and the necessities he judges.

    In Lectionem

    • R 11,12Paul’s catalogue of deprivation and manual labor is not self-pity but credentialing: the Corinthians’ status-games are judged by an economy where authority is authenticated by endurance and non-retaliation. The apostle’s “we bless… we suffer” establishes a pattern of power that refuses to mirror the city’s honor system.
    • R 14,15He shifts from satire to paternity: not to flatter but to assert jurisdiction. “Instructors” can multiply without obligation; a father claims origin, responsibility, and the right to correct—an ecclesial structure grounded in begetting rather than charisma.

    In Evangelium

    • G 3,5Jesus argues by Scripture to expose a deeper hermeneutic: David’s exceptional act reveals that the law already contains a hierarchy of goods. The conclusion is not mere exception-making but a christological claim—authority to order sacred time belongs to the Son of Man, so interpretation is finally personal before it is textual.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul is not teaching humility; he is staging a humiliation. He drips with sarcasm—“you are full… rich… you reign”—because their spiritual confidence is a fantasy built to avoid the raw fact of dependence. His real move is psychological: he makes himself the abject object, the “spectacle,” the “offscouring,” so their triumphal self-image has to look at its own cruelty. Then he tightens the leash with the word they can’t resist: father. Not teacher, not peer—father. Love is the velvet, possession the steel.

    Luke shows the same nerve. Hunger walks through a field and breaks the law with its hands. The Pharisees defend time; Jesus defends appetite. He doesn’t argue purity; he tells a story of sacred theft and crowns it with a claim of authority: the sabbath serves the living body, not the other way around. The dread underneath is simple: once need is admitted, rules lose their innocence. Desire becomes king, and piety has to decide whether it will feed people or only judge them.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s refusal of status and his embrace of being “refuse” reads like Cynic parrhesia and Diogenes’ anti-prestige theater: the teacher proves freedom by consenting to contempt. Speculation: it also resembles the Zen move of taking the lowest seat, letting insult burn off the self that wants to be “distinguished.”

    His claim to fatherhood is not hierarchy but psychic adoption: a demand for gratitude that breaks the adolescent fantasy of self-creation. Confucian xiao and the guru–shishya bond in Advaita both treat formation as received life, not self-made achievement.

    Jesus’ sabbath argument turns law into a servant of hunger, like the rabbinic instinct of pikuach nefesh extended into ordinary need, and like Aristotle’s epieikeia where equity corrects rigid rule. Speculation: it echoes Mahayana upaya, where compassion authorizes bending form without despising it.

    “Lord of the sabbath” asserts mastery of time’s sacred boundary; it rhymes with Shiva as Mahakala and with Stoic sovereignty, the inner ruler who is not owned by the calendar.
