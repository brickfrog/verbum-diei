    IIILectioAmos 9:11-15

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Amos

    Amos 9:11-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    11In that day I will raise up the tabernacle of David, that is fallen: and I will close up the breaches of the walls thereof, and repair what was fallen: and I will rebuild it as in the days of old.

    ☞R 11
    Ruins named “David” become a project of carpentry: breaches, repairs, rebuild—politics recast as architecture.

    12That they may possess the remnant of Edom, and all nations, because my name is invoked upon them: saith the Lord that doth these things.

    ☞R 12
    Possession language sits beside invocation: conquest justified by a Name, not a sword.

    13Behold the days come, saith the Lord, when the ploughman shall overtake the reaper, and the treader of grapes him that soweth seed: and the mountains shall drop sweetness, and every hill shall be tilled. Shall overtake, etc... By this is meant the great abundance of spiritual blessings; which, as it were, by a constant succession, shall enrich the church of Christ.

    ☞R 13
    Harvest collapses into sowing; time itself is compressed into surplus.

    14And I will bring back the captivity of my people Israel: and they shall build the abandoned cities, and inhabit them: and they shall plant vineyards, and drink the wine of them: and shall make gardens, and eat the fruits of them. And I will plant them upon their own land: and I will no more pluck them out of their land which I have given them, saith the Lord thy God. THE PROPHECY OF ABDIAS ABDIAS, whose name is interpreted THE SERVANT OF THE LORD, is believed to have prophesied about the same time as OSEE, JOEL, and AMOS: though some of the Hebrews, who believe him to be the same with ACHAB's steward, make him much more ancient. His prophecy is the shortest of any in number of words, but yields to none, says ST. JEROME, in the sublimity of mysteries. It contains but one chapter.

    ☞R 14
    “Plant” answers “pluck”: the same hand that uproots can make permanence.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 9:14-17

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 9:14-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    14Then came to him the disciples of John, saying: Why do we and the Pharisees, fast often, but thy disciples do not fast?

    15And Jesus said to them: Can the children of the bridegroom mourn, as long as the bridegroom is with them? But the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken away from them, and then they shall fast. Can the children of the bridegroom... This, by a Hebraism, signifies the friends or companions of the bridegroom.

    ☞G 15
    Presence suspends mourning; absence institutes fasting—asceticism as a response to loss, not a technique.

    16And nobody putteth a piece of raw cloth unto an old garment. For it taketh away the fulness thereof from the garment, and there is made a greater rent.

    ☞G 16
    Patchwork worsens the tear: partial renovation can be more violent than decay.

    17Neither do they put new wine into old bottles. Otherwise the bottles break, and the wine runneth out, and the bottles perish. But new wine they put into new bottles: and both are preserved.

    ☞G 17
    New wine is not merely added; it demands a new container or it destroys what receives it.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s restoration is not a repair of the old world but the creation of a new covenantal order. Christ’s presence and removal govern the Church’s practices, which must be remade to receive what he brings.

    In Lectionem

    • R 11,12The oracle moves from dynastic collapse to a widened horizon: David’s restoration is not an end in itself but a reordering of peoples under divine claim. The “remnant” and “all nations” are drawn into a single jurisdiction defined by the Lord’s action, not Israel’s capacity.
    • R 13,14The imagery shifts from sovereignty to fertility and settlement: abundance, rebuilding, and land-security function as signs of a covenantal reversal. The point is not comfort but the public visibility of a God who can reverse exile into rootedness.

    In Evangelium

    • G 14,15The question about fasting is a question about time: what season is it? Jesus answers by locating practice inside a nuptial economy—joy while the bridegroom is present, disciplined lack when he is removed—so the same act (fasting) changes meaning with the event.
    • G 16,17The two mini-parables reject a hybrid religion that tries to domesticate the inbreaking kingdom within inherited forms. The issue is not novelty for its own sake but incompatibility: the advent of Christ creates a new order that cannot be contained by merely adjusted old structures.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Amos promises repair, but the repair is a conquest fantasy dressed as homecoming. The “tabernacle of David” is a collapsed ego-image: patch the breaches, rebuild the old days, and you can finally possess what’s left of Edom and “all nations.” Restoration here is not peace; it’s appetite. Even the lush agrarian overflow—ploughman overtaking reaper, hills dripping sweetness—reads like manic compensation, a world forced to produce without pause so the wound doesn’t reopen in silence.

    Then Matthew refuses the pious rhythm that would keep desire manageable. Fasting is not denied; it’s postponed, because the center is present. The bridegroom is a live stimulus, and mourning can’t compete with that charge. But the line that matters is “taken away”: joy is already shadowed by abduction, and the future fast is grief made inevitable.

    The cloth and the wine are not gentle metaphors about “newness.” They’re warnings about containment. Old garments and old skins are the self’s familiar disciplines—rules that once held. The new thing doesn’t politely improve them; it tears them, bursts them, wastes what it came to save. The text insists that desire demands a new vessel, and that the cost of refusing is rupture: not moral failure, but psychic breakage. The kingdom arrives like surplus, and surplus is dangerous.

    Semina Verbi

    This is repair and rupture held in the same hand: a house rebuilt, and a garment that must not be patched. Psychologically it’s the difference between restoration after trauma and the compulsive attempt to glue the old self back together with a shiny new practice. The “bridegroom” image names a phase of presence when joy is metabolized directly, without ascetic proof; fasting arrives later as grief-work, not virtue-signaling. The warning about skins is a warning about containers: when desire intensifies, the personality’s old defenses can’t hold it; forcing it in only produces a bigger tear.

    Speculation: the rebuilt “tabernacle” echoes the Hindu idea of dharma restored after collapse, a re-stitching of cosmic order rather than mere nostalgia. The ploughman overtaking the reaper resembles Daoist wu-wei abundance, where effort stops feeling sequential and life flows without the usual friction. “New wine in new skins” parallels Zen’s insistence on beginner’s mind: insight can’t be housed in inherited forms without turning them into brittle dogma. The bridegroom’s presence and absence tracks Sufi longing, where ecstasy is followed by separation that deepens the heart’s capacity rather than punishing it.
