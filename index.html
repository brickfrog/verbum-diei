    IIILectioEzekiel 18:25-28

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

    Ezekiel 18:25-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    25And you have said: The way of the Lord is not right. Hear ye, therefore, O house of Israel: Is it my way that is not right, and are not rather your ways perverse?

    ☞R 25
    Accusation reversed: the judge is judged; “not right” becomes “perverse.”

    26For when the just turneth himself away from his justice, and comitteth iniquity, he shall die therein: in the injustice that he hath wrought he shall die.

    ☞R 26
    Justice is not a possession but a path; exit it and death is not imposed but entered.

    27And when the wicked turneth himself away from his wickedness, which he hath wrought, and doeth judgment, and justice: he shall save his soul alive.

    ☞R 27
    The wicked is granted a future; judgment is not nostalgia for the past but a new direction.

    28Because he considereth and turneth away himself from all his iniquities which he hath wrought, he shall surely live, and not die.

    ☞R 28
    “Considereth” precedes turning: thought is treated as moral action’s hinge.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 21:28-32

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 21:28-32 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    28But what think you? A certain man had two sons: and coming to the first, he said: Son, go work to day in my vineyard.

    29And he answering, said: I will not. But afterwards, being moved with repentance, he went.

    ☞G 29
    Refusal can be truer than assent; repentance outbids politeness.

    30And coming to the other, he said in like manner. And he answering said: I go, Sir. And he went not.

    ☞G 30
    Speech without motion: obedience counterfeited by courtesy.

    31Which of the two did the father's will? They say to him: The first. Jesus saith to them: Amen I say to you that the publicans and the harlots shall go into the kingdom of God before you.

    ☞G 31
    Priority scandal: the disreputable enter first, not because sin is small but because reversal is real.

    32For John came to you in the way of justice: and you did not believe him. But the publicans and the harlots believed him: but you, seeing it, did not even afterwards repent, that you might believe him.

    ☞G 32
    Evidence increases guilt: “seeing it” and still no turn—hardness after proof.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s judgment is rectitude, not caprice: it follows the lived turn of the will. The kingdom is entered by repentance enacted, not by claims of obedience.

    In Lectionem

    • R 25,26The dispute is about the character of divine judgment: Israel charges arbitrariness; the oracle answers by locating continuity in moral causality—life and death track the present orientation, not inherited labels.
    • R 27,28The passage dismantles fatalism. It treats conversion as a genuine change of standing, making responsibility dynamic: neither prior righteousness nor prior wickedness is allowed to function as a permanent shield or sentence.

    In Evangelium

    • G 28,31The parable is a courtroom trap: Jesus forces his hearers to pronounce a verdict that condemns their own posture, because “will” is defined by enacted compliance rather than declared loyalty.
    • G 32John’s ministry functions as a public criterion of legitimacy. Refusal to believe is exposed as refusal to repent; the leaders’ problem is not ignorance but resistance to a witnessed moral realignment in others.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ezekiel stages a courtroom where the defendant is not God but the self that wants a stable identity. “Your ways are perverse” means: you have made virtue into property, a hoard you can live off. The dread is that nothing you did yesterday will save you today; the self is not a pension plan. The threat “he shall die therein” is psychological before it is metaphysical: you can become the act you repeat, until it is the only language your life can speak.

    The supposed comfort—wickedness can turn and live—cuts the other way too. If the wicked can pivot, then the “just” can rot. No one is safe inside a label. God’s “way” is not fairness as bookkeeping; it is a relentless present tense that refuses to let you hide behind your résumé.

    Matthew sharpens it into desire. The first son’s “I will not” is honest appetite, a refusal that admits conflict; later he goes because he can still be moved. The second son’s “I go, Sir” is the narcotic of compliance, a performance that keeps desire untouched and therefore unchanged. The vineyard is not a moral workplace; it is the place where your body has to show up.

    Publicans and harlots go first because they have already lost the luxury of appearing righteous. Their lives are exposed, so repentance can be real: not a speech, but a turn. The religious hear John and feel only the threat to their self-image; they would rather be correct than alive. The kingdom begins where the lie of being “the good one” finally becomes unbearable.

    Semina Verbi

    Ezekiel’s pivot is interior: the self is not a fixed moral caste but a direction that can reverse, and the complaint about “unfairness” is exposed as a defense of one’s own crookedness. That resembles the Stoic insistence that virtue is a present act of assent, not a stored asset, and the Buddhist emphasis on intention and turning the mind; speculation: both traditions would hear “live and not die” as the psychological fact that a reoriented will stops generating its own suffering.

    Matthew’s two sons stage the split between speech and embodied choice: refusal can be honest contact with desire, while compliance can be a performance that anesthetizes conscience. That aligns with Confucian suspicion of glib propriety without ren, and with Sufi talk of tawba as a real turning rather than a pious pose; speculation: the “publicans and harlots” function like Zen’s preference for the unvarnished beginner over the self-satisfied adept, because shame can be a doorway when it isn’t converted into hypocrisy.
