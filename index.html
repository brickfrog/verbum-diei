    IIILectioEcclesiastes 3:1-11

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ecclesiastes

    Ecclesiastes 3:1-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1All things have their season, and in their times all things pass under heaven.

    ☞R 1
    Seasonality is law; heaven is the ceiling, not the escape.

    2A time to be born and a time to die. A time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted.

    ☞R 2
    Birth and death paired without sentiment; agriculture as the grammar of flesh.

    3A time to kill, and a time to heal. A time to destroy, and a time to build.

    4A time to weep, and a time to laugh. A time to mourn, and a time to dance.

    5A time to scatter stones, and a time to gather. A time to embrace, and a time to be far from embraces.

    ☞R 5
    Stones: either field-clearing or weaponry; embrace has an appointed absence.

    6A time to get, and a time to lose. A time to keep, and a time to cast away.

    7A time to rend, and a time to sew. A time to keep silence, and a time to speak.

    ☞R 7
    Silence and speech are not virtues but timings; ethics reduced to clockwork.

    8A time of love, and a time of hatred. A time of war, and a time of peace.

    9What hath man more of his labour?

    ☞R 9
    The ledger question: surplus of labor is demanded, not assumed.

    10I have seen the trouble, which God hath given the sons of men to be exercised in it.

    11He hath made all things good in their time, and hath delivered the world to their consideration, so that man cannot find out the work which God hath made from the beginning to the end.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 9:18-22

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 9:18-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    18And it came to pass, as he was alone praying, his disciples also were with him: and he asked them, saying: Whom do the people say that I am?

    ☞G 18
    Prayer in solitude, interrogation in company; identity posed as public rumor.

    19But they answered and said: John the Baptist; but some say Elias: and others say that one of the former prophets is risen again.

    20And he said to them: But whom do you say that I am? Simon Peter answering, said: The Christ of God.

    ☞G 20
    The title lands clean: not teacher, not prophet—Messiah.

    21But he strictly charging them, commanded they should tell this to no man.

    ☞G 21
    Revelation is throttled; truth is not yet for circulation.

    22Saying: The Son of man must suffer many things and be rejected by the ancients and chief priests and scribes and be killed and the third day rise again.

    ☞G 22
    Necessity (“must”) governs the Passion; rejection is institutional, death is scheduled, rising is dated.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisProvidence orders times and withholds total comprehension, exposing human labor as incapable of final gain. The Christ is known truly only when his messiahship is interpreted through the necessity of suffering, death, and resurrection.

    In Lectionem

    • R 3,4The poem’s antitheses are not moral equivalences but a taxonomy of creaturely limits: agency exists, yet it is bounded by alternation. The sequence moves from bodily events to social bonds to speech, then to the political (war/peace), widening the scope until the question of profit exposes the futility of mastery.
    • R 10,11God is named as the giver of “trouble” and the maker of fittingness; the world is handed over for contemplation, not control. The final clause installs epistemic frustration as part of the gift: total comprehension is withheld, forcing humility before providence.

    In Evangelium

    • G 19,20The scene stages a transfer from hearsay to confession: the crowd’s categories cannot contain him, and the disciples are pressed into decision. Peter’s answer functions as a hinge—recognition is granted, but it immediately reconfigures what messiahship will mean.
    • G 22Jesus defines his identity by trajectory rather than acclaim: suffering and rejection are not accidents but the ordained path. The mention of specific authorities locates the conflict in religious governance, and the resurrection clause prevents the Passion from being read as defeat.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ecclesiastes isn’t soothing you with cosmic balance. It’s teaching you the narcotic rhythm of inevitability: everything gets a slot, everything gets excused. Birth and death, healing and killing, love and hatred—filed like paperwork. The dread is that the list makes atrocity feel natural. If there is “a time to kill,” then the killer can call himself obedient to the clock. The poem is a sedative for conscience, a way to survive by surrendering agency. You ask, “What have I gained?” and the answer is: a mind trained to accept loss as schedule.

    Then Luke turns the screw. Identity is a trap. Jesus asks for a name, gets one, and immediately forbids it. The secret isn’t humility; it’s strategy. Once you say “Christ,” the story hardens, roles snap into place, and the machine of seasons starts demanding its payment. He announces suffering as a must, not a risk. The desire here is to be recognized; the dread is what recognition summons: rejection, execution, and a resurrection that sounds less like comfort than like the final proof you never owned your life’s timing at all.

    Semina Verbi

    Ecclesiastes’ seasons rhyme with the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence on acting within one’s given moment without pretending to command outcomes; both treat time as a moral weather system you learn to read rather than defeat. The Stoics’ amor fati sits nearby: not passive surrender, but trained consent to necessity. Speculation: the Taoist sense of wu-wei also fits, where wisdom is timing, not force.

    Luke’s question of identity echoes the Zen demand for direct seeing beyond borrowed names; the crowd’s labels are secondhand, the disciple’s answer is existential. The secrecy and the turn to suffering resemble the bodhisattva vow in Mahayana Buddhism, where compassion is not triumphal but costly. Speculation: in Sufism, the beloved’s hiddenness and the lover’s ordeal form the same psychological arc—recognition that deepens into loss, then remade self.
