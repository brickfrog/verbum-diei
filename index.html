    IIILectio2 Kings 25:1-12

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Second Book of Kings

    2 Kings 25:1-12 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And it came to pass in the ninth year of his reign, in the tenth month, the tenth day of the month, that Nabuchodonosor, king of Babylon, came, he and all his army, against Jerusalem: and they surrounded it: and raised works round about it.

    ☞R 1
    Dates stacked like stones: judgment is calendared, not improvised.

    2And the city was shut up and besieged till the eleventh year of king Sedecias,

    3The ninth day of the month: and a famine prevailed in the city, and there was no bread for the people of the land.

    ☞R 3
    Famine is the final siege-engine; breadlessness unmakes citizenship.

    4And a breach was made into the city: and all the men of war fled in the night between the two walls by the king's garden (now the Chaldees besieged the city round about), and Sedecias fled by the way that leadeth to the plains of the wilderness.

    ☞R 4
    A “breach” in walls becomes a breach in courage; flight chooses night and gardens.

    5And the army of the Chaldees pursued after the king, and overtook him in the plains of Jericho: and all the warriors that were with him were scattered, and left him:

    ☞R 5
    The king is pursued; the warriors scatter. Authority is revealed as borrowed.

    6So they took the king, and brought him to the king of Babylon, to Reblatha, and he gave judgment upon him.

    7And he slew the sons of Sedecias before his face, and he put out his eyes, and bound him with chains, and brought him to Babylon.

    ☞R 7
    Sons slain “before his face,” then eyes removed: vision is punished after it is forced to see.

    8In the fifth month, the seventh day of the month, the same is the nineteenth year of the king of Babylon, came Nabuzardan, commander of the army, a servant of the king of Babylon, into Jerusalem.

    9And he burnt the house of the Lord, and the king's house, and the houses of Jerusalem, and every great house he burnt with fire.

    ☞R 9
    Temple and palace burn together: cult and crown share one pyre.

    10And all the army of the Chaldees, which was with the commander of the troops, broke down the walls of Jerusalem round about.

    11And Nabuzardan, the commander of the army, carried away the rest of the people, that remained in the city, and the fugitives, that had gone over to the king of Babylon, and the remnant of the common people.

    12But of the poor of the land he left some dressers of vines and husbandmen.

    ☞R 12
    The poor remain as tools of the soil; conquest keeps what feeds it.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 8:1-4

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 8:1-4 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And when he was come down from the mountain, great multitudes followed him:

    2And behold a leper came and adored him, saying: Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.

    ☞G 2
    The leper’s theology is conditional: not power questioned, only will.

    3And Jesus stretching forth his hand, touched him, saying: I will, be thou made clean. And forthwith his leprosy was cleansed.

    ☞G 3
    Touch crosses the quarantine line; cleanliness is transferred by contact, not avoided by distance.

    4And Jesus saith to him: See thou tell no man: but go, shew thyself to the priest, and offer the gift which Moses commanded, for a testimony unto them.

    ☞G 4
    Silence commanded, law observed: miracle is routed through Moses as evidence, not spectacle.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s judgment can dismantle a holy city down to its walls and dynasties, and God’s mercy can rebuild a ruined body with a word and a touch. Both acts assert sovereign will that orders history and restores the unclean without surrendering the law.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2,6,8,10,11The narrative moves from containment (siege) to adjudication (Reblatha) to dismantling (walls, deportation). Babylon’s victory is administrative as much as military: it judges, burns, breaks, and sorts populations, leaving a managed residue.
    • R 1,9,10Jerusalem is treated as a total system—sanctuary, monarchy, fortification—so the destruction is comprehensive. The text’s precision about time and agents underscores that collapse is not chaos but a measured unmaking.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,4The descent from the mountain shifts from public teaching to targeted restoration. Yet the healed man is immediately placed back under priestly verification and prescribed offering, binding mercy to covenant order and making the cure a public legal fact.
    • G 2,3The exchange is minimal—request, will, touch—suggesting authority that does not negotiate with impurity but overrules it. The act redefines proximity: holiness is not threatened by the unclean; it is operative upon it.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Jerusalem doesn’t fall because Babylon is strong; it falls because the city has already decided to die. The siege just makes the decision audible: hunger speaks louder than prophecy. Sedecias runs through the garden like a man trying to slip out of his own story, and the story punishes him with a precision that feels erotic in its cruelty: watch your sons die, then lose the organ of watching. Desire for escape curdles into the one thing he cannot flee, the gaze of judgment.

    Then the Gospel offers a smaller violence, intimate and scandalous. The leper is the city in miniature, a body declared uninhabitable. He doesn’t ask for justice or meaning; he asks for contact. Jesus touches what everyone has trained themselves to fear, and the fear collapses. But the cure is not liberation; it’s rerouting. Silence. Priest. Gift. Testimony. The system that branded him must now certify him.

    Put them together and you get a bleak mercy: empires burn temples, and healers still send you back to the gatekeepers. Cleanliness is granted, but belonging is always negotiated. The poor left in the land are not spared out of kindness; they are left as function, as hands for vines. Even grace, here, has paperwork and a price.

    Semina Verbi

    Jerusalem’s fall reads like a psyche losing its last enclosing defenses: siege, famine, breach, flight, capture. The blinding of the king is the inner collapse made literal, the moment leadership becomes pure survival and then pure helplessness. Yet the conqueror leaves vine-dressers and farmers: a thin mercy, but also a grim truth that life persists at the level of tending, after grand houses and walls are reduced to ash.

    The leper is the opposite movement: the excluded body walks straight into the crowd and asks for contact. The touch is not only cure but re-entry, a restoration of belonging that doesn’t bypass structure; he is sent to the priest, not to hide the wound but to have it publicly recognized as healed. The command of silence is psychological hygiene: don’t let the miracle become identity theater; let the healed life speak later.

    Semina Verbi: the king’s blinding echoes Oedipus (Greek tragedy) where sight is forfeited when truth becomes unbearable; speculation, but the same logic of authority shattered into inward darkness. The leper’s touch recalls Buddhist karuṇā in stories where a bodhisattva meets impurity without recoil, and also Sufi adab where intimacy with the broken is a form of reverence; different metaphysics, same human reversal of disgust into presence. The remnant left to farm resembles Daoist plainness and post-catastrophe simplicity: when the city-mind burns, the real work is vines, soil, breath.
