Commentary

On the Reading
  • R 18:6,19:3,19:4,19:5,19:7The passage pivots from public acclaim to private mediation. Jonathan functions as covenantal buffer inside the royal house, translating political paranoia into moral language (‘sin… innocent blood’) and briefly restoring administrative order (‘as… yesterday’). The narrative exposes kingship without interior obedience: the state can be stabilized by rhetoric and relationship while the underlying disorder remains unresolved.
On the Gospel
  • G 7,8The geography widens to the edges—Judea, Idumea, Tyre, Sidon—signaling a messianic field larger than Israel’s internal rivalries. Yet the movement is managed: withdrawal to the sea, not enthronement in the city. Mark shows authority that draws nations while refusing the apparatus of publicity, because the meaning of sonship will be set by the cross, not by regional enthusiasm.

DoctrinalGod’s anointed is threatened by jealous rule, while God’s Son restrains premature acclaim: the kingdom advances not by popular noise but by ordered revelation and obedience.

Heterodox Reading
Saul’s collapse begins as a lyric. The women’s chorus doesn’t merely praise David; it edits Saul’s identity in public, reducing him to a smaller number. Envy here is not petty but annihilating: the fear that the crowd’s desire will migrate, and with it the right to be real. Saul hears arithmetic and feels a coup. Jonathan’s love is the real scandal. He wants David alive not as an abstract innocent but as the object that holds his own longing together. His diplomacy is erotic in its finesse: he hides David, stages the scene, speaks “good things,” manages Saul’s gaze. The oath Saul swears is not repentance; it’s a brief remission bought by his son’s intimacy. The dread remains, dormant, waiting for the next song. Jesus, by contrast, is crushed by need rather than praise. Bodies press to touch him; sickness becomes a kind of hunger that threatens to consume the healer. The unclean spirits name him correctly, and he silences them because their confession is possession. To be proclaimed is to be used, to be dragged into other people’s narratives. Put together, the readings suggest a bleak economy of acclaim. Praise makes Saul murderous; truth makes Jesus withdraw. Love negotiates a truce for David, but it cannot cure the ache beneath the crown, the terror that another will be desired more—and therefore deserve to live.
Semina Verbi
Saul’s envy is Girard’s mimetic fever before Girard names it: praise becomes contagion, desire triangulates, the rival must be erased to restore a wounded self. The women’s chant is the spark; the king’s identity collapses into comparison. In Buddhist terms, it’s taṇhā and māna—craving and conceit—turning a victory into suffering; the “kingdom” is just the next object fantasy promises will seal the crack. Jonathan is the dharmic friend inside the court, the one person who can speak reality without spectacle. His intervention resembles the Confucian minister’s remonstrance, loyalty expressed as moral correction rather than flattery. It also echoes a Stoic move: he recalls Saul to what he already knows, trying to re-anchor judgment in facts instead of passion. Jesus’ retreat and the waiting boat read like disciplined containment of charisma. The crowd wants contact as extraction; he manages distance so the healing doesn’t become possession. That restraint has kinship with Taoist non-grasping, and with yogic pratyāhāra, a deliberate turning of the senses away from the stampede of demand. The silencing of the spirits feels like an antidote to premature naming, a refusal to let the deepest truth be carried by compulsive, noisy forces. Speculatively, it parallels apophatic traditions—Christian negative theology, the Upaniṣadic neti neti, even Zen’s distrust of declarative slogans—where the right word at the wrong time becomes another idol. The demons confess, but confession can be another form of control; he denies them the narrative.
