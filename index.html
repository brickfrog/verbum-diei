    IIILectioEzekiel 36:23-28

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

    Ezekiel 36:23-28 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    23And I will sanctify my great name, which was profaned among the Gentiles, which you have profaned in the midst of them: that the Gentiles may know that I am the Lord, saith the Lord of hosts, when I shall be sanctified in you before their eyes.

    ☞R 23
    The Name is the stake; Israel is the theater; the nations are the audience.

    24For I will take you from among the Gentiles, and will gather you together out of all the countries, and will bring you into your own land.

    25And I will pour upon you clean water, and you shall be cleansed from all your filthiness, and I will cleanse you from all your idols.

    ☞R 25
    Cleansing is not cosmetic: filth and idols are treated as one contamination.

    26And I will give you a new heart, and put a new spirit within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and will give you a heart of flesh.

    ☞R 26
    Replacement, not repair: stone removed, flesh given—identity by transplant.

    27And I will put my spirit in the midst of you: and I will cause you to walk in my commandments, and to keep my judgments, and do them.

    ☞R 27
    Commandment-keeping is caused, not merely commanded; obedience as effect.

    28And you shall dwell in the land which I gave to your fathers, and you shall be my people, and I will be your God.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 22:1-14

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 22:1-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And Jesus answering, spoke again in parables to them, saying:

    2The kingdom of heaven is likened to a king who made a marriage for his son.

    ☞G 2
    Kingdom framed as wedding: joy with a throne behind it.

    3And he sent his servants to call them that were invited to the marriage: and they would not come.

    4Again he sent other servants, saying: Tell them that were invited, Behold, I have prepared my dinner: my beeves and fatlings are killed, and all things are ready. Come ye to the marriage.

    5But they neglected and went their ways, one to his farm and another to his merchandise.

    ☞G 5
    Refusal is banal: farm, merchandise—ordinary life as contempt.

    6And the rest laid hands on his servants and, having treated them contumeliously, put them to death.

    7But when the king had heard of it, he was angry: and sending his armies, he destroyed those murderers and burnt their city.

    ☞G 7
    Invitation spurned becomes judgment; the feast has an edge.

    8Then he saith to his servants: The marriage indeed is ready; but they that were invited were not worthy.

    9Go ye therefore into the highways; and as many as you shall find, call to the marriage.

    10And his servants going forth into the ways, gathered together all that they found, both bad and good: and the marriage was filled with guests.

    ☞G 10
    Indiscriminate gathering: ‘bad and good’—the net is wide, the sorting later.

    11And the king went in to see the guests: and he saw there a man who had not on a wedding garment.

    ☞G 11
    The king inspects; presence at the feast is not the final criterion.

    12And he saith to him: Friend, how camest thou in hither not having on a wedding garment? But he was silent.

    ☞G 12
    ‘Friend’ is forensic, not tender; the question exposes presumption.

    13Then the king said to the waiters: Bind his hands and feet, and cast him into the exterior darkness. There shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

    ☞G 13
    Hands and feet bound: exclusion is total; darkness is the anti-banquet.

    14For many are called, but few are chosen.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod sanctifies his Name by creating a cleansed, obedient people through his Spirit. The kingdom’s invitation is wide, but only those conformed to the king’s provision are finally admitted.

    In Lectionem

    • R 24,28The sequence is geopolitical restoration followed by covenant formula: return to land culminates in reconstituted belonging. The movement is from dispersion to a re-made people, with God’s self-identification as the terminus.
    • R 23The logic is doxological: God acts to vindicate his public reputation among the nations; Israel’s renewal functions as evidence in a contested world.

    In Evangelium

    • G 3,4,8,9,14The parable runs in two phases: an initial invited class refuses, then the invitation is reissued outward, yet the final word is not inclusion but election. The narrative insists that access is real and broad, while final standing is adjudicated by the king.
    • G 6,12,13Violence against messengers and the silent guest bracket the same point: the king’s authority governs both rejection and improper acceptance. The feast is not a neutral space; it is the king’s order made visible.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ezekiel’s “new heart” is not therapy; it’s a takeover. The problem is not your pain but your embarrassment: the Name has been “profaned,” and you are the stage on which it must be repaired. Clean water is less a bath than an erasure. Idols are not argued with, they are rinsed out. The stony heart is not healed; it is removed. Desire is rewritten at the root so obedience feels like your own impulse. You come home, yes, but as a reclaimed instrument, a body made legible again.

    Matthew’s banquet makes the same move with sharper teeth. The invitation is generous until it is refused; then the king’s desire for a full hall turns punitive, and the city burns as a kind of wounded pride. The highways guests are not “the included”; they are the available, swept in to satisfy the feast’s need to happen. Even inside, the terror remains: one man fails the costume requirement and is bound like contraband. The garment is the new heart in fabric form, the demanded self that proves you’ve been remade. The dread is that the door is wide but the gaze is exacting, and the desire offered is inseparable from domination: come celebrate, but only as the person the king can recognize.

    Semina Verbi

    Ezekiel’s clean water and new heart reads like an alchemical washing that is really a re-formation of desire: the ego’s hard carapace softens so conduct can follow without constant coercion. That resembles Stoic oikeiōsis, where the self is re-centered so virtue becomes second nature, and also the Bhagavad Gita’s shift from restless attachment to steadied action; both imagine an interior reordering that makes obedience feel like freedom.

    Matthew’s banquet turns on a psychological insult: refusing the invitation is refusing relation, and the violence against messengers is the psyche attacking the very summons that could reorganize it. The wedding garment is not etiquette but consent to transformation; showing up unchanged is a covert rejection. That parallels Zen’s insistence on “entering the gate” empty-handed, and Sufi adab as the inner clothing of presence; speculation: the darkness is what it feels like when the self refuses the new heart yet cannot unhear the call.
