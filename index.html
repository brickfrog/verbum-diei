    IVEvangeliumMark 11:27-33

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Mark

    Mark 11:27-33 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    27And they come again to Jerusalem. And when he was walking in the temple, there come to him the chief priests and the scribes and the ancients.

    ☞G 27
    Temple walk: jurisdictional space; the delegation arrives as a composite institution.

    28And they say to him: By what authority dost thou these things? And who hath given thee this authority that thou shouldst do these things?

    ☞G 28
    Authority demanded as paperwork: act reduced to credential.

    29And Jesus answering, said to them: I will also ask you one word. And answer you me: and I will tell you by what authority I do these things.

    ☞G 29
    Counter-question as gatekeeping: they must pass his terms to interrogate him.

    30The baptism of John, was it from heaven or from men? Answer me.

    ☞G 30
    John’s baptism becomes the litmus: heaven/men, no third category.

    31But they thought with themselves, saying: If we say, From heaven; he will say, Why then did you not believe him?

    ☞G 31
    Their calculus is retrospective guilt: belief is the missing receipt.

    32If we say, From men, we fear the people. For all men counted John that he was a prophet indeed.

    ☞G 32
    Fear of people exposes their “authority” as crowd-management.

    33And they answering, say to Jesus: We know not. And Jesus answering, saith to them: Neither do I tell you by what authority I do these things.

    ☞G 33
    “We know not” is strategic ignorance; Jesus mirrors it as judgment.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisDivine authority is recognized by truthful judgment, not conferred by institutions. When leaders treat truth as a tool for self-preservation, they forfeit the right to interrogate Christ.

    In Evangelium

    • G 27,28The scene is a formal challenge: temple elites attempt to reassert control after disruptive acts by forcing Jesus into their authorization framework.
    • G 29,30Jesus relocates the trial to John, making their prior public stance the evidence; the issue is not information but allegiance—whether they can name a divine initiative without losing face.
    • G 31,32Their deliberation reveals a closed system: truth is weighed only by consequences (self-incrimination vs. public backlash), so they cannot render a theological verdict at all.
    • G 33Jesus’ refusal is not evasion but sentence: those who will not judge rightly about John are disqualified from judging his authority, and the temple’s court collapses into silence.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    This scene is not about credentials; it’s about who gets to name reality in public. The priests and scribes want a clean chain of authorization because their power depends on it. Jesus has just acted like the temple is his to judge, and they rush in to force him back into their paperwork.

    He refuses the trap by offering a more intimate test: John. Not doctrine, but desire. John is the embarrassing memory of a moment when the crowd’s hunger for cleansing outran the institution’s control. The leaders don’t answer because any answer would expose what they actually worship: not God, but safety. “From heaven” condemns their cynicism; “from men” risks the crowd. So they choose the one thing that keeps them intact: blankness.

    Jesus’s silence is not evasive; it’s punishment. He withholds the explanation they crave because they have already shown they can’t bear truth unless it protects them. Authority, here, belongs to whoever can endure the consequences of naming what they see. The leaders can’t. Jesus can. And the temple becomes a stage where dread masquerades as prudence, and the desire for control calls itself wisdom.

    Semina Verbi

    Authority here is not a credential but a kind of inner alignment that exposes itself under pressure. The leaders treat truth as a move in a public game; their “we know not” is not humility but self-protection, a refusal to risk status. Jesus’ counterquestion functions like a mirror: if they cannot name the source of a prophet’s legitimacy without calculating fallout, they cannot recognize legitimacy when it walks in front of them.

    This resembles the Zen use of a counterquestion to puncture performative knowing, where the point is not information but revealing attachment to face. It also echoes Socratic elenchus, which forces the interlocutor to see how fear and reputation steer what passes for reason. Speculatively, it parallels the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence that right action flows from a deeper ground than social approval, and the Daoist suspicion that official titles obscure the living Dao.
