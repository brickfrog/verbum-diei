    IIILectio1 Corinthians 2:10-16

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 2:10-16 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    10But to us God hath revealed them by his Spirit. For the Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of God.

    ☞R 10
    Revelation is not deduction; the Spirit is an explorer, not a courier.

    11For what man knoweth the things of a man, but the spirit of a man that is in him? So the things also that are of God, no man knoweth, but the Spirit of God.

    12Now, we have received not the spirit of this world, but the Spirit that is of God: that we may know the things that are given us from God.

    13Which things also we speak: not in the learned words of human wisdom, but in the doctrine of the Spirit, comparing spiritual things with spiritual.

    ☞R 13
    Style is theology: Spirit-speech refuses the prestige dialect of “learned words.”

    14But the sensual man perceiveth not these things that are of the Spirit of God. For it is foolishness to him: and he cannot understand, because it is spiritually examined.

    ☞R 14
    The “sensual” calls it foolish because the instrument is wrong; blindness mistakes itself for critique.

    15But the spiritual man judgeth all things: and he himself is judged of no man.

    ☞R 15
    Judgment runs one-way: the spiritual appraises, yet cannot be appraised by the unqualified.

    16For who hath known the mind of the Lord, that he may instruct him? But we have the mind of Christ.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 4:31-37

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 4:31-37 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    31And he went down into Capharnaum, a city of Galilee: and there he taught them on the sabbath days.

    32And they were astonished at his doctrine: for his speech was with power.

    33And in the synagogue there was a man who had an unclean devil: and he cried out with a loud voice,

    34Saying: Let us alone. What have we to do with thee, Jesus of Nazareth? Art thou come to destroy us? I know thee who thou art, the holy one of God.

    ☞G 34
    The demon’s confession is accurate and useless; orthodoxy can be hostile.

    35And Jesus rebuked him, saying: Hold thy peace and go out of him. And when the devil had thrown him into the midst, he went out of him and hurt him not at all.

    ☞G 35
    A bare imperative breaks a tyrant; no ritual, no negotiation, no spectacle.

    36And there came fear upon all; and they talked among themselves, saying: What word is this, for with authority and power he commandeth the unclean spirits, and they go out?

    ☞G 36
    They notice “word” before wonder: authority is linguistic before it is dramatic.

    37And the fame of him was published into every place of the country.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisThe Spirit grants real participation in God’s own knowledge, creating a mind not native to the world. Christ’s authoritative word manifests that same divine jurisdiction by compelling unclean spirits without appeal.

    In Lectionem

    • R 11,12Paul builds an epistemology with a strict boundary: only God’s own Spirit crosses into God’s own interior. The community’s knowledge is therefore participatory—received—so the content (“given us”) and the capacity to know it arrive together, collapsing any claim to autonomous religious expertise.
    • R 16The citation about the Lord’s mind is not a humility trope but a jurisdictional claim: instruction cannot run upward. “Mind of Christ” names a new corporate subject, a shared intellect formed by union rather than by information.

    In Evangelium

    • G 31,32,37The scene moves from local liturgy to public diffusion: sabbath teaching becomes regional report. Luke frames power as something heard and then carried—doctrine generating fame—so the exorcism functions as verification of a teaching that already arrives with force.
    • G 33,36The unclean spirit’s interruption turns the synagogue into a contested courtroom. The crowd’s question—“What word is this?”—marks a shift from amazement at rhetoric to recognition of a command-structure that reaches beyond human listeners into the invisible order.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “Spirit” is not a comforting mist but an invasive intelligence. It “searches” you the way a blade searches a wound, insisting that the only real knowledge is the kind that bypasses your explanations and rewrites your interior life. The “sensual man” isn’t a libertine; he’s the person who refuses this penetration, who clings to ordinary sense-making as self-defense. “Judges all things” reads like freedom, but it also reads like isolation: once you claim access to the deep things, no one can call you back, and you can’t be reached by the common language anymore.

    In Capernaum the unclean spirit is the most lucid voice in the room. It names Jesus correctly, instantly, and its terror is the honest reaction everyone else hides under astonishment. “Let us alone” is the plea of the psyche that has learned to survive by compartmentalizing its demons; Jesus is the one who won’t permit that truce. The exorcism is not gentle therapy but a public eviction: the man is thrown down in the middle, exposed, made a spectacle, and only then declared unharmed. Authority here feels like desire with teeth: it wants the whole person, and it will tear up the arrangements that kept him functioning.

    Put together, the texts suggest a disturbing intimacy: the holy knows you better than you know yourself, and that knowledge arrives as command. The dread isn’t that God is absent; it’s that God is present, searching, speaking with power, and not negotiating with whatever in you has learned to say, intelligently, “leave me alone.”

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “Spirit” reads like a disciplined faculty of knowing that is not reducible to cleverness: an interior attunement that can recognize meaning only by becoming congruent with it. That has close kinship with Plato’s anamnesis and the later Neoplatonic ascent, where truth is not acquired as data but recollected through likeness; speculation, but Paul’s contrast with “the spirit of this world” feels like a critique of prestige-knowledge in the same key.

    Luke’s exorcism frames evil as a parasitic voice that both knows and resists: it names the holy accurately while trying to control the encounter. That resembles Buddhist accounts of Māra, not as a rival god but as the mind’s sabotaging pattern that can quote the dharma and still cling; and it echoes Sufi descriptions of the nafs that can perform piety while remaining possessive. In each, liberation comes less by argument than by a word of authority that interrupts the compulsive script.

    The “word with power” also parallels mantra and dhāraṇī traditions in Hinduism and Mahāyāna, where speech is not mere sign but act; speculation, but the scene suggests language that reorganizes the psyche rather than persuades it. The demon’s convulsion before release looks like what depth psychology calls symptom flare when a complex is confronted: resistance peaks as it loses its hold.

    “We have the mind of Christ” sits near the Vedāntic claim that knowledge is participation in the real, not representation of it, and near Zen’s insistence that seeing is itself transformation. Different metaphysics, same psychological wager: a new mode of consciousness judges the old, and the old calls it foolish because it cannot inhabit it.
