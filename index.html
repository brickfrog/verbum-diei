    IIILectioEzekiel 33:7-9

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

    Ezekiel 33:7-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    7So thou, O son of man, I have made thee a watchman to the house of Israel: therefore thou shalt hear the word from my mouth, and shalt tell it them from me.

    ☞R 7
    Watchman: not seer but sentry; hearing precedes speaking; mouth-to-mouth chain of custody.

    8When I say to the wicked: O wicked man, thou shalt surely die: if thou dost not speak to warn the wicked man from his way: that wicked man shall die in his iniquity, but I will require his blood at thy hand.

    ☞R 8
    Silence becomes complicity; the wicked dies for himself, the watchman is charged for another’s blood.

    9But if thou tell the wicked man, that he may be converted from his ways, and he be not converted from his way he shall die in his iniquity: but thou hast delivered thy soul.

    ☞R 9
    Outcome is not yours; obligation is. Conversion offered, refusal permitted; the messenger survives by discharge, not success.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 18:15-20

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 18:15-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    15But if thy brother shall offend against thee, go, and rebuke him between thee and him alone. If he shall hear thee, thou shalt gain thy brother.

    ☞G 15
    Private first: correction without spectacle; the aim is acquisition—“gain” not victory.

    16And if he will not hear thee, take with thee one or two more: that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may stand.

    ☞G 16
    Escalation is juridical: witnesses stabilize speech; truth is made to stand, not to sparkle.

    17And if he will not hear them: tell the church. And if he will not hear the church, let him be to thee as the heathen and publican.

    ☞G 17
    Church as court of last appeal; refusal hardens into a new category—outside the covenantal ‘brother.’

    18Amen I say to you, whatsoever you shall bind upon earth, shall be bound also in heaven: and whatsoever you shall loose upon earth, shall be loosed also in heaven.

    ☞G 18
    Binding/loosing: earthly decisions carry heavenly ratification; authority is real and dangerous.

    19Again I say to you, that if two of you shall consent upon earth, concerning anything whatsoever they shall ask, it shall be done to them by my Father who is in heaven.

    20For where there are two or three gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them.

    ☞G 20
    Small-number presence: Christ locates himself in gathered judgment, not in solitary certainty.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod binds his people to speak and judge under delegated authority: silence and disorder both incur guilt. Christ authorizes the church’s disciplined process so that earthly correction and exclusion carry heavenly consequence.

    In Lectionem

    • R 7,8The passage frames moral speech as delegated office: God installs a human intermediary so that judgment is not mute. Responsibility is redistributed—guilt remains with the wicked, but accountability for warning is assigned to the appointed speaker.
    • R 8,9The logic is forensic rather than therapeutic: the question is not whether the warning persuades, but whether the required testimony was rendered. Salvation-language appears as legal deliverance—one’s own life is secured by faithful notification.

    In Evangelium

    • G 15,17A graded procedure is established for handling sin inside the community: from personal confrontation to communal adjudication to exclusion. The point is to prevent both private resentment and public chaos by giving offense a controlled path.
    • G 18,19The community’s disciplinary and petitionary acts are placed under a vertical correspondence: heaven is not merely observing but confirming. This makes ecclesial decisions weighty—neither arbitrary nor merely administrative.
    • G 16,20The minimum quorum (two or three) links evidence, agreement, and presence: the same small assembly that can establish a matter can also act with Christ’s attendant authority. The church is defined less by scale than by ordered concurrence in his name.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    These readings don’t praise courage; they conscript it. The “watchman” is a man turned into an instrument of other people’s outcomes, told that silence makes him a murderer by proxy. It’s not holiness, it’s hostage logic: speak or you inherit the blood. Desire is recast as duty, and duty is enforced by terror.

    Matthew sharpens the same mechanism into procedure. Intimacy becomes a tribunal: first a private wound, then witnesses, then the crowd. The goal is called “gaining your brother,” but the method is escalation, a slow conversion of relationship into casework. “Let him be as the heathen” isn’t just exclusion; it’s the relief of finally being allowed to stop wanting him.

    “Bind and loose” reads like spiritual authority, but it functions as psychological permission. The group gets to make its verdict feel cosmic, to turn consensus into heaven’s echo. “Two or three gathered” is not comfort; it’s the thrill and dread of a small circle discovering that together they can name reality, and that their naming will not be contradicted.

    Semina Verbi

    Ezekiel’s watchman is the psyche’s burden of foresight: once you see the cliff, silence makes you complicit. That resembles the bodhisattva vow in Mahayana Buddhism, where insight incurs obligation to intervene; speculation: the “blood at thy hand” feels like karmic entanglement translated into legal imagery. It also echoes the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence on svadharma, the duty you cannot evade without inner corrosion.

    Matthew’s staged confrontation reads like a technology for preserving relationship without feeding the ego’s appetite for spectacle. The private first step parallels Confucian remonstrance, where correction is loyal only if it is tactful and proportionate; speculation: “two or three witnesses” functions like li, a ritual container that keeps truth from becoming mere power. “Bind and loose” resembles rabbinic halakhic authority, but psychologically it is the community naming what is real and what is released, akin to restorative justice practices.

    “Two or three gathered” has a close cousin in the Hindu and Buddhist idea of satsang: presence intensifies when intention is shared. Speculation: it also matches the Sufi sense of baraka in companionship, where the invisible becomes palpable not in solitude but in a small, aligned circle.
