    IIILectioEphesians 1:1-10

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Letter of St. Paul to the Ephesians

    Ephesians 1:1-10 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ, by the will of God, to all the saints who are at Ephesus and to the faithful in Christ Jesus.

    2Grace be to you and peace, from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ.

    3Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who hath blessed us with spiritual blessings in heavenly places, in Christ:

    4As he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and unspotted in his sight in charity.

    ☞R 4
    Election precedes ethics; holiness is downstream of choice.

    5Who hath predestinated us unto the adoption of children through Jesus Christ unto himself: according to the purpose of his will:

    ☞R 5
    Adoption is not metaphor but transfer of status; will, not merit, is the engine.

    6Unto the praise of the glory of his grace, in which he hath graced us, in his beloved son.

    7In whom we have redemption through his blood, the remission of sins, according to the riches of his, grace,

    ☞R 7
    Blood is the currency; forgiveness is priced, not waved through.

    8Which hath superabounded in us, in all wisdom and prudence,

    9That he might make known unto us the mystery of his will, according to his good pleasure, which he hath purposed in him,

    10In the dispensation of the fulness of times, to re-establish all things in Christ, that are in heaven and on earth, in him.

    ☞R 10
    History has a telos: recapitulation—everything dragged back under one head.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s will elects and orders history toward Christ, and the same history exposes institutions that sanctify the dead while resisting the living word. Redemption gathers all things under Christ; refusal of that gathering becomes culpable obstruction.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,2The address and blessing establish a vertical chain of authority: God’s will → apostolic commission → sanctified recipients. The community is defined by location (“at Ephesus”) and by incorporation (“in Christ”), collapsing geography into participation.
    • R 3,6,8,9The passage is a doxology that functions like a legal preamble: it names benefits, grounds them in divine initiative, and discloses the rationale—glory, grace, and a disclosed “mystery.” Wisdom and prudence are not human achievements here but the mode in which grace arrives.

    In Evangelium

    • G 49The “wisdom of God” speaks as a sender of witnesses; the pattern is not accidental but programmatic. Prophets and apostles are dispatched into a system already disposed to reject them.
    • G 47,48,52,53,54Jesus targets institutional continuity: building tombs and controlling access to knowledge are two forms of managing the past and the present. The reaction—interrogation and entrapment—shows the mechanism by which institutions defend themselves: convert revelation into a prosecutable statement.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ephesians is a love letter written in the vocabulary of administration. “Chosen before the foundation” isn’t comfort; it’s the terror of being decided in advance, drafted into holiness like a job you can’t quit. “Adoption” sounds tender, but it’s also a legal transfer of name and loyalty, a new Father whose “good pleasure” is the only reason you exist. Even the “mystery” is not intimacy so much as controlled disclosure: you are told just enough to consent. Redemption “through his blood” reads like the price of admission into a family that insists it never needed you, yet demands your whole self, spotless.

    Luke shows what people do when they can’t bear that demand: they aestheticize it. They build monuments to prophets as a way to keep prophecy safely dead. The tomb is a compliment that silences. Jesus names the family resemblance: you didn’t kill them, but you inherit the appetite and call it reverence. The “key of knowledge” is withheld not by ignorance but by a jealous gatekeeping; the lawyers prefer a locked door they control to an open door that would change them. So they swarm his speech, not to learn but to trap, because accusation is easier than conversion.

    Put together, the dread sharpens: a God who “re-establishes all things” threatens every little kingdom, every curated identity. Predestination becomes the ultimate insult to self-authorship, and the monuments become the coping mechanism—pious architecture built to avoid being chosen. Desire runs under both texts: the desire to belong without being remade, to praise grace while keeping the key in your own pocket.

    Semina Verbi

    Ephesians’ “before the foundation of the world” reads like the Stoic logos spermatikos: a rational pattern seeded through reality, not earned, only consented to. Speculation: it also brushes against Advaita’s sense that liberation is recognition of what already is, though here the “already” is personal and willed, not impersonal.

    The adoption motif has a quiet kinship with Roman legal adoption and with Confucian ritual belonging: identity conferred by a household and its practices, not manufactured by private will. Speculation: Pure Land Buddhism’s “other-power” devotion parallels the relief of being carried by grace rather than self-justification, while keeping a different metaphysics.

    Luke’s denunciation of monument-building matches a universal defense: turning living truth into commemorative stone so it can’t interrogate you. The Hebrew prophets, the Sufi poets, and the Zen iconoclasts all warn about reverence as a way to domesticate the dangerous.

    The “key of knowledge” taken away echoes Plato’s cave and the Upanishadic complaint that words can become a screen. It is not ignorance but gatekeeping: the priestly instinct to control access to meaning, then call the control “order.”

    The demand for “blood…from the foundation of the world” sounds like karma as inherited consequence, but psychologically: a generation can carry the unpaid moral debt of its institutions. Speculation: Girard’s scapegoat mechanism sits nearby—communities stabilize themselves by sacrificing the truth-teller, then sanctifying the tomb.
