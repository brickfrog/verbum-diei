    IIILectioLamentations 2:2,10-14,18-19

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Lamentations

    Lamentations 2:2,10-14,18-19 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    2Beth. The Lord hath cast down headlong, and hath not spared, all that was beautiful in Jacob: he hath destroyed in his wrath the strong holds of the virgin of Juda, and brought them down to the ground: he hath made the kingdom unclean, and the princes thereof.

    ☞R 2
    Beauty is not spared; judgment is aesthetic as well as political.

    10Jod. The ancients of the daughter of Sion sit upon the ground, they have held their peace: they have sprinkled their heads with dust, they are girded with haircloth, the virgins of Jerusalem hang down their heads to the ground.

    ☞R 10
    Elders on the ground: authority reduced to posture.

    11Caph. My eyes have failed with weeping, my bowels are troubled: my liver is poured out upon the earth, for the destruction of the daughter of my people, when the children, and the sucklings, fainted away in the streets of the city.

    12Lamed. They said to their mothers: Where is corn and wine? when they fainted away as the wounded in the streets of the city: when they breathed out their souls in the bosoms of their mothers.

    ☞R 12
    The last refuge is the mother’s body; even that becomes a place of expiration.

    13Mem. To what shall I compare thee? or to what shall I liken thee, O daughter of Jerusalem? to what shall I equal thee, that I may comfort thee, O virgin daughter of Sion? for great as the sea is thy destruction: who shall heal thee?

    ☞R 13
    No analogy can domesticate this ruin; comfort fails for lack of likeness.

    14Nun. Thy prophets have seen false and foolish things for thee: and they have not laid open thy iniquity, to excite thee to penance: but they have seen for thee false revelations and banishments.

    ☞R 14
    Prophecy as anesthesia: visions that protect guilt from exposure.

    18Sade. Their heart cried to the Lord upon the walls of the daughter of Sion: Let tears run down like a torrent day and night: give thyself no rest, and let not the apple of thy eye cease.

    ☞R 18
    Tears are commanded into liturgy; grief is disciplined into a torrent.

    19Coph. Arise, give praise in the night, in the beginning of the watches: pour out thy heart like water, before the face of the Lord: lift up thy hands to him for the life of thy little children, that have fainted for hunger at the top of all the streets.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 8:5-17

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 8:5-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    5And when he had entered into Capharnaum, there came to him a centurion, beseeching him,

    6And saying, Lord, my servant lieth at home sick of the palsy, and is grievously tormented.

    7And Jesus saith to him: I will come and heal him.

    8And the centurion, making answer, said: Lord, I am not worthy that thou shouldst enter under my roof; but only say the word, and my servant shall be healed.

    ☞G 8
    Unworthiness becomes the doorway; distance is solved by speech.

    9For I also am a man subject to authority, having under me soldiers; and I say to this, Go, and he goeth, and to another Come, and he cometh, and to my servant, Do this, and he doeth it.

    ☞G 9
    He reads the world as chain-of-command; faith is an argument from authority.

    10And Jesus hearing this, marvelled; and said to them that followed him. Amen I say to you, I have not found so great faith in Israel.

    ☞G 10
    Israel is measured and found smaller than a foreigner’s trust.

    11And I say to you that many shall come from the east and the west, and shall sit down with Abraham, and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven:

    12But the children of the kingdom shall be cast out into the exterior darkness: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

    ☞G 12
    Inheritance can be forfeited; darkness is the counter-sacrament.

    13And Jesus said to the centurion: Go, and as thou hast believed, so be it done to thee. And the servant was healed at the same hour.

    14And when Jesus was come into Peter's house, he saw his wife's mother lying, and sick of a fever;

    15And he touched her hand, and the fever left her, and she arose and ministered to them.

    ☞G 15
    Touch converts fever into service; healing recruits.

    16And when evening was come, they brought to him many that were possessed with devils: and he cast out the spirits with his word: and all that were sick he healed:

    17That it might be fulfilled, which was spoken by the prophet Isaias, saying: He took our infirmities, and bore our diseases.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s judgment strips false securities and exposes guilt; God’s word, received in faith, exercises sovereign authority to heal and to reconstitute the people beyond inherited claims.

    In Lectionem

    • R 11The poem’s center is bodily collapse as civic theology: the city’s fall is narrated through organs, milk, and streets, making catastrophe unavoidably intimate.
    • R 19The night-watch summons turns lament into a procedural act—prayer as siegecraft—aimed not at explanation but at survival of the children.

    In Evangelium

    • G 7Jesus’ readiness to enter the centurion’s house is a quiet breach of boundary; the centurion refuses the honor, forcing the encounter onto the plane of sheer command.
    • G 11The banquet saying relocates belonging from bloodline to response, making the kingdom a table with unexpected guests and excluded heirs.
    • G 16,17The sequence of healings culminates in Isaiah’s frame: cures are not mere repairs but a transfer—another bears what others suffer.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Lamentations refuses the comfort of meaning. The city is not merely punished; it is unmade, made “unclean,” its beauty hurled down as if beauty itself were the provocation. The old sit in mute shock, the young collapse into the only remaining posture: the head to the ground. The most obscene image is not the rubble but the children dying in their mothers’ arms, desire for corn and wine turning into a final question with no answer. The prophets are indicted as anesthetists: they offered visions that kept the wound closed, so the rot could deepen.

    Then Matthew turns and the shock is that power appears not as consolation but as command. The centurion understands language as force: a word that makes bodies obey. His “not worthy” is not humility so much as erotic distance, the thrill of authority that can act without touch, without intimacy, without risk. Jesus admires that cold clarity and immediately sharpens it into exclusion: the insiders cast into exterior darkness. Healing arrives, but it arrives with a border.

    Put together, the dread is that catastrophe and cure share a grammar. In Lamentations, words failed because they lied; in Matthew, words succeed because they dominate. The desire underneath both is unbearable: to be ruled by a voice strong enough to stop the crying, even if that voice also decides who stays in the light and who gnashes in the dark.

    Semina Verbi

    Lamentations’ grief that cannot find a comparison—“great as the sea”—has a close cousin in Greek tragedy, where catastrophe exceeds measure and language breaks under it; the chorus can only keep vigil with tears, like a civic liturgy of loss. The demand to weep without rest also resembles Buddhist dukkha seen without anesthesia: not punishment but the mind forced to look straight at impermanence and the cost of attachment; speculation, but the text’s insistence on unblinking attention feels shared.

    The centurion’s trust in a healing word at a distance parallels the Tao Te Ching’s confidence in non-coercive efficacy: action that works by alignment, not intrusion; speculation, but the logic of authority-as-order rather than authority-as-force is similar. His “not worthy” reads like Confucian li at its best, a humility that preserves relationship by refusing entitlement. The widening table “from east and west” echoes Stoic cosmopolitanism and the Bhagavad Gita’s claim that the path can meet people where they are: belonging grounded in inward disposition rather than pedigree, offered without contempt for the old household.

    The healings that end in service—fever lifted, then she ministers—touch a Hindu and Buddhist motif: liberation expresses itself as ordinary care, not spectacle. And the line about bearing infirmities has an analogue in the bodhisattva vow and in Kuan Yin’s compassion: a figure who takes suffering into themselves so others can breathe; speculation, but the emotional grammar matches.
