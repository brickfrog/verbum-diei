    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Proverbs

    Proverbs 30:5-9 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    5Every word of God is fire tried: he is a buckler to them that hope in him.

    ☞R 5
    The word is tested; the shield is for hopers, not analysts.

    6Add not any thing to his words, lest thou be reproved and found a liar:

    ☞R 6
    To edit revelation is to manufacture perjury.

    7Two things I have asked of thee, deny them not to me before I die.

    8Remove far from me vanity, and lying words. Give me neither beggary, nor riches: give me only the necessaries of life:

    ☞R 8
    The prayer is for measure: enough to live, not enough to forget.

    9Lest perhaps being filled, I should be tempted to deny, and say: Who is the Lord? or being compelled by poverty, I should steal, and forswear the name of my God.

    ☞R 9
    Satiety breeds atheism; scarcity breeds sacrilege.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 9:1-6

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 9:1-6 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Then calling together the twelve apostles, he gave them power and authority over all devils and to cure diseases.

    ☞G 1
    Authority precedes itinerary: power is issued, not discovered.

    2And he sent them to preach the kingdom of God and to heal the sick.

    3And he said to them: Take nothing for your journey, neither staff, nor scrip, nor bread, nor money; neither have two coats.

    ☞G 3
    Radical lightness: the mission is not financed by contingency plans.

    4And whatsoever house you shall enter into, abide there and depart not from thence.

    ☞G 4
    Stability as discipline: no shopping for better hosts.

    5And whosoever will not receive you, when ye go out of that city, shake off even the dust of your feet, for a testimony against them.

    ☞G 5
    Rejection is recorded, not avenged; dust becomes evidence.

    6And going out, they went about through the towns, preaching the gospel and healing every where.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s word admits no supplementation, and discipleship therefore lives by received authority rather than self-provision. The faithful ask for sufficiency and are sent to witness a kingdom that validates itself in both truth and healing.

    In Lectionem

    • R 7The speaker frames his petition as a bounded request “before I die,” placing ethics under eschatological pressure: life is short, so the terms must be clear.

    In Evangelium

    • G 2,6The commission fuses proclamation and repair; the kingdom is announced in speech and made credible in embodied restoration, then replicated as a pattern across towns.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Proverbs is not pious restraint; it is fear of the self. The speaker knows the mind is a hungry animal that turns any surplus into amnesia and any lack into crime. “Give me neither beggary nor riches” is not moderation but damage control, a plea to be kept in the narrow band where a person can still recognize his own name. The dread is that identity is conditional, and God is the first thing the satiated forget.

    Luke answers with a harsher experiment: strip the apostles down until they can’t buy dignity or pack backup selves. No staff, no bag, no extra coat—no private insurance against humiliation. They must live on other people’s thresholds, exposed to refusal, forced to feel how quickly the world closes. Power over demons comes paired with powerlessness in the street.

    The desire underneath both texts is dangerous: to be clean of lies. But the only way offered is dependence so total it risks contempt. The kingdom is preached by men who can be turned away, and the dust-shaking is not righteous theater; it’s the bitter proof that rejection still clings to you, so you ritualize it into a gesture and keep walking.

    Semina Verbi

    This psychology of speech and need has close cousins. The Vedic sense of mantra as tested heat, refined by austerity, sits near the image of words proven in fire; speculation: both treat language as something that either carries reality or collapses into noise when adulterated. Zen’s distrust of “adding” through conceptual overlay echoes the warning against embellishment, not as anti-intellectualism but as a guardrail against self-serving narration.

    The request for “enough” matches Aristotle’s mean and Epicurus’ plain sufficiency, but with a sharper moral diagnosis: surplus breeds amnesia, lack breeds coercion. Buddhism’s Middle Way and the monastic rule of few possessions align with the travel command to carry nothing; speculation: deprivation here functions like exposure therapy, stripping the ego’s props so attention can reattach to what is given.

    The apostolic pattern resembles the Hindu sannyasi and the Jain mendicant: authority expressed as vulnerability, power shown by not stockpiling. The house-to-house dependence recalls Islamic tawakkul, trust enacted through reliance on hospitality rather than reserves. Shaking dust has a Stoic and Cynic analogue: refusal met without rancor, the self kept unentangled; speculation: it is boundary-setting ritualized, preventing rejection from becoming identity.
