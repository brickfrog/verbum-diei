IIILectio2 Samuel 24:2,9-17

Lectio Prima

From the Second Book of Samuel

2 Samuel 24:2,9-17 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

2And the king said to Joab the general of his army: Go through all the tribes of Israel from Dan to Bersabee, and number ye the people that I may know the number of them.

☞R 2
The census as lust for certainty: kings count what God promises.

9And Joab gave up the sum of the number of the people to the king, and there were found of Israel eight hundred thousand valiant men that drew the sword: and of Juda five hundred thousand fighting men.

10But David's heart struck him, after the people were numbered: and David said to the Lord: I have sinned very much in what I have done: but I pray thee, O Lord, to take away the iniquity of thy servant, because I have done exceeding foolishly. David's heart struck him, after the people were numbered... That is he was touched with a great remorse for the vanity and pride which had put him upon numbering the people.

☞R 10
Conscience arrives late; the sin is already filed in the archives.

11And David arose in the morning, and the word of the Lord came to Gad the prophet and the seer of David, saying:

12Go, and say to David: Thus saith the Lord: I give thee thy choice of three things, choose one of them which thou wilt, that I may do it to thee.

☞R 12
Judgment offered as a “choice”: freedom preserved even in sentencing.

13And when Gad was come to David, he told him, saying: Either seven years of famine shall come to thee in thy land: or thou shalt flee three months before thy adversaries, and they shall pursue thee: or for three days there shall be a pestilence in thy land. Now therefore deliberate, and see what answer I shall return to him that sent me.

14And David said to Gad: I am in a great strait: but it is better that I should fall into the hands of the Lord (for his mercies are many) than into the hands of men.

☞R 14
He selects the Judge over men: better the absolute than the arbitrary.

15And the Lord sent a pestilence upon Israel, from the morning unto the time appointed, and there died of the people from Dan to Bersabee seventy thousand men.

16And when the angel of the Lord had stretched out his hand over Jerusalem to destroy it, the Lord had pity on the affliction, and said to the angel that slew the people: It is enough: now hold thy hand. And the angel of the Lord was by the thrashingfloor of Areuna the Jebusite.

☞R 16
The angel pauses over Jerusalem; destruction halted at a threshingfloor—judgment meeting sacrifice-space.

17And David said to the Lord, when he saw the angel striking the people: It is I; I am he that have sinned, I have done wickedly: these that are the sheep, what have they done? let thy hand, I beseech thee, be turned against me, and against my father's house.

IVEvangeliumMark 6:1-6

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Mark

Mark 6:1-6 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1And going out from thence, he went into his own country; and his disciples followed him.

2And when the Sabbath was come, he began to teach in the synagogue: and many hearing him were in admiration at his doctrine, saying: How came this man by all these things? and what wisdom is this that is given to him, and such mighty works as are wrought by his hands?

☞G 2
Astonishment without surrender: curiosity posing as discernment.

3Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary, the brother of James, and Joseph, and Jude, and Simon? are not also his sisters here with us? And they were scandalized in regard of him.

☞G 3
They reduce the Word to a trade and a family tree; familiarity as dismissal.

4And Jesus said to them: A prophet is not without honour, but in his own country, and in his own house, and among his own kindred.

☞G 4
The proverb names the scandal: proximity breeds contempt, not clarity.

5And he could not do any miracles there, only that he cured a few that were sick, laying his hands upon them. He could not... Not for want of power, but because he would not work miracles in favour of obstinate and incredulous people, who were unworthy of such favours.

☞G 5
“Could not” as judicial restraint: power withheld where faith is refused.

6And he wondered because of their unbelief, and he went through the villages round about teaching.

☞G 6
He marvels at their unbelief; their limits become the occasion of his continued itinerancy.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s power is not managed by human reckoning or human familiarity: pride provokes judgment, and unbelief provokes withdrawal. Mercy remains sovereign, but it is not distributed on the terms of the proud or the contemptuous.

In Lectionem

  • R 9The numbers are given, but no victory follows; data produces only vulnerability, exposing that Israel’s strength is not a statistic but a covenantal gift.
  • R 13The triad of punishments traces a descent from slow deprivation (famine), to political humiliation (flight), to pure mortality (pestilence): the king is made to measure what he tried to measure.
  • R 15The plague moves “from Dan to Bersabee,” the same span as the command to count—judgment mirrors the ambition’s geography.
  • R 17David attempts substitution: the shepherd offers himself for the sheep, but his offer cannot unkill the dead; royal guilt has public consequences even when confessed.

In Evangelium

  • G 1Return to “his own country” frames the episode as a test of reception: the local audience becomes the decisive courtroom for revelation.
  • G 2Their questions are framed as procurement—“how came,” “what wisdom is given”—treating grace as an import that must be explained, not as a claim that must be obeyed.
  • G 6The narrative resolves not with defeat but with redirection: rejection does not end the mission; it multiplies it into surrounding villages.

Lectio Heterodoxa

David’s census is not a bookkeeping error; it’s erotic. He wants the nation as a number because a number can be possessed without being loved. Counting is a way of touching without touch, of turning living men into a smooth surface of control. Then the recoil: his heart strikes him, the body protesting what the mind craved. The punishment is shaped like his desire. He wanted a total; he receives a sweeping subtraction. The pestilence is the dark twin of administration: the same reach, the same abstraction, moving from Dan to Beersheba, making bodies disappear as cleanly as ink.

His choice is the most revealing line. Better God than men, he says, because God’s violence feels intimate, like a hand that hurts you because it knows you. But the intimacy is obscene: the king survives while seventy thousand “sheep” pay for the shepherd’s appetite. His late offer—take me instead—sounds noble and also theatrical, the guilty man trying to reclaim the narrative at the last second, to be the protagonist even of catastrophe. The angel pauses at a threshing floor, that place where grain is separated from husk. The story insists that kingship is a machine that always threshes someone.

In Nazareth the same hunger for control takes a smaller, pettier form. They reduce Jesus to his job and his family, as if naming him can domesticate him. Familiarity becomes a weapon: if we can file you under “carpenter,” we don’t have to desire what you’re offering. Their scandal is not prudishness; it’s envy. They can’t bear that something extraordinary might have grown in their own dirt without their permission.

So “he could not do miracles there” reads less like divine limitation than like refusal to be used. Miracles would become just another census, proof to tally, a power to own. The dread in both readings is that the sacred won’t cooperate with our accounting. Desire wants mastery; the holy keeps slipping out of the hand that counts.

Semina Verbi

David’s census is the mind’s compulsion to convert living dependence into controllable inventory. Numbering becomes a charm against fragility: if I can count it, I can master it. The remorse is the snap-back of conscience when the psyche notices it has traded trust for calculation. The “choice” of punishments externalizes what guilt already knows: every strategy of control carries a cost, and the least degrading suffering is the one that keeps the self from outsourcing cruelty to other humans.

The plague reads as leadership’s shadow: private vanity metastasizes into public consequence. David’s sudden desire to take the blow himself is not nobility tacked on at the end; it is the first moment he stops treating people as totals and starts seeing them as sheep. He can face God because God is not a rival power; he cannot face “the hands of men” because men will make his error into a political weapon. His plea is a late recovery of the personal.

In Nazareth, the crowd’s contempt is the small-town defense against the uncanny. Familiarity is used as a solvent: reduce the threatening voice to its résumé, and nothing new can demand surrender. Their “scandal” is not moral outrage; it is status-maintenance. The refusal of wonder shrinks the field of the possible, so healing becomes rare not by lack of power but by the community’s decision to keep reality domesticated.

Speculation: this pair stages two failures of relation—one vertical, one horizontal. David breaks relation upward by counting; Nazareth breaks relation outward by dismissing. In both, the cure is exposure: remorse that punctures pride, and astonishment that punctures cynicism.

Semina Verbi: the census-as-delusion echoes Taoism’s suspicion of naming and measuring as a loss of the Way, and Buddhist critiques of grasping that turns fluid life into objects. David choosing the “hands of the Lord” resembles Islamic tawakkul, where surrender is strength because it refuses human vengeance. Nazareth’s reduction of the prophet to “just a carpenter” parallels Zen’s warning that conceptual familiarity blinds, and the Sufi theme that the friend of God is hidden by ordinariness. Speculation: the “unworked miracle” motif rhymes with Stoic thought that a closed assent makes even truth ineffective.
