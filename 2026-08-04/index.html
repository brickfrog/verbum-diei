    IIILectioJeremiah 30:1-2,12-15,18-22

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Jeremiah

    Jeremiah 30:1-2,12-15,18-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1This is the word that came to Jeremias from the Lord, saying:

    2Thus saith the Lord, the God of Israel, saying: Write thee all the words that I have spoken to thee, in a book.

    ☞R 2
    Revelation is archived: command to write precedes comfort.

    12For thus saith the Lord: Thy bruise is incurable, thy wound is very grievous.

    ☞R 12
    “Incurable” names judgment as diagnosis, not mood.

    13There is none to judge thy judgment to bind it up: thou hast no healing medicines.

    14All thy lovers have forgotten thee, and will not seek after thee: for I have wounded thee with the wound of an enemy, with cruel chastisement: by reason of the multitude of thy iniquities, thy sins are hardened.

    ☞R 14
    Lovers vanish; the enemy’s wound is God’s hand.

    15Why criest thou for thy affliction? thy sorrow is incurable: for the multitude of thy iniquity, and for thy hardened sins I have done these things to thee.

    ☞R 15
    Tears don’t refute guilt; they confirm consequence.

    18Thus saith the Lord: Behold I will bring back the captivity of the pavilions of Jacob, and will have pity on his houses, and the city shall be built in her high place, and the temple shall be founded according to the order thereof.

    19And out of them shall come forth praise, and the voice of them that play: and I will multiply them, and they shall not be made few: and I will glorify them, and they shall not be lessened.

    20And their children shall be as from the beginning, and their assembly shall be permanent before me: and I will visit against all that afflict them.

    21And their leader shall be of themselves: and their prince shall come forth from the midst of them: and I will bring him near, and he shall come to me: for who is this that setteth his heart to approach to me, saith the Lord?

    ☞R 21
    The prince rises from within, yet must be “brought near” to approach.

    22And you shall be my people: and I will be your God.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 15:1-2,10-14

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 15:1-2,10-14 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Then came to him from Jerusalem scribes and Pharisees, saying:

    2Why do thy disciples transgress the tradition of the ancients? For they wash not their hands when they eat bread.

    ☞G 2
    Tradition polices bread; the charge is small because the power is large.

    10And having called together the multitudes unto him, he said to them: Hear ye and understand.

    11Not that which goeth into the mouth defileth a man: but what cometh out of the mouth, this defileth a man. Not that which goeth into, etc... No uncleanness in meat, nor any dirt contracted by eating it with unwashed hands, can defile the soul: but sin alone; or a disobedience of the heart to the ordinance and will of God. And thus when Adam took the forbidden fruit, it was not the apple, which entered into the mouth, but the disobedience to the law of God which defiled him. The same is to be said if a Jew, in the time of the old law, had eaten swine's flesh; or a Christian convert, in the days of the apostles, contrary to their ordinance, had eaten blood; or if any of the faithful at present should transgress the ordinance of God's church, by breaking the fasts: for in all these cases the soul would be defiled; not indeed by that which goeth into the mouth; but by the disobedience of the heart, in wilfully transgressing the ordinance of God, or of those who have their authority from him.

    ☞G 11
    Defilement is outbound: speech as sewer of the heart.

    12Then came his disciples, and said to him: Dost thou know that the Pharisees, when they heard this word, were scandalized?

    13But he answering, said: Every plant which my heavenly Father hath not planted, shall be rooted up.

    ☞G 13
    Unplanted growth is not pruned but uprooted—no reform, only removal.

    14Let them alone: they are blind, and leaders of the blind. And if the blind lead the blind, both fall into the pit.

    ☞G 14
    Blindness is contagious; leadership is a shared fall.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod judges and restores by reordering the heart and the community, not by managing external purity. Authority that is not planted by the Father will be uprooted, and only the one drawn near may lead without falling.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,18,19,20,22The oracle moves from authorized speech to civic reconstruction: houses, city, temple, then liturgy (“praise”) and demography (“multiply”). Restoration is not private consolation but reconstitution of a polity under divine inspection, including retaliation against oppressors and a renewed covenant formula.
    • R 21The riddle—“who is this that setteth his heart to approach”—frames access to God as hazardous privilege. Legitimate rule is defined by proximity to God, not by foreign appointment; yet proximity is granted, not seized.

    In Evangelium

    • G 1,10,12,14A delegation from the center interrogates a peripheral teacher; Jesus bypasses the tribunal by addressing the crowd, then refuses to manage elite offense. The disciples’ concern for scandal is met with a policy of non-engagement: false authority is left to collapse under its own blindness.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Jeremiah’s God speaks like an abuser who insists on authorship. Write it down, he says: make the wound official. The bruise is “incurable,” lovers are gone, and the voice behind the sentence is the same voice that inflicted it. The cruelty isn’t a problem to solve; it’s a bond to accept. Then, without apology, restoration arrives as a kind of captivity rearranged: the city rebuilt, music returning, a leader rising “from the midst.” Desire is permitted again, but only after dread has taught the body who owns it.

    Matthew sharpens the same dynamic by shifting the battlefield inward. The Pharisees police the surface, because surface is controllable; Jesus refuses their hygiene not to free people from rules, but to relocate defilement to speech, to the tongue as the leak of the self. What comes out exposes what you serve. His verdict is pitiless: uproot the unplanted, let the blind fall. The scandal isn’t that tradition is wrong; it’s that some traditions are a way of staying numb, and he wants the raw, dangerous truth of the heart on record, like Jeremiah’s book—because only what is confessed can be possessed.

    Semina Verbi

    Jeremiah’s “incurable wound” that is nonetheless met by a return and a rebuilt city echoes the Greek tragic sense that a house can be cursed by its own acts yet still be re-founded by a hard-won turn in fate, as in Aeschylus where justice arrives after ruin. It also resembles the Buddhist diagnosis-and-cure structure: the recognition that craving and habit make suffering feel “incurable,” yet a path reorders the inner city; that parallel is speculative, but the psychological contour matches.

    Matthew’s shift from external contamination to what issues from the mouth converges with Stoic moral psychology: the only true stain is a disordered assent, not the world’s materials. It also aligns with Confucian suspicion of empty ritual when it is severed from ren and yi; rites without inward formation become performance that blinds. “Every plant not planted” has a close cousin in Hindu and Jain talk of karmic seeds: what is not rooted in right intention cannot bear wholesome fruit; speculative, but the shared image of cultivation versus invasive growth is striking.
