    IIILectio1 Kings 21:1-16

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Book of Kings

    1 Kings 21:1-16 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And after these things, Naboth the Jezrahelite, who was in Jezrahel, had at that time a vineyard, near the palace of Achab, king of Samaria.

    2And Achab spoke to Naboth, saying: Give me thy vineyard, that I may make me a garden of herbs, because it is nigh, and adjoining to my house; and I will give thee for it a better vineyard: or if thou think it more convenient for thee, I will give thee the worth of it in money.

    ☞R 2
    The king speaks in market terms; he wants to rezone an inheritance into a herb-garden.

    3Naboth answered him: The Lord be merciful to me, and not let me give thee the inheritance of my fathers.

    ☞R 3
    Refusal is theological, not economic: land as fathers’ portion, not a commodity.

    4And Achab came into his house angry and fretting, because of the word that Naboth, the Jezrahelite, had spoken to him, saying: I will not give thee the inheritance of my fathers. And casting himself upon his bed, he turned away his face to the wall, and would eat no bread.

    ☞R 4
    Royal power reduced to sulking appetite; the wall gets his face, bread gets refused.

    5And Jezabel, his wife, went in to him, and said to him: What is the matter that thy soul is so grieved? and why eatest thou no bread?

    6And he answered her: I spoke to Naboth, the Jezrahelite, and said to him: Give me thy vineyard, and take money for it: or if it please thee, I will give thee a better vineyard for it. And he said: I will not give thee my vineyard.

    7Then Jezabel, his wife, said to him. Thou art of great authority indeed, and governest well the kingdom of Israel. Arise, and eat bread, and be of good cheer; I will give thee the vineyard of Naboth, the Jezrahelite.

    8So she wrote letters in Achab's name, and sealed them with his ring, and sent them to the ancients, and the chief men that were in his city, and that dwelt with Naboth.

    ☞R 8
    The ring makes murder administrative; authority becomes a stamp.

    9And this was the tenor of the letters: Proclaim a fast, and make Naboth sit among the chief of the people;

    ☞R 9
    A fast is proclaimed to sanctify a theft; piety as staging.

    10And suborn two men, sons of Belial, against him, and let them bear false witness; that he hath blasphemed God and the king: and then carry him out, and stone him, and so let him die.

    ☞R 10
    Two witnesses purchased; God and king fused into one charge to kill the inconvenient.

    11And the men of his city, the ancients and nobles, that dwelt with him in the city, did as Jezabel had commanded them, and as it was written in the letters which she had sent to them;

    12They proclaimed a fast, and made Naboth sit among the chief of the people.

    13And bringing two men, sons of the devil, they made them sit against him: and they, like men of the devil, bore witness against him before the people: saying: Naboth hath blasphemed God and the king. Wherefore they brought him forth without the city, and stoned him to death.

    ☞R 13
    Outside the city: expulsion precedes execution; the community performs its own lie.

    14And they sent to Jezabel, saying: Naboth is stoned, and is dead.

    15And it came to pass, when Jezabel heard that Naboth was stoned, and dead, that she said to Achab: Arise, and take possession of the vineyard of Naboth, the Jezrahelite, who would not agree with thee, and give it thee for money: for Naboth is not alive, but dead.

    16And when Achab heard this, to wit, that Naboth was dead, he arose, and went down into the vineyard of Naboth, the Jezrahelite, to take possession of it.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 5:38-42

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 5:38-42 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    38You have heard that it hath been said: An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth.

    ☞G 38
    Measured retaliation: justice as arithmetic.

    39But I say to you not to resist evil: but if one strike thee on thy right cheek, turn to him also the other: Not to resist evil, etc... What is here commanded, is a Christian patience under injuries and affronts, and to be willing even to suffer still more, rather than to indulge the desire of revenge: but what is further added does not strictly oblige according to the letter, for neither did Christ nor St. Paul turn the other cheek. St. John 18., and Acts 23.

    ☞G 39
    The command targets the reflex to answer injury with symmetry; it breaks the mirror.

    40And if a man will contend with thee in judgment, and take away thy coat, let go thy cloak also unto him.

    ☞G 40
    Litigation becomes a place to lose on purpose; property is treated as detachable.

    41And whosoever will force thee one mile, go with him other two.

    ☞G 41
    Compulsion is met with surplus; coercion is answered by voluntary excess.

    42Give to him that asketh of thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not away.

    ☞G 42
    Open hand as policy: refusal is the default being revoked.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s justice is not the management of equivalent harms but the renunciation of vengeance and the refusal to commodify what is given as inheritance. When authority and community sanctify theft through procedure, the disciple’s counter-law is voluntary loss rather than retaliatory gain.

    In Lectionem

    • R 1,6,7The narrative turns on a collision of jurisdictions: palace proximity versus ancestral tenure. Ahab’s offer is framed as reasonable exchange, but it presumes the crown can redefine what Israel treats as allotted and bounded; Jezebel supplies the missing premise—kingship as sheer capacity to get what it wants.
    • R 11,14,15,16The city’s elders function as the real engine: they translate a private desire into public verdict, then report upward like clerks. The sequence—verdict, notification, possession—shows how violence hides behind procedure; the vineyard is acquired not by conquest but by paperwork and communal compliance.

    In Evangelium

    • G 39,40,41,42Jesus does not refine talion; he displaces it with a pattern of non-competitive action that refuses to let the offender set the terms of the self. The examples move from insult to court to forced labor to begging, mapping escalating claims on body, goods, time, and resources; the disciple answers each by acting from a different economy than equivalence.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ahab wants a vineyard the way a child wants a toy: not for need, for nearness. “Nigh, and adjoining” is the whole argument. Naboth’s refusal isn’t stubbornness; it’s libido for the past, the inheritance that makes his life feel continuous. Ahab collapses into bed like a man discovering he can’t buy what would soothe him.

    Jezebel reads his sulk as a crisis of masculinity. She doesn’t just get him the land; she restores the fantasy that power can erase refusal. The fast is the masterstroke: a public performance of piety used as a murder weapon. The city’s elders comply because the lie is convenient; they prefer the clean ritual of stoning to the messy truth that a neighbor’s “no” is sacred.

    Then Jesus arrives sounding like the antidote, but he also sounds like the nightmare. Don’t resist evil; yield more than is demanded; give to the asker. In a world of Ahabs and Jezebels, that counsel can be read as training the Naboths of history to cooperate with their own dispossession. The other cheek becomes a second signature on the forged letter.

    But there’s a darker defiance inside it: refusal to let the oppressor choose the script. The extra cloak, the second mile, the open hand aren’t capitulation; they’re an exposure, a way of making greed visible by feeding it until it chokes. Ahab goes down to take possession; the teaching dares you to stand there, empty-handed, and make his taking look like what it is.

    Semina Verbi

    Naboth’s refusal reads like the Roman idea of res sacrae and ancestral land as a moral boundary the state should not price; Ahab’s sulk is the child-king fantasy that desire is entitlement, and Jezebel supplies the bureaucratic technology to convert appetite into “law.” The staged fast resembles Girard’s scapegoat mechanism: a community purifies itself by unanimous violence, laundering theft into righteousness. Speculation: it also echoes the Athenian fear of sycophants, where paid accusation becomes a civic instrument for private gain.

    The “other cheek” sits close to ahimsa in Jainism and the Buddhist training in non-reactivity, not as passivity but as refusal to let injury recruit you into the same circuitry. It also resembles Taoist wu-wei as social judo: by not meeting force with force, you deny the aggressor the drama that justifies escalation. Speculation: like Gandhi’s satyagraha, it treats suffering as a kind of public speech that exposes coercion’s dependence on your participation.
