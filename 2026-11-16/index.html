IIILectioRevelation 1:1-4; 2:1-5

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Second Letter of Revelation

Revelation 1:1-4; 2:1-5 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1:1The Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave unto him, to make known to his servants the things which must shortly come to pass: and signified, sending by his angel to his servant John,

☞R 1:1
Revelation arrives as chain-of-command: God → Jesus → angel → John → servants; distance is part of the message.

1:2Who hath given testimony to the word of God and the testimony of Jesus Christ, what things soever he hath seen.

1:3Blessed is he that readeth and heareth the words of this prophecy: and keepeth those things which are written in it. For the time is at hand.

☞R 1:3
Three verbs: read, hear, keep. The blessing is procedural, not sentimental; urgency supplies the pressure.

1:4John to the seven churches which are in Asia. Grace be unto you and peace, from him that is and that was and that is to come: and from the seven spirits which are before his throne:

☞R 1:4
Time is named as a Person: “is/was/to come.” Peace is sourced in ontology, not circumstance.

2:1Unto the angel of the church of Ephesus write: These things saith he who holdeth the seven stars in his right hand, who walketh in the midst of the seven golden candlesticks:

2:2I know thy works and thy labour and thy patience and how thou canst not bear them that are evil. And thou hast tried them who say they are apostles and are not: and hast found them liars:

2:3And thou hast patience and hast endured for my name and hast not fainted.

2:4But I have somewhat against thee, because thou hast left thy first charity.

☞R 2:4
The indictment is not heresy but amnesia: orthodoxy without “first charity” is a fall.

2:5Be mindful therefore from whence thou art fallen: and do penance and do the first works. Or else I come to thee and will move thy candlestick out of its place, except thou do penance.

☞R 2:5
Penance is framed as return to origin; the threat is ecclesial removal—lightstand displaced, not merely dimmed.

IVEvangeliumLuke 18:35-43

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Luke

Luke 18:35-43 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

35Now it came to pass, when he drew nigh to Jericho, that a certain blind man sat by the way side, begging.

☞G 35
Blindness is stationed at the roadside: marginal, dependent, public.

36And when he heard the multitude passing by, he asked what this meant.

37And they told him that Jesus of Nazareth was passing by.

38And he cried out, saying: Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me.

☞G 38
He names what he cannot see: “Son of David.” Insight precedes sight.

39And they that went before rebuked him, that he should hold his peace: but he cried out much more: Son of David, have mercy on me.

☞G 39
The crowd polices noise; mercy is treated as disorder. Persistence becomes his liturgy.

40And Jesus standing, commanded him to be brought unto him. And when he was come near, he asked him,

41Saying; What wilt thou that I do to thee? But he said: Lord, that I may see.

☞G 41
The request is bare and singular: not alms, not status—vision.

42And Jesus said to him: Receive thy sight: thy faith hath made thee whole.

☞G 42
Healing is adjudicated as faith’s effect; wholeness is credited before it is displayed.

43And immediately he saw and followed him, glorifying God. And all the people, when they saw it, gave praise to God.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisChrist judges churches by whether love remains first, and he restores persons by faith that clings to him despite social suppression. Where charity and faith are absent, the lampstand is removed; where they persist, sight and praise follow.

In Lectionem

  • R 2:1,2:2,2:3The Ephesian audit distinguishes endurance and discernment from the one thing that cannot be outsourced: love’s primacy. The letter assumes a church can win its purity tests and still be in collapse.
  • R 1:1,1:2John’s role is evidentiary—“testimony”—so the apocalypse is presented as courtroom material, not private rapture; it demands a verdict and a change of allegiance.

In Evangelium

  • G 36,37,40The narrative turns on hearing: the blind man interprets a passing crowd, and Jesus interrupts the procession by stopping. Salvation here is a break in traffic, not a private moment.
  • G 43The healed man’s first act is attachment—he follows—then doxology spreads contagiously to the crowd. The miracle functions as public reordering of attention.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Revelation opens like a sealed letter slid under the door at night: blessed is the one who reads because reading is already compliance. The voice that “walks in the midst” is not comforting; it is inspection. Ephesus is praised for suspicion, for its talent at detecting impostors, for its hard-won stamina. And then comes the wound: you have left your first charity. Not your doctrine, not your discipline—your desire. The threat is intimate and surgical: I will move your candlestick. Not punish you elsewhere, but unhouse your light, erase your place, make your vigilance meaningless.

The blind man in Luke is the inverse of Ephesus. He has no purity, no résumé of works, only need. The crowd polices silence the way Ephesus polices error; both are addicted to control. His faith is not correctness but insistence, a refusal to be managed. Jesus’ question—what do you want?—is the dangerous one, because it forces desire into speech. “That I may see” is not piety; it is appetite for reality, even if reality burns.

Put together, the texts say the same unsettling thing: you can be right and still be dead. You can guard the gates so well you lock out your own first love. Salvation here is not a reward for vigilance; it is the restoration of wanting. The candlestick stays where it is only if the heart returns. The blind man is healed because he will not stop asking, and Ephesus is threatened because it has stopped.

Semina Verbi

The seer’s warning to Ephesus—competence without first love—resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s critique of action severed from devotion, and Sufism’s distrust of piety without ishq, love that burns through self-regard. The threatened removal of the lampstand echoes the Daoist sense that when the Way is lost, institutions keep the shell and lose the light.

The blind beggar’s refusal to be silenced matches Zen’s insistence on great doubt and great determination, and the Psalms’ holy audacity in petition. Jesus’ question, “What do you want me to do for you?” parallels therapeutic traditions that force desire into speech: in psychoanalysis the cure begins when the subject risks naming what he wants. Speculation: the cry “have mercy” functions like the Jesus Prayer and like dhikr, a repetitive utterance that gathers a scattered self until perception changes.

“Your faith has made you whole” has kinship with yogic and Stoic accounts of attention: what you entrust yourself to reorganizes you. The crowd’s shift from rebuke to praise recalls Girard’s contagion of desire and emotion, but here redirected toward gratitude, as if sight spreads socially once one person dares to ask for it.
