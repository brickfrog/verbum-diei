IIILectioGenesis 2:7-9; 3:1-7

Lectio Prima

A readign from the Book of Genesis

Genesis 2:7-9; 3:1-7 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

2:7And the Lord God formed man of the slime of the earth: and breathed into his face the breath of life, and man became a living soul.

☞R 2:7
Earth + breath: the creature is animated dust, not self-originating.

2:8And the Lord God had planted a paradise of pleasure from the beginning: wherein he placed man whom he had formed.

2:9And the Lord God brought forth of the ground all manner of trees, fair to behold, and pleasant to eat of: the tree of life also in the midst of paradise: and the tree of knowledge of good and evil. The tree of life... So called because it had that quality, that by eating of the fruit of it, man would have been preserved in a constant state of health, vigour, and strength, and would not have died at all. The tree of knowledge... To which the deceitful serpent falsely attributed the power of imparting a superior kind of knowledge, beyond that which God was pleased to give.

☞R 2:9
Two trees in the center: immortality offered, discernment bounded.

3:1Now the serpent was more subtle than any of the beasts of the earth which the Lord God had made. And he said to the woman: Why hath God commanded you, that you should not eat of every tree of paradise?

☞R 3:1
Temptation begins as a question about God’s speech, not fruit.

3:2And the woman answered him, saying: Of the fruit of the trees that are in paradise we do eat:

3:3But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of paradise, God hath commanded us that we should not eat; and that we should not touch it, lest perhaps we die.

☞R 3:3
The command dilates: from “eat” to “touch.” Scruple breeds vulnerability.

3:4And the serpent said to the woman: No, you shall not die the death.

☞R 3:4
Flat contradiction: the lie is simple, not sophisticated.

3:5For God doth know that in what day soever you shall eat thereof, your eyes shall be opened: and you shall be as Gods, knowing good and evil.

☞R 3:5
“Be as Gods”: autonomy marketed as enlightenment.

3:6And the woman saw that the tree was good to eat, and fair to the eyes, and delightful to behold: and she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave to her husband, who did eat.

3:7And the eyes of them both were opened: and when they perceived themselves to be naked, they sewed together fig leaves, and made themselves aprons. And the eyes, etc... Not that they were blind before, (for the woman saw that the tree was fair to the eyes, ver. 6.) nor yet that their eyes were opened to any more perfect knowledge of good; but only to the unhappy experience of having lost the good of original grace and innocence, and incurred the dreadful evil of sin. From whence followed a shame of their being naked; which they minded not before; because being now stript of original grace, they quickly began to be subject to the shameful rebellions of the flesh.

☞R 3:7
Opening of eyes yields clothing, not glory; knowledge arrives as shame.

IVEvangeliumMatthew 4:1-11

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Matthew

Matthew 4:1-11 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1Then Jesus was led by the spirit into the desert, to be tempted by the devil.

☞G 1
The Spirit leads into combat; trial is not an accident.

2And when he had fasted forty days and forty nights, afterwards he was hungry.

3And the tempter coming said to him: If thou be the Son of God, command that these stones be made bread.

☞G 3
Stones-to-bread: sonship pressed into self-provision.

4Who answered and said: It is written, Not in bread alone doth man live, but in every word that proceedeth from the mouth of God.

5Then the devil took him up into the holy city, and set him upon the pinnacle of the temple,

6And said to him: If thou be the Son of God, cast thyself down, for it is written: That he hath given his angels charge over thee, and in their hands shall they bear thee up, lest perhaps thou dash thy foot against a stone.

☞G 6
Scripture weaponized: piety becomes a pretext for presumption.

7Jesus said to him: It is written again: Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God.

8Again the devil took him up into a very high mountain, and shewed him all the kingdoms of the world, and the glory of them, Shewed him, etc... That is, pointed out to him where each kingdom lay; and set forth in words what was most glorious and admirable in each of them. Or also set before his eyes, as it were in a large map, a lively representation of all those kingdoms.

9And said to him: All these will I give thee, if falling down thou wilt adore me.

☞G 9
Kingdoms offered without cross: worship redirected, rule detached from obedience.

10Then Jesus saith to him: Begone, Satan: for it is written: The Lord thy God shalt thou adore, and him only shalt thou serve.

☞G 10
A single imperative breaks the bargain: God alone is non-negotiable.

11Then the devil left him; and behold angels came and ministered to him.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisSin is the attempt to seize godlike autonomy by distrusting God’s word; Christ reverses it by subordinating need, safety, and power to worshipful obedience.

In Lectionem

  • R 2:8,3:2,3:6The scene is ordered gift (placement, permission) followed by disordered acquisition (taking, giving). Authority is mediated through command; the rupture is not appetite but jurisdiction—who defines good and evil, and by what right.
  • R 2:7,3:7Humanity’s dignity is received (breath) and lost experientially (nakedness). The narrative treats sin less as rule-breaking than as a catastrophic change of condition: from unselfconscious communion to self-surveillance.

In Evangelium

  • G 2,4,7,11The temptations escalate from necessity to spectacle to dominion; each is answered by appeal to a higher order than circumstance. The victory is not miraculous display but interpretive fidelity—Scripture read as obedience rather than leverage—ending with a restoration of proper service (ministering angels) without self-assertion.
  • G 5,8Movement to city and mountain frames a counterfeit liturgy: sacred height and worldly height used to induce false worship. The geography dramatizes that temptation seeks platforms—religious and political—where compromise can be called strategy.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Eden is not a nursery; it is a terrarium. The breath in the face is animation, not intimacy, and the “paradise of pleasure” reads like a controlled experiment: abundance ringed around two obsessive centers, one promising endless maintenance, one forbidden precisely because it would make desire self-aware. The serpent doesn’t introduce rebellion so much as language. The first question is therapy in a hiss: what, exactly, is your prohibition? Eve’s “lest perhaps we die” gives away the real condition—uncertainty already lives in them. The fruit is “delightful to behold” because it offers a new kind of pleasure: the pleasure of seeing oneself seeing. Their eyes open and immediately they manufacture clothing; knowledge arrives as panic, as the sudden discovery that a body can be looked at and therefore judged. Shame is the first technology.

In the desert the drama flips. Jesus is not the obedient child refusing candy; he is the human who refuses to be managed by appetite, spectacle, and achievement. The temptations are not sins so much as identities on offer: provider, miracle, emperor. Each would dissolve hunger for a moment and replace it with a role everyone can recognize. He answers with quotations, which is a colder move than it sounds: he stays inside language without letting language become a deal. Where Adam and Eve reach for experience to end ambiguity, Jesus tolerates the ache. The devil offers bread, angels, kingdoms—ways to stop feeling contingent. Jesus chooses contingency itself, the dread of being hungry and unproven, and in that refusal desire is purified into something sharper than pleasure: a will that cannot be bought.

Semina Verbi

Speculation: the garden scene reads like a study in mimetic desire; the serpent performs what Girard later names, not inventing hunger but redirecting it into rivalry with the divine image. That turn echoes the Buddhist account of taṇhā: craving dressed as awakening, where “eyes opened” is not illumination but the birth of self-conscious comparison and the anxiety of exposure. The fig leaf is proto-persona, close to Jung’s shadow moment: the instant the psyche splits into what is shown and what is managed.

Speculation: the desert trial resembles the yogic and Stoic discipline of refusing false goods. Stones-to-bread is the Epicurean temptation to collapse the whole self into appetite; the leap is the urge to coerce reality into spectacle, a Taoist warning against forcing the Way; the kingdoms are the classic kṣatriya seduction of power Krishna relativizes in the Gita. The through-line is interior freedom: desire is not killed but re-ordered, so the self stops bargaining with emptiness for control.
