    IIILectioSirach 27:30—28:7

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book ok Sirach

    Sirach 27:30—28:7 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    27:30A mischievous counsel shall be rolled back upon the author, and he shall not know from whence it cometh to him.

    ☞R 27:30
    Counsel returns like a wheel; the blow is anonymous until it lands.

    27:31Mockery and reproach are of the proud, and vengeance as a lion shall lie in wait for him.

    27:32They shall perish in a snare that are delighted with the fall of the just: and sorrow shall consume them before they die.

    27:33Anger and fury are both of them abominable, and the sinful man shall be subject to them.

    28:1He that seeketh to revenge himself, shall find vengeance from the Lord, and he will surely keep his sins in remembrance.

    ☞R 28:1
    Private revenge recruits a public prosecutor: the Lord as archivist of sins.

    28:2Forgive thy neighbour if he hath hurt thee: and then shall thy sins be forgiven to thee when thou prayest.

    ☞R 28:2
    Forgiveness is transactional, but the currency is prayer.

    28:3Man to man reserveth anger, and doth he seek remedy of God?

    28:4He hath no mercy on a man like himself, and doth he entreat for his own sins?

    28:5He that is but flesh, nourisheth anger, and doth he ask forgiveness of God? who shall obtain pardon for his sins?

    28:6Remember thy last things, and let enmity cease:

    ☞R 28:6
    Eschatology as solvent: “last things” dissolve grudges.

    28:7For corruption and death hang over in his commandments.

    IVEvangeliumMatthew 18:21-35

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Matthew

    Matthew 18:21-35 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    21Then came Peter unto him and said: Lord, how often shall my brother offend against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?

    22Jesus saith to him: I say not to thee, till seven times; but till seventy times seven times.

    ☞G 22
    Arithmetic breaks: forgiveness exits the realm of counting.

    23Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened to a king, who would take an account of his servants.

    24And when he had begun to take the account, one as brought to him, that owed him ten thousand talents.

    ☞G 24
    Ten thousand talents: debt inflated to the edge of the absurd.

    25And as he had not wherewith to pay it, his lord commanded that he should be sold, and his wife and children, and all that he had, and payment to be made.

    26But that servant falling down, besought him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.

    27And the lord of that servant being moved with pity, let him go and forgave him the debt.

    28But when that servant was gone out, he found one of his fellow- servants that owed him an hundred pence: and laying hold of him, he throttled him, saying: Pay what thou owest.

    ☞G 28
    Released hands become hands that throttle; mercy unlearned instantly.

    29And his fellow-servant falling down, besought him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.

    30And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he paid the debt.

    31Now his fellow servants seeing what was done, were very much grieved, and they came, and told their lord all that was done.

    32Then his lord called him: and said to him: Thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all the debt, because thou besoughtest me:

    33Shouldst not thou then have had compassion also on thy fellow servant, even as I had compassion on thee?

    34And his lord being angry, delivered him to the torturers until he paid all the debt.

    ☞G 34
    Torturers, not accountants: the debt becomes pain.

    35So also shall my heavenly Father do to you, if you forgive not every one his brother from your hearts.

    ☞G 35
    The condition is interior: “from your hearts,” not from your ledger.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s mercy, once received, binds the recipient to extend mercy without measure. Refusal to forgive converts forgiven debt into judgment and torment.

    In Lectionem

    • R 27:31,27:32The text frames vengeance as a predatory ecology: pride breeds mockery, mockery invites the lion, delight in another’s fall becomes a snare. Retribution is not merely chosen; it is inhabited.
    • R 28:3,28:4,28:5A forensic interrogation: the one who withholds mercy attempts to litigate with God. The argument is anthropological—“flesh” cannot sustain wrath and still plausibly petition for pardon.
    • R 28:7Commandments are set under a shadow: mortality presses obedience. The point is not sentiment but urgency—death makes enmity irrational.

    In Evangelium

    • G 23,25,27The parable stages sovereignty: the king can liquidate a household, then cancels instead. Mercy appears as a royal prerogative, not a negotiated settlement.
    • G 26,29,32,33The servant repeats the same plea he used, exposing the moral test: he knows the language of mercy but refuses its logic. The king’s rebuke is analogical—received compassion establishes an obligation to mirror it.
    • G 31The community functions as witness and conduit; hidden cruelty is made reportable. Judgment enters through testimony, not omniscient narration.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    These readings don’t praise forgiveness as virtue; they stage it as a technology of control. Sirach watches anger like a contagion that makes you predictable, a trap you step into with your eyes open. Vengeance is not heroic heat but an animal crouched in you, waiting to spring, and the punishment is intimate: you are consumed before you die.

    Matthew sharpens the dread. The king’s mercy isn’t tenderness; it’s an accounting move that creates a man who now belongs to the ledger. The forgiven servant panics at that new identity. If his own debt can vanish by fiat, then reality is arbitrary, and he needs someone beneath him to feel the world hard again. So he throttles the smaller debtor, trying to make justice physical, trying to prove there are still rules.

    The horror is that forgiveness is reversible. The king’s pity is not a clean absolution but a conditional suspension, and the condition is imitation. You are spared only if you become the same instrument of sparing. Refuse, and the system reasserts itself with “torturers,” as if the deepest punishment is being forced back into a moral economy you briefly thought you’d escaped.

    “From your hearts” lands like a threat: not merely behave, but desire correctly. The text knows we don’t want to forgive; we want to be forgiven while keeping the right to choke someone else. It calls that desire what it is, and it terrifies it into submission.

    Semina Verbi

    This is the psychology of resentment as self-harm: anger pretends to be strength, but it is dependence on the offender, a leash you keep in your own hand. The “debt” is an inner ledger that turns the mind into a petty court, endlessly reopening cases so it can feel righteous. The shock is scale: the forgiven servant cannot metabolize mercy, so he converts it into entitlement; he leaves the throne room lighter, then immediately needs someone else to be heavier. Unforgiveness is not justice but a refusal to live in the same universe where you were spared.

    The torturers are not merely external punishment; they are what the hard heart summons—rumination, vigilance, the compulsive replay that keeps the nervous system braced. The demand for payment becomes a way to avoid mourning: if the other “pays,” you never have to admit you were wounded and finite. “From your hearts” names the only real release: not a transaction, but a reorientation where you stop using injury as identity.

    Semina Verbi: Buddhism’s metta and the release of ill-will parallels the claim that clinging to anger burns the one who holds it; speculation: the “torturers” resemble the mind’s kleshas when fed. Stoicism’s refusal to be mastered by another’s act echoes the call to drop the inner tribunal. Hindu karma yoga’s insistence on acting without grasping at outcomes parallels forgiving without waiting for repayment. In Confucian ren, the shame of failing reciprocity after receiving benevolence mirrors the servant’s moral collapse. In Islam, rahma received obliges rahma given; speculation: the parable’s moral economy rhymes with that symmetry.
