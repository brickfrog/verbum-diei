    IIILectioActs 18:9-18

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 18:9-18 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    9And the Lord said to Paul in the night, by a vision: Do not fear, but speak. And hold not thy peace,

    ☞R 9
    Night-vision: command replaces courage; speech is obedience, not temperament.

    10Because I am with thee and no man shall set upon thee, to hurt thee. For I have much people in this city.

    ☞R 10
    “I am with thee” is the premise; immunity is derivative, not absolute.

    11And he stayed there a year and six months, teaching among them the word of God.

    12But when Gallio was proconsul of Achaia, the Jews with one accord rose up against Paul and brought him to the judgment seat,

    ☞R 12
    “With one accord”: liturgical unanimity repurposed as prosecution.

    13Saying: This man persuadeth men to worship God contrary to the law.

    14And when Paul was beginning to open his mouth, Gallio said to the Jews: If it were some matter of injustice or an heinous deed, O Jews, I should with reason bear with you.

    15But if they be questions of word and names and of your law, look you to it. I will not be judge of such things.

    ☞R 15
    Empire refuses to arbitrate theology; jurisdictional shrug becomes providence.

    16And he drove them from the judgment seat.

    17And all laying hold on Sosthenes, the ruler of the synagogue, beat him before the judgment seat. And Gallio cared for none of those things.

    ☞R 17
    Violence spills onto the wrong body; the judge’s indifference is its own verdict.

    18But Paul, when he had stayed yet many days, taking his leave of the brethren, sailed thence into Syria (and with him Priscilla and Aquila), having shorn his head in Cenchrae. For he had a vow.

    ☞R 18
    Shorn head: mission advances under vow—freedom expressed as constraint.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 16:20-23

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 16:20-23 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    20Amen, amen, I say to you, that you shall lament and weep, but the world shall rejoice: and you shall be made sorrowful, but your sorrow shall be turned into joy.

    ☞G 20
    Two audiences, opposite affect: disciples weep; “world” celebrates—moral inversion.

    21A woman, when she is in labour, hath sorrow, because her hour is come; but when she hath brought forth the child, she remembereth no more the anguish, for joy that a man is born into the world.

    ☞G 21
    Labour: pain is not denied; it is reclassified as passage, not endpoint.

    22So also you now indeed have sorrow: but I will see you again and your heart shall rejoice. And your joy no man shall take from you.

    ☞G 22
    Joy secured by encounter (“I will see you”), not by circumstance; theft-proof.

    23And in that day you shall not ask me any thing. Amen, amen, I say to you: if you ask the Father any thing in my name, he will give it you.

    ☞G 23
    Prayer shifts from interrogation to petition; access is mediated by the Name.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod secures the church’s speech by his presence and by ordering even secular indifference to serve the gospel. Christ converts grief into irreversible joy and establishes prayerful access to the Father through his name.

    In Lectionem

    • R 11The vision’s promise cashes out as duration: a long, ordinary tenure of teaching. Protection is shown not by spectacle but by time bought for instruction.
    • R 13,14,16The accusation tries to force the gospel into the category of civil illegality; the hearing collapses because the state recognizes no actionable harm. The church’s public space is opened by a pagan’s procedural minimalism.

    In Evangelium

    • G 20,22,23The discourse moves from prediction to permanence: sorrow is temporary, joy is durable, and the durable joy grounds a new mode of relating to God. The “day” marks a post-seeing economy where communion is maintained through asking the Father in the Son’s name.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    The vision is not comfort; it is recruitment. “Do not fear” isn’t tenderness but command, the way power speaks to a body it intends to spend. God promises Paul safety, yet the scene immediately proves the promise’s real meaning: someone will be hurt, just not Paul. The guarantee is selective, transactional. “I have much people in this city” sounds like affection, but it lands like inventory. Corinth is claimed in advance; Paul is the instrument that makes the claim visible.

    Gallio’s indifference is the hidden engine of the mission. Empire doesn’t need to believe; it only needs to yawn. Paul survives because the state refuses to care, and that refusal creates a vacuum where a new identity can spread. But the vacuum is filled with violence anyway: Sosthenes is beaten in public while the magistrate looks away. The text lets that ugliness stand. The gospel’s “joy” is purchased by a world that keeps rejoicing at somebody else’s pain.

    Then the vow: Paul shears his head like a man trying to put a boundary around his own complicity. A private wound to answer a public one. He cannot stop the machine, so he marks himself, as if sacrifice could be controlled by ritual. Desire for a clean conscience becomes another kind of bondage.

    John’s promise that sorrow will turn to joy reads less like consolation than like anesthesia. Labour pain is not redeemed; it is forgotten. The world rejoices now, the disciples weep now, and the proposed victory is a memory rewrite: anguish swallowed by a new attachment. “No one will take your joy” because it will be locked inside you, untouchable, solitary, and therefore politically useless. Ask in my name, and you will receive—prayer as sanctioned desire, desire as obedience. The dread remains; it’s merely given a holy vocabulary.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s night-vision reads like the psyche’s permission slip: the superego’s terror loosens, speech returns, and the self can inhabit a hostile city without collapsing into silence. That “I have much people in this city” is the intuition that belonging precedes proof; you speak first, and only later discover the listeners who were already there. Gallio’s refusal is the cold neutrality of power: it won’t arbitrate meaning, only violence. The beating in the courthouse is the scapegoat mechanism spilling out when institutions decline to name truth; order is preserved by letting someone else pay.

    The vow and the shorn head are embodiment against abstraction: after rhetoric and trial, the body keeps a private ledger. It is a way of binding anxiety to a concrete act so the mind can keep moving.

    John’s image of labor is not consolation but a theory of transformation: pain becomes unrecognizable once it has been metabolized into new life. The world’s rejoicing is the crowd’s appetite for spectacle; the disciple’s joy is interior, not because it is fragile, but because it is no longer negotiable. “In that day you shall not ask” suggests the end of compulsive questioning: when presence returns, interrogation falls away.

    Semina Verbi: the night-voice that says “do not fear, speak” resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s exhortation to act without paralysis, and the Stoic daemon of vocation; speculation: the “many people in this city” echoes Mahayana bodhisattva intuition that the field is already seeded. Gallio’s stance parallels Roman legal minimalism and, more charitably, Daoist non-interference, though here it curdles into moral vacancy. The labor-to-joy turn touches Buddhist dukkha transmuted into insight and Sufi fana giving way to baqa—loss that becomes a different kind of life.
