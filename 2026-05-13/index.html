    IIILectioActs 17:15,22—18:1

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of the Acts of the Apostles

    Acts 17:15,22—18:1 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    17:15And they that conducted Paul brought him as far as Athens: and receiving a commandment from him to Silas and Timothy, that they should come to him with all speed, they departed.

    17:22But Paul, standing in the midst of the Areopagus, said: Ye men of Athens, I perceive that in all things you are too superstitious.

    ☞R 17:22
    Opens with a diagnosis: piety as excess, not absence.

    17:23For passing by and seeing your idols, I found an altar also, on which was written: To the Unknown God. What therefore you worship without knowing it, that I preach to you:

    ☞R 17:23
    The “Unknown” becomes a rhetorical hostage: their inscription turned into his premise.

    17:24God, who made the world and all things therein, he being Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands. Dwelleth not in temples... God is not contained in temples; so as to need them for his dwelling, or any other uses, as the heathens imagined. Yet by his omnipresence, he is both there and everywhere.

    ☞R 17:24
    Creator-talk detonates local cult: heaven/earth Lordship makes shrines redundant.

    17:25Neither is he served with men's hands, as though he needed any thing: seeing it is he who giveth to all life and breath and all things:

    17:26And hath made of one, all mankind, to dwell upon the whole face of the earth, determining appointed times and the limits of their habitation.

    ☞R 17:26
    “Of one” collapses ethnic mythologies; providence draws borders and calendars.

    17:27That they should seek God, if haply they may feel after him or find him, although he be not far from every one of us.

    ☞R 17:27
    Seeking described as groping—religion as tactile ignorance under near-presence.

    17:28For in him we live and move and are: as some also of your own poets said: For we are also his offspring.

    17:29Being therefore the offspring of God, we must not suppose the divinity to be like unto gold or silver or stone, the graving of art and device of man.

    ☞R 17:29
    If offspring, then no metal parent: image-making is a category error.

    17:30And God indeed having winked at the times of this ignorance, now declareth unto men that all should every where do penance.

    ☞R 17:30
    “Winked” is not approval but suspended sentence; now the summons is universal.

    17:31Because he hath appointed a day wherein he will judge the world in equity, by the man whom he hath appointed: giving faith to all, by raising him up from the dead.

    17:32And when they had heard of the resurrection of the dead, some indeed mocked. But others said: We will hear thee again concerning this matter.

    ☞R 17:32
    Resurrection is the tripwire: philosophy tolerates theism, not bodies returned.

    17:33So Paul went out from among them.

    17:34But certain men, adhering to him, did believe: among whom was also Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris and others with them.

    18:1After these things, departing from Athens, he came to Corinth.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 16:12-15

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 16:12-15 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    12I have yet many things to say to you: but you cannot bear them now.

    ☞G 12
    Revelation is paced by capacity; truth can crush as well as save.

    13But when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will teach you all truth. For he shall not speak of himself: but what things soever he shall hear, he shall speak. And the things that are to come, he shall shew you. Will teach you all truth... See the annotation on chap. 14. ver. 26.

    ☞G 13
    The Spirit is a courier, not an originator: speech as obedient transmission.

    14He shall glorify me: because he shall receive of mine and shall shew it to you.

    15All things whatsoever the Father hath are mine. Therefore I said that he shall receive of me and shew it to you.

    ☞G 15
    Total shared possession: Father’s “all” becomes the Son’s “mine,” without division.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod is not an object within the world’s religious economy but its Creator and Judge, therefore all worship must convert from images to repentance. The Spirit’s teaching is the authorized continuation of Christ’s revelation, binding the Church to the Son’s truth and glory.

    In Lectionem

    • R 17:15,17:31,17:33,17:34,18:1The scene is a controlled collision between public reason and apostolic proclamation: Paul is escorted into the city’s intellectual court (1), then anchors his address in creation and providence to establish jurisdiction over all peoples (11). The speech does not aim at cultural synthesis but at verdict—judgment is fixed and personal (“by the man”), and the resurrection functions as the public credential. The outcome is a threefold sorting (13–14): dismissal, delay, adhesion; the mission advances regardless (15).

    In Evangelium

    • G 13,14The discourse maps Trinitarian pedagogy: the Spirit’s mission is epistemic and doxological—he mediates what is heard and received, and his teaching terminates in the Son’s glorification. “Truth” is not mere information but ordered disclosure within divine relations, so the Church’s later understanding is not innovation but authorized reception.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s speech in Athens is not a triumph of reason over idolaters; it is a seduction that begins by flattering their anxiety. “Too superstitious” is a diagnosis of hunger. He spots the altar to the Unknown God and treats their most honest confession—there is a blank at the center—as an opening to colonize the blank. He offers a God too large for temples, which sounds like liberation, but it also strips them of every handle. A deity you cannot house is a deity you cannot bargain with. The gift is omnipresence; the cost is helplessness.

    He promises proximity—“not far from every one of us”—yet describes a world where humans grope in the dark, “feel after him,” like bodies searching for a lover who refuses to be seen. Then comes the turn: ignorance was “winked at,” but now the wink hardens into a stare. Penance is demanded, judgment scheduled, the resurrection set down like a verdict. The Athenians’ laughter is not shallow skepticism; it is panic at the thought that the Unknown might suddenly have a name and a court date.

    John sharpens the same pressure. Jesus withholds not because the disciples are unworthy, but because truth is weight, and the psyche has limits. The Spirit does not invent; it repeats, relays, transmits—truth as an internal voice that is never quite yours. Desire for “all truth” is answered by a system of possession: the Father’s things are the Son’s, the Spirit takes and shows. The consolation is guidance; the dread is that revelation arrives as something you cannot bear, delivered on a timetable not your own, until your inner life becomes a room where another speaks through you.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s “Unknown God” is the religious mind admitting its own blind spot, a disciplined humility that refuses to confuse the felt presence with the images that soothe it. That move resembles apophatic strands in Neoplatonism and later in Sufism, where the truest speech about the Real begins by denying that any crafted form can contain it. His claim that the divine “needs nothing” echoes the Bhagavad Gita’s insistence that the Absolute is not fed by offerings, and the Stoic sense of a self-sufficient logos that underwrites life without being improved by it.

    “In him we live and move and are” sits close to Vedanta’s nondual dependence and to Daoism’s sense that all motion rides an ungraspable source; speculation: it also brushes Zen’s refusal to locate awakening in a particular shrine or object. The turn from idol to inward seeking parallels the Upanishadic pivot from ritual to insight, and the Jewish prophetic critique of empty cult.

    “You cannot bear it now” names a psychology of staged disclosure: truth as dosage, not data. That resembles the Buddhist idea of skillful means and the Platonic suspicion that certain realities require moral and emotional preparation before intellectual access. The “Spirit of truth” who speaks what he hears suggests a model of inspiration as reception rather than self-assertion, akin to the Qur’anic image of revelation as transmitted speech and, more distantly, the Vedic seer as one who ‘hears’ rather than invents.

    The mockery at resurrection reads like the perennial clash between a culture trained to accept only what fits its metaphysics and a message that breaks the frame; that fracture is familiar in every tradition when experience refuses the reigning categories and demands conversion of imagination.
