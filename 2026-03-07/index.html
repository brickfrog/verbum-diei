IIILectioMicah 7:14-15,18-20

Lectio Prima

A reading from the Book of Micah

Micah 7:14-15,18-20 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

14Feed thy people with thy rod, the flock of thy inheritance, them that dwell alone in the forest, in the midst of Carmel: they shall feed in Basan and Galaad, according to the days of old.

☞R 14
Shepherding by a rod: care that also strikes. Inheritance pictured as feral solitude.

15According to the days of thy coming out of the land of Egypt, I will shew him wonders.

☞R 15
Exodus recalled as a template: salvation is precedent, not novelty.

18Who is a God like to thee, who takest away iniquity, and passest by the sin of the remnant of thy inheritance? he will send his fury in no more, because he delighteth in mercy.

☞R 18
Rhetorical incomparability of God grounded in a scandal: He “passes by” sin.

19He will turn again, and have mercy on us: he will put away our iniquities: and he will cast all our sins into the bottom of the sea.

☞R 19
Violence redirected: sins, not sinners, are drowned—obliteration by disposal.

20Thou wilt perform the truth to Jacob, the mercy to Abraham: which thou hast sworn to our fathers from the days of old. THE PROPHECY OF NAHUM NAHUM, whose name signifies A COMFORTER, was a native of Elcese, or Elcesai, supposed to be a little town in Galilee. He prophesied, after the ten tribes were carried into captivity, and foretold the utter destruction of Ninive, by the Babylonians and Medes: which happened in the reign of JOSIAS.

☞R 20
Mercy and truth are not moods but oaths; the past binds the present.

IVEvangeliumLuke 15:1-3,11-32

Evangelium

From the Gospel according to Luke

Luke 15:1-3,11-32 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

1Now the publicans and sinners drew near unto him to hear him.

☞G 1
The draw is auditory: they come to hear before they are cleaned.

2And the Pharisees and the scribes murmured, saying: This man receiveth sinners and eateth with them.

☞G 2
Table fellowship becomes the indictment; proximity is treated as contamination.

3And he spoke to them this parable, saying:

11And he said: A certain man had two sons.

12And the younger of them said to his father: Father, give me the portion of substance that falleth to me. And he divided unto them his substance.

☞G 12
Inheritance demanded as if the father were already dead: premature possession.

13And not many days after, the younger son, gathering all together, went abroad into a far country: and there wasted his substance, living riotously.

☞G 13
“Far country” is theology: distance monetized, then liquefied.

14And after he had spent all, there came a mighty famine in that country: and he began to be in want.

15And he went and cleaved to one of the citizens of that country. And he sent him into his farm to feed swine.

☞G 15
He “cleaves” to a stranger: counterfeit belonging purchased by servitude.

16And he would fain have filled his belly with the husks the swine did eat: and no man gave unto him.

17And returning to himself, he said: How many hired servants in my father's house abound with bread, and I here perish with hunger!

☞G 17
Conversion described as self-return, not self-invention.

18I will arise and will go to my father and say to him: Father, I have sinned against heaven and before thee.

19I am not worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants.

☞G 19
He negotiates downward: from sonship to wage; repentance as contract offer.

20And rising up, he came to his father. And when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him and was moved with compassion and running to him fell upon his neck and kissed him.

☞G 20
The father runs—undignified speed—while the son is still “a great way off.”

21And the son said to him: Father: I have sinned against heaven and before thee I am not now worthy to be called thy son.

22And the father said to his servants: Bring forth quickly the first robe and put it on him: and put a ring on his hand and shoes on his feet.

☞G 22
Robe, ring, shoes: restoration is public, total, and irreversible.

23And bring hither the fatted calf, and kill it: and let us eat and make merry:

24Because this my son was dead and is come to life again, was lost and is found. And they began to be merry.

25Now his elder son was in the field and when he came and drew nigh to the house, he heard music and dancing.

26And he called one of the servants, and asked what these things meant.

27And he said to him: Thy brother is come and thy father hath killed the fatted calf, because he hath received him safe.

28And he was angry and would not go in. His father therefore coming out began to entreat him.

☞G 28
The father goes out twice: mercy interrupts both vice and virtue.

29And he answering, said to his father: Behold, for so many years do I serve thee and I have never transgressed thy commandment: and yet thou hast never given me a kid to make merry with my friends.

☞G 29
Obedience weaponized into a wage-claim; “serve” replaces “son.”

30But as soon as this thy son is come, who hath devoured his substance with harlots, thou hast killed for him the fatted calf.

31But he said to him: Son, thou art always with me; and all I have is thine.

☞G 31
“All I have is thine”: resentment reveals ignorance of abundance.

32But it was fit that we should make merry and be glad: for this thy brother was dead and is come to life again; he was lost, and is found.

VGlossaCommentarium

Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s mercy is covenantal restoration: he removes sin to recover sons, not to ratify their bargains. Refusal of the feast is refusal of the Father’s definition of life.

In Lectionem

  • R 14,18,20Micah frames restoration as covenant administration: a flock, a remnant, and a sworn history. The logic is juridical—God’s uniqueness is displayed not in raw power but in the way judgment is restrained to preserve an inheritance.
  • R 15,19The Exodus allusion sets the horizon: deliverance is an act that creates a people and then continually re-creates them. Forgiveness is depicted as an objective removal that makes future communion possible rather than mere emotional leniency.

In Evangelium

  • G 3,11,24Luke positions the parable as an answer to a dispute about boundaries. The story’s hinge is not the younger son’s collapse but the father’s declaration of death-to-life: he redefines reality, then demands the household accept that definition.
  • G 25,31,32The elder son functions as the mirror of the Pharisaic complaint: he refuses participation in the father’s economy of gift. The resolution is not an argument about fairness but a summons to share the father’s joy as the proper expression of sonship.

Lectio Heterodoxa

Micah’s “mercy” is not softness; it is disposal. God is pictured as a handler with a rod, pushing a skittish flock out of lonely forest into older pastures, then taking the community’s rot and throwing it where no one can retrieve it: the sea’s bottom. Forgiveness is an act of violence against memory, a drowning of the evidence so the story can keep going.

Luke’s father does the same thing, and it is terrifying. The younger son returns not from enlightenment but from hunger, with a rehearsed speech designed to secure a minimum wage and survive. The father interrupts the script with costume and meat, not to reward virtue but to rewrite reality in public before the household can fix blame. The kiss is a gag; the robe is a verdict.

The elder son is the only honest one. He names the erotic waste, the unfairness, the way “mercy” humiliates the dutiful by making their endurance invisible. The father’s line, “all I have is thine,” is not comfort but a trap: you already own the estate, so you cannot leave without becoming the kind of son who takes and runs. The party is coercion. Either enter and consent to the new narrative, or stand outside with your righteousness and starve on it.

Both texts confess the same desire: not justice, but erasure. The miracle is not that the lost are found; it’s that everyone is forced to live as if the loss never happened.

Semina Verbi

Micah’s mercy that buries guilt in the sea has an Indian cousin in the Ganges’ imagined power to carry stain away; both picture moral residue as something that can be submerged, not argued into submission. The father running to the ruined son echoes the bodhisattva ideal in Mahayana Buddhism, compassion moving first toward the broken before any proof of reform; speculative, but the emotional grammar matches. The elder brother’s cold bookkeeping resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s warning that clinging to merit corrodes devotion, and also the Stoic temptation to turn virtue into status. The “returning to himself” moment feels like Platonic anamnesis and the Sufi tawba: not self-invention but a wake-up into what one already knows, a homecoming of the mind. The feast that re-seats the son parallels Japan’s Naikan practice’s aim of restoring relational belonging through gratitude rather than legal balance; speculative, but it shares the insistence that reconciliation is enacted, not merely declared.
