    IIILectioEzekiel 47:1-2,8-9,12

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the Book of Ezekiel

    Ezekiel 47:1-2,8-9,12 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1And he brought me again to the gate of the house, and behold waters issued out from under the threshold of the house toward the east: for the forefront of the house looked toward the east: but the waters came down to the right side of the temple to the south part of the altar.

    ☞R 1
    Water from under the threshold: life leaks from holiness, not from human plumbing.

    2And he led me out by the way of the north gate, and he caused me to turn to the way without the outward gate to the way that looked toward the east: and behold there ran out waters on the right side.

    ☞R 2
    He is led out to see what cannot be seen from inside: revelation requires displacement.

    8And he said to me: These waters that issue forth toward the hillocks of sand to the east, and go down to the plains of the desert, shall go into the sea, and shall go out, and the waters shall be healed.

    ☞R 8
    Desert-to-sea itinerary: the stream seeks the dead place on purpose.

    9And every living creature that creepeth whithersoever the torrent shall come, shall live: and there shall be fishes in abundance after these waters shall come thither, and they shall be healed, and all things shall live to which the torrent shall come.

    ☞R 9
    Creeping things live: the lowest forms are not beneath the torrent’s reach.

    12And by the torrent on the banks thereof on both sides shall grow all trees that bear fruit: their leaf shall not fall off, and their fruit shall not fail: every month shall they bring forth firstfruits, because the waters thereof shall issue out of the sanctuary: and the fruits thereof shall be for food, and the leaves thereof for medicine.

    ☞R 12
    Trees on both banks: symmetry of gift; no privileged shoreline.

    IVEvangeliumJohn 2:13-22

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to John

    John 2:13-22 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    13And the pasch of the Jews was at hand: and Jesus went up to Jerusalem.

    14And he found in the temple them that sold oxen and sheep and doves, and the changers of money sitting.

    15And when he had made, as it were, a scourge of little cords, he drove them all out of the temple, the sheep also and the oxen: and the money of the changers he poured out, and the tables he overthrew.

    ☞G 15
    Scourge of cords: judgment is handmade, deliberate, not a tantrum.

    16And to them that sold doves he said: Take these things hence, and make not the house of my Father a house of traffic.

    ☞G 16
    House of Father vs house of traffic: filiation excludes monetization.

    17And his disciples remembered, that it was written: The zeal of thy house hath eaten me up.

    ☞G 17
    Zeal that eats: devotion as self-consuming fire, not polite preference.

    18The Jews, therefore, answered, and said to him: What sign dost thou shew unto us, seeing thou dost these things?

    19Jesus answered and said to them: Destroy this temple; and in three days I will raise it up.

    ☞G 19
    “Destroy this temple”: permission to break the sign once the reality arrives.

    20The Jews then said: Six and forty years was this temple in building; and wilt thou raise it up in three days?

    21But he spoke of the temple of his body.

    22When therefore he was risen again from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this: and they believed the scripture and the word that Jesus had said.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisGod’s dwelling is no longer secured by a managed sanctuary but is identified with the crucified-and-raised body of Christ, from whom healing life flows into what is dead. True worship therefore purges commerce and generates restoration rather than revenue.

    In Lectionem

    • R 2,8The vision is staged as a guided inspection: the prophet is walked around the structure so the source can be identified as the sanctuary itself, then the stream is traced outward until it reaches what Israel cannot fix by policy—desert and sea. The point is not irrigation but a new center of causality: holiness becomes the origin of ecological and social restoration.
    • R 9,12The effects are comprehensive: from “creeping” life to cultivated trees, the torrent reorganizes the whole food chain and calendar. The monthly yield implies a reordered time, where worship is no longer episodic tribute but continuous production of life that can be eaten and administered as remedy.

    In Evangelium

    • G 13,14,18Passover frames the action as a contest over liberation and its symbols. The question for a “sign” exposes the temple’s crisis: authority is demanded in the idiom of spectacle, while Jesus acts as if ownership is already settled.
    • G 20,21,22The narrative relocates the temple from architecture to a body, and then from body to interpretation after resurrection. The misunderstanding about construction time is not incidental; it marks the end of building as the mode of access to God, replaced by a raised body that becomes the definitive site of presence and the key that makes Scripture intelligible.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Ezekiel’s water is not comfort; it is a leak. The sanctuary can’t hold itself together, and what escapes becomes a torrent that colonizes everything it touches. The “healing” is invasive: the desert gets rewritten, the sea gets corrected, trees are conscripted into endless productivity, fruit on schedule, leaves that never fall. It’s a fantasy of life without winter, which is another way of saying life without permission to die. The holy place emits a substance that won’t let the world remain itself.

    John’s temple scene is the same pressure turned violent. Jesus doesn’t purify; he breaks the social skin that makes worship manageable. The whip is theater and threat, a public nervous system snapping. “My Father’s house” is not a shared civic space but a private body he claims, and the claim demands eviction. Then he shifts the building into himself: the real temple is a body that can be destroyed and rebuilt, which is to say a body that will not stay dead.

    Put them together and the dread sharpens. The sacred is not a refuge from appetite and exchange; it is appetite intensified, desire for a world remade at any cost. Water pours from the altar like blood from a wound, and the cure is indistinguishable from takeover. The sign is not serenity but a promise: everything you rely on can be overturned, and the thing that overturns it will call itself life.

    Semina Verbi

    Ezekiel’s river resembles the Hindu Ganga: not mere water but a current of sanctity that “heals” what it touches; speculation: both imagine holiness as transmissible, almost ecological, turning geography into physiology. The fruit trees with medicinal leaves echo the Buddhist image of dharma as medicine and the bodhisattva as physician; here, the cure is not advice but a living environment grown from the sacred source.

    John’s temple scene parallels the Stoic and Cynic suspicion of commerce as a corrosion of the inner citadel; the whip is a dramatized psychotherapy, an intervention against compulsive exchange that has colonized the psyche’s sanctuary. “Destroy this temple” aligns with Zen’s iconoclasm toward external forms; speculation: the shock is meant to break attachment to the building so the body becomes the only credible altar, a relocation of the sacred from institution to person.
