    IIILectio1 Corinthians 8:1-7,11-13

    Lectio Prima

    A reading from the First Letter of St. Paul to Corinthians

    1 Corinthians 8:1-7,11-13 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    1Now concerning those things that are sacrificed to idols: we know we all have knowledge. Knowledge puffeth up: but charity edifieth.

    ☞R 1
    Knowledge inflates; love builds. Two architectures.

    2And if any man think that he knoweth any thing, he hath not yet known as he ought to know.

    ☞R 2
    Epistemic humility as a moral requirement, not a mood.

    3But if any man love God, the same is known by him.

    ☞R 3
    The decisive verb is passive: to be known.

    4But as for the meats that are sacrificed to idols, we know that an idol is nothing in the world and that there is no God but one.

    ☞R 4
    Idol = null; the danger is not metaphysical but relational.

    5For although there be that are called gods, either in heaven or on earth (for there be gods many and lords many):

    6Yet to us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we unto him: and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him.

    7But there is not knowledge in every one. For some until this present, with conscience of the idol, eat as a thing sacrificed to an idol: and their conscience, being weak, is defiled.

    ☞R 7
    Conscience can be weak and still binding; defilement is internal.

    11And through thy knowledge shall the weak brother perish, for whom Christ hath died?

    ☞R 11
    Your “right” becomes another’s ruin; knowledge turns lethal.

    12Now when you sin thus against the brethren and wound their weak conscience, you sin against Christ.

    ☞R 12
    Wounding a conscience is christological violence.

    13Wherefore, if meat scandalize my brother, I will never eat flesh, lest I should scandalize my brother.

    ☞R 13
    Freedom voluntarily amputated to prevent scandal.

    IVEvangeliumLuke 6:27-38

    Evangelium

    From the Gospel according to Luke

    Luke 6:27-38 · Douay-Rheims 1899 American Edition

    27But I say to you that hear: Love your enemies. Do good to them that hate you.

    ☞G 27
    Enemy-love is commanded to “those that hear”: obedience filters the audience.

    28Bless them that curse you and pray for them that calumniate you.

    29And to him that striketh thee on the one cheek, offer also the other. And him that taketh away from thee thy cloak, forbid not to take thy coat also.

    ☞G 29
    Non-retaliation escalates into dispossession; the self is de-armed.

    30Give to every one that asketh thee: and of him that taketh away thy goods, ask them not again.

    ☞G 30
    Refusal to reclaim goods: renunciation of leverage.

    31And as you would that men should do to you, do you also to them in like manner.

    32And if you love them that love you, what thanks are to you? For sinners also love those that love them.

    ☞G 32
    Natural reciprocity is dismissed as morally trivial.

    33And if you do good to them who do good to you, what thanks are to you? For sinners also do this.

    34And if you lend to them of whom you hope to receive, what thanks are to you? For sinners also lend to sinners, for to receive as much.

    35But love ye your enemies: do good, and lend, hoping for nothing thereby: and your reward shall be great, and you shall be the sons of the Highest. For he is kind to the unthankful and to the evil.

    ☞G 35
    Hope is severed from return; sonship is patterned on divine kindness to the vile.

    36Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.

    37Judge not: and you shall not be judged. Condemn not: and you shall not be condemned. Forgive: and you shall be forgiven.

    38Give: and it shall be given to you: good measure and pressed down and shaken together and running over shall they give into your bosom. For with the same measure that you shall mete withal, it shall be measured to you again.

    ☞G 38
    Measure-for-measure: generosity installs the metric that will judge you.

    VGlossaCommentarium

    Sententia DoctrinalisChristian freedom is real but never sovereign: it is governed by love that refuses to harm the weak and by mercy that breaks the logic of retaliation and exchange.

    In Lectionem

    • R 5,6Paul concedes the crowded pantheon as a social fact, then re-centers the community’s ontology in a twofold confession: all things “from” the Father and “through” the Lord. The ethical problem of food is solved by locating the Church inside that origin-and-agency grammar; behavior must match the world it claims.
    • R 1,11,12The argument turns on representation: the “strong” act publicly, and their act becomes instruction. Because Christ’s death has already assigned infinite value to the weak, the strong cannot treat their own clarity as private property; their conduct is ecclesial speech.

    In Evangelium

    • G 28,31,36,37Luke’s sequence moves from interpersonal hostility to the interior tribunal: blessing and prayer, then the rule of symmetry, then mercy, then the suspension of judgment. The point is not niceness but the dismantling of the mechanisms by which the self secures superiority.
    • G 33,34,35,38Jesus attacks the economy of exchange—doing good for good, lending for return—and replaces it with an economy of resemblance: act without repayment so that your life mirrors the Father’s indiscriminate beneficence. The promised “reward” is not wages but filial likeness made visible.

    Lectio Heterodoxa

    Paul’s “knowledge” is not clarity; it is permission. The idol is “nothing,” so the enlightened can eat, stroll through the marketplace untouched, and call it freedom. But the real god in the room is the self that loves being right. Knowledge puffs because it turns other people into props: the weak brother becomes a lesson, a cautionary tale, a convenient casualty. Paul’s renunciation of meat is not piety; it is an admission that the community is held together by contagion, by suggestion, by the violence of example. One person’s liberty becomes another person’s relapse.

    Luke’s love of enemies is the same dread, sharpened. It is not kindness; it is disarmament. Turn the other cheek, give the cloak, lend without return: these are tactics for breaking the loop where injury demands payment and payment demands more injury. Mercy here is not soft; it is terrifyingly asymmetrical. You refuse the sweet relief of retaliation, the pleasure of being owed, the identity of victim. “Judge not” is not moral advice; it is a warning that condemnation is a narcotic and you will take the dose you prescribe.

    Put together, the texts strip the ego of its favorite luxuries: being correct, being wronged, being superior. They ask for a love that costs you your rights, your appetites, your stories. The reward is not comfort. It is the strange overflow that comes when you stop feeding the idol of the self.

    Semina Verbi

    Paul’s restraint for the sake of the “weak” has a close cousin in the Buddhist vow to avoid harm through “skillful means” (upaya): truth without timing becomes cruelty. It also resembles the Jain discipline of ahimsa, where even permissible acts are refused if they agitate another’s conscience; the point is not purity but non-injury.

    Luke’s enemy-love echoes the Stoic cosmopolis: the offender is still kin in the same rational city, so retaliation is a kind of self-mutilation. It also brushes the Daoist ideal of returning softness for hardness, letting aggression exhaust itself against nonresistance. Speculation: the “measure” image aligns with karmic mirroring in Hindu and Buddhist thought, not as cosmic bookkeeping but as the psyche’s habit of recreating the world it expects—mercy training perception until mercy is what one can receive.
